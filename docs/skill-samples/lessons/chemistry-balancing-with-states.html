Balance a chemical equation

Choose which number to change, prove that every element matches across the arrow, and show the physical state of each substance.

Before you start

In SO2, how many sulfur atoms and oxygen atoms are in one molecule?

Check the count

There is one sulfur atom and there are two oxygen atoms. A subscript is a small number after an element symbol. The 2 belongs to O. With no small number after S, count one S.

01 / Count

Change the number of groups

A chemical equation places the reactants (starting substances) to the left of the arrow and the products (substances formed) to the right. The formulas are fixed. Change a coefficient, the number in front, to multiply every atom count in that formula. A missing coefficient means 1.

Meet the oxygen pair

Given. Solid silver oxide breaks down into solid silver and oxygen gas. In O2, we count oxygen in two-atom molecules. Ag in solid silver counts atoms in an extended solid.

Goal. Make both elements match by changing only coefficients. Start with one of each formula.

A state symbol goes immediately after its formula. Here, (s) means solid and (g) means gas.

Ag2O(s) → Ag(s) + O2(g)

The left has one O atom per Ag2O unit. The right has two O atoms per O2 molecule. The first total both sides can reach is two. Predict. If you change the Ag2O coefficient to 2 and leave Ag at 1, which row will match? How much silver will you then need?

Ag2O(s) → Ag(s) + O2(g)

Trial totals in atoms
AtomLeftRight
Ag21
O12

Compare the trials

2Ag2O(s) → Ag(s) + O2(g)

O matches at two atoms on each side. Ag still differs, with four atoms on the left and one on the right.

Keep the two Ag2O units and change the Ag coefficient to 4. That adds Ag on the right while leaving both O totals alone.

Final. Four Ag atoms and two O atoms on each side. All formulas represent neutral substances, so total charge is zero on each side. The coefficients have no common divisor greater than 1.

2Ag2O(s) → 4Ag(s) + O2(g)

Try a different breakdown

Write the balanced equation for heated ammonium nitrite. Tell which element changes along with hydrogen when you adjust the amount of water.

NH4NO2(s) → N2(g) + H2O(g)

Compare your equation

NH4NO2(s) → N2(g) + 2H2O(g)

Two N atoms already match. Water needs a coefficient of 2 to match four H atoms, and that same change brings O to two on each side. If you saw only one N on the left, add the two appearances of N in NH4NO2.

Retry. If every coefficient in your balanced equation were tripled, what would happen to the atom counts and what would you write as the final ratio?

Check the retry

Each side would have 6 N, 12 H, and 6 O atoms. The totals still match. Divide all three coefficients by 3 to get the simplest ratio.

02 / Match

Read the size of each group

When one group already fits

Given. H2 and Cl2 form HCl. All three substances are gases at the stated conditions. Goal. Write the balanced equation with states.

H2(g) + Cl2(g) → HCl(g)

One H2 gives two H atoms. Each HCl takes one H, so try 2HCl. This also uses two Cl atoms, exactly one Cl2. There is no need to change either reactant coefficient.

H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g)

Put (g) after each gas. The state and the coefficient tell you different things. Labeling the state leaves the atom totals alone.

When both groups have to grow

Given. Solid white phosphorus and chlorine gas form phosphorus trichloride, collected as a liquid. Goal. Match both elements and show each state.

P4(s) + Cl2(g) → PCl3(l)

  1. Cl comes in groups of two on the left and three on the right. The first shared total is six Cl atoms. Try three Cl2 molecules and two PCl3 molecules.

    P4(s) + 3Cl2(g) → 2PCl3(l)

  2. That trial gives four P atoms on the left and two on the right. Double both Cl terms to keep Cl matched. The right then has four P atoms, enough to use one P4 molecule.

P4(s) + 6Cl2(g) → 4PCl3(l)

Check four P and twelve Cl atoms on each side. The product is labeled (l) because it is collected as a liquid. The two reactants keep their own states. In particular, a formula with two atoms can be a gas here, but a subscript never tells you the state.

Balance first, then write the states

Balance each equation on paper. Then use your own balanced equation to make a second version with states.

  • NO + O2 → NO2 For the second version, all substances are gases.
  • CS2 + H2O → CO2 + H2S For the second version, all substances are gases except the carbon disulfide (CS2) feed, which is liquid before it vaporizes.
Compare your notation

In the first equation, keep NO and NO2 at the same coefficient so N matches. Try two of each. O then totals 2 + 2 = 4 on the left and 2 × 2 = 4 on the right.

2NO(g) + O2(g) → 2NO2(g)

In the second, match two S atoms with two H2S. Match its four H atoms with two H2O. Check one C and two O atoms on both sides.

CS2(l) + 2H2O(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2S(g)

Apply the gas label to each term it describes. The stated exception is CS2, so water is still marked (g).

Retry. Write the second equation for conditions after the feed has vaporized, with the other states unchanged.

Check the retry

CS2(g) + 2H2O(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2S(g)

Only CS2 changes label. The coefficients and formulas still give the same atom counts.

03 / Track

When atoms are spread across terms

Given. In one overall reaction for making iron from iron oxide, CO is used up and CO2 forms. Goal. Find the smallest whole-number coefficients.

Fe2O3(s) + CO(g) → Fe(s) + CO2(g)

O is in two reactant formulas. Starting there means guessing more than one amount. Start where an element occurs in only one formula on each side.

  1. Match Fe. One Fe2O3 has two Fe atoms, so try 2Fe. Iron oxide is counted in formula units and solid Fe in atoms.

    Fe2O3(s) + CO(g) → 2Fe(s) + CO2(g)

  2. Keep C matched. Each CO and each CO2 contains one C. Use the same number of CO and CO2 molecules.
  3. Finish O. Each matched CO/CO2 pair puts one more O atom on the right than on the left. Three pairs account for the three O atoms that came from the oxide.

Fe2O3(s) + 3CO(g) → 2Fe(s) + 3CO2(g)

Final count in atoms
AtomLeftRight
Fe22
C33
O3 + 3 = 63 × 2 = 6

The charges total zero on each side and the 1 keeps the ratio in simplest form. These counts describe the overall reaction. They give no timing or steps for how the atoms move.

Keep a match while you change a trial

For sulfur dioxide made from solid pyrite, suppose a trial matches Fe and S as shown. Can you finish O by changing O2 alone to a whole number? Write the smallest whole-number equation and explain your move.

2FeS2(s) + O2(g) → Fe2O3(s) + 4SO2(g)

Compare your move

There are 3 + 4 × 2 = 11 O atoms on the right. O2 alone gives even totals. Double the already matched group of FeS2, Fe2O3 and SO2 terms, then use 11 O2 molecules for the new total of 22 O atoms.

4FeS2(s) + 11O2(g) → 2Fe2O3(s) + 8SO2(g)

If you double only one product, Fe or S stops matching. The finished counts are 4 Fe, 8 S, and 22 O on both sides. The coefficients share no divisor above 1.

Retry. In the trial Cu2S(s) + O2(g) → 2Cu(s) + SO2(g) should you double the matched group or keep it?

Check the retry

Keep it. Both sides already contain two Cu, one S, and two O atoms, so the trial is the finished equation.

04 / Transfer

Balance an air scrubber equation

Sodium peroxide and sodium carbonate are solids. Carbon dioxide and oxygen are gases.

Na2O2 + CO2 → Na2CO3 + O2

On paper, write the balanced equation with physical states. Explain why you started with your chosen element, what you kept matched while editing the equation, and how you checked your result.

Compare after you write it

Start with Na or C. Each occurs in one formula on each side. One Na2CO3 needs the Na from one Na2O2 and the C from one CO2. That trial leaves four O atoms on the left and three already in the carbonate. The spare O atom cannot make a whole O2 molecule.

Double the three linked amounts so there are two spare O atoms. One O2 now uses both. If you doubled only the carbonate, recheck the Na and C counts.

2Na2O2(s) + 2CO2(g) → 2Na2CO3(s) + O2(g)

Both sides have four Na, two C, eight O, and zero net charge. The 1 for O2 makes this the simplest whole-number ratio. Its (g) label comes from the stated conditions.

Retry. Close the comparison. Sketch your own three-row atom count for a different equation in this lesson. Show what would change if you increased just one coefficient, then repair the balance.

When you come back

Before rereading, sketch a trial equation and mark the one part of a term that you can change to balance atoms. Explain how you would spot a useful starting element and what you would count again after every move.

On a new problem, check each element on both sides, total charge, the simplest whole-number ratio, and whether each requested state comes from the stated conditions.

Glossary

Subscript
The small number after an element symbol or group in a formula. A missing subscript means one.
Chemical equation
A symbolic record of substances consumed and formed in a reaction.
Reactant
A starting substance, written to the left of the reaction arrow.
Product
A substance formed, written to the right of the arrow.
Coefficient
The number before a formula. It scales the whole formula. The coefficient 1 is usually left unwritten.
Formula unit
The smallest whole-number ratio of components shown by the formula of an ionic solid. It serves as a counting unit.
State symbol
A label placed after a formula. (s) means solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, and (aq) dissolved in water.
