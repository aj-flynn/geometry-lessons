Balance a chemical equation

Match each element’s atom count across the arrow. Then use the stated conditions to label each substance’s physical state.

Before you start, name the elements and count their atoms in one molecule of SO2. Use the first figure to check.

Open the glossary for words and symbols.

Count copies of the formula

A subscript, the small number inside a formula, gives the atom count for the element before it. In SO2, one molecule has one sulfur atom and two oxygen atoms. A coefficient, the number in front, multiplies the entire formula. A missing number in either position means one.

Predict what a third tile would add to each atom count. Then check the new oxygen total. If a check is unavailable, use the comparison below it.

Compare the third tile

Three SO2 molecules give 3 × 2 = 6 O atoms and 3 × 1 = 3 S atoms. The third tile adds two O atoms and one S atom. Nine would count both elements together.

Retry with a different formula

For 3N2O, predict the nitrogen and oxygen totals. Imagine a tile for one molecule, then draw all the copies.

Compare the retry

Each N2O tile has two N atoms and one O atom. 3N2O has 3 × 2 = 6 N atoms and 3 × 1 = 3 O atoms.

Build the ratio, then audit it

At the arrow, reactants go on the left and products on the right. In a balanced equation, each element has the same atom count on both sides. Adjust coefficients only. Changing a subscript changes what the formula describes.

Given. In one simplified pathway for roasting pyrite, the substances are fixed as shown.

FeS2 + O2 → Fe2O3 + SO2

Goal. Balance this equation in the smallest positive whole-number ratio.

  1. Match Fe first. Fe occurs in just one formula per side. One Fe2O3 formula unit has two Fe atoms, so start with 2FeS2. A formula unit is one count of the formula’s atom ratio for a solid like this.

  2. Recount S. 2FeS2 brings four S atoms. Use 4SO2 to match those four.

    2FeS2 + O2 → Fe2O3 + 4SO2

  3. Add both oxygen contributions. One Fe2O3 unit gives 3 O atoms. Four SO2 molecules give 4 × 2 = 8 O atoms. The total is 3 + 8 = 11 O atoms. Whole O2 molecules supply oxygen in pairs, so they cannot supply exactly 11 O atoms.

  4. Scale the matched terms together. Double the two product coefficients and the pyrite coefficient. Fe and S stay matched. Now the products contain 22 O atoms, so use 11 O2 molecules.

    4FeS2 + 11O2 → 2Fe2O3 + 8SO2

Final atom count for the written ratio
ElementLeftRight
Fe42 × 2 = 4
S4 × 2 = 88
O11 × 2 = 222 × 3 + 8 × 2 = 22

Every formula here is neutral, so the total charge is zero on each side. The coefficients 4, 11, 2, and 8 have no common factor greater than one. The balanced equation gives a ratio for this pathway. Other pathways can occur, and atom counts alone cannot tell which will occur.

Check a balanced equation that still needs work

4H2O2 → 4H2O + 2O2

For this decomposition, check whether the atoms match. Then write the ratio you would turn in.

Compare the ratio

Both sides have 8 H atoms and 8 O atoms. Divide every coefficient by 2 to get the smallest ratio.

2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2

Label the physical states

A state label in parentheses goes just after its formula. Read it from the conditions. It adds no atoms and leaves the coefficients unchanged. For the pyrite pathway above, take the iron compounds as solids and oxygen and sulfur dioxide as gases.

4FeS2(s) + 11O2(g) → 2Fe2O3(s) + 8SO2(g)

Here (s) means solid and (g) means gas. Use (l) for liquid and (aq) for a substance dissolved in water.

Choose the water term for peroxide decomposition

Start with the balanced equation below. The product water is recorded after it collects as a liquid. How should its term read?

2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2

Compare the water term

2H2O(l) preserves the two water molecules in the ratio and labels the given liquid. H2O(l) would change the atom count, and (aq) describes a substance dissolved in water.

Retry. Use that same balanced ratio. Write the full equation if the starting peroxide and product water are liquids and oxygen leaves as a gas.

Compare the three labels

2H2O2(l) → 2H2O(l) + O2(g)

Each formula carries its own state, including O2(g), whose coefficient of one is left unwritten.

Write your own equations

Use paper. For each case, write a complete balanced equation with states and make a quick atom tally. Compare after you’ve made an attempt.

A. Oxidizing nitric oxide

In this equation, all three substances are gases.

NO + O2 → NO2

Compare your equation

2NO(g) + O2(g) → 2NO2(g)

Match N by using the same number of copies of NO and NO2. Each copy of NO2 needs one more O atom than a copy of NO supplies. Two extra O atoms come from one O2. The totals are 2 N and 4 O atoms on each side.

B. Oxidizing ammonia

Ammonia and oxygen enter as gases. The reaction forms nitric oxide gas and water vapor. Write the equation for these conditions. Then write it for a description in which only the product water is recorded after it condenses.

NH3 + O2 → NO + H2O

Compare your equations

Start with two NH3 molecules to give six H atoms, enough for three whole H2O molecules. That sets 2NO for the two N atoms. The products now need 2 + 3 = 5 O atoms. Double the three matched terms, then supply the ten O atoms with 5O2. The final totals are 4 N, 12 H, and 10 O atoms on each side, with zero total charge on each side. There is no common factor greater than one.

4NH3(g) + 5O2(g) → 4NO(g) + 6H2O(g)

4NH3(g) + 5O2(g) → 4NO(g) + 6H2O(l)

The second line is a description of the same overall atom ratio with a different recorded state for the water. Condensing that product changes only its state label.

Take the method back

  1. Copy the formulas and arrow. Identify which coefficients you can change.
  2. Start with an element in one term on each side. Recount shared elements after changes, summing contributions from every term on that side.
  3. Check every element, total charge, and any common factor. Add states from the stated conditions.

Next time, recall what an odd total tells you when the only available source of those atoms supplies pairs. Explain what must stay matched as you fix it.

Ask in the private chat if you get stuck. Describe the first atom count that you couldn’t make agree.

Glossary

Subscript
A lowered number that belongs to a formula and gives an atom count for the preceding element or a multiplier for the preceding parenthesized group.
Coefficient
The number in front of a formula that gives its relative amount. On the particle-count scale, it counts copies of the complete formula.
Reactants and products
Reactants are the starting substances, on the left. The arrow points to products, the substances formed, on the right.
Balanced equation
A chemical equation with the same total number of atoms of each element and the same total electric charge on both sides. Report the coefficients in the smallest positive whole-number ratio for the reactions here.
Formula unit
The entity counted using a formula’s atom ratio. A formula unit of a solid like iron(III) oxide describes a ratio within the solid rather than a separate molecule.
State label
A notation after a formula that gives its physical state or indicates that it is dissolved in water. The labels are (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, and (aq) aqueous or dissolved in water.
