Balance a chemical equation

Use coefficients (numbers in front of formulas) to show how much of each substance reacts or forms. Then use state labels such as (g) for gas to record the form of each substance under the given conditions.

Open the glossary for the notation in this lesson.

Before you start

In 3CO2, carbon dioxide is represented by three molecules, each a group of bonded atoms. Say how many carbon atoms and how many oxygen atoms this represents. Which number tells you the oxygen count per molecule?

Compare your count

There are 3 carbon atoms and 6 oxygen atoms. The subscript (small lower number) 2 after O tells you there are 2 oxygen atoms in each molecule. Each molecule also has 1 carbon atom, indicated by C with no subscript.

Count both sides

The arrow points from the reactants (what reacts) to the products (what forms). In an ordinary chemical reaction atoms rearrange, so a balanced equation has equal counts of each element on the two sides. For each element, multiply the count in one formula by the coefficient. Add those contributions when the element appears in several formulas on a side.

Changing the coefficient repeats the same group. Changing the subscript changes the formula itself. An unwritten coefficient is 1. For an ionic solid such as Na2CO3, a formula unit records the simplest ratio of its ions (charged atoms or groups) in the solid. In the count, one formula unit contributes 2 Na, 1 C, and 3 O.

Match oxygen in pairs

Given. Liquid phosphorus trichloride reacts with oxygen gas to make liquid phosphorus oxychloride. Goal. Find the smallest whole-number coefficients for the given formulas.

PCl3(l) + O2(g) → POCl3(l)

  1. With all coefficients at 1, P and Cl already match. Oxygen comes in pairs on the left and appears once per product molecule on the right. With one product molecule, the right has an odd O count. An integer number of oxygen molecules supplies an even count.
  2. Try 2 product molecules, so the right has 2 oxygen atoms. One oxygen molecule on the left already supplies those 2 O atoms.

    PCl3(l) + O2(g) → 2POCl3(l)

  3. The right now has 2 P and 6 Cl atoms. Each phosphorus trichloride molecule contributes 1 P and 3 Cl. Using 2 such molecules matches both counts.

    2PCl3(l) + O2(g) → 2POCl3(l)

  4. Check P, Cl and O. Each side has 2 P, 6 Cl and 2 O atoms. Both sides have total charge zero because the formulas here are neutral. The ratio 2 to 1 to 2 is already the smallest whole-number ratio.

Compare the trials on paper

Write down P, Cl and O counts on each side with all coefficients at 1. Then change only the coefficient of phosphorus trichloride to 2 and recount. Reset to one of each and choose a different first change to match O.

First trial, P is 1 to 1, Cl is 3 to 3 and O is 2 to 1. Second trial, P is 2 to 1, Cl is 6 to 3 and O is still 2 to 1. Phosphorus trichloride contributes no O. After resetting, double the product to match O. Recheck P and Cl before deciding how to finish.

When you change a coefficient, check every element in that formula before deciding what to change next. The count model reports proportions. It leaves out reaction speed, conditions and intermediate steps.

Follow a count through several formulas

Given. Heating solid baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) produces solid sodium carbonate, carbon dioxide gas and water vapor. Goal. Balance the equation.

NaHCO3(s) → Na2CO3(s) + CO2(g) + H2O(g)

  1. O appears in three products, so start with Na, which appears in just one formula on each side. One unit of sodium carbonate contains 2 Na, so use 2 units of sodium bicarbonate on the left.

    2NaHCO3(s) → Na2CO3(s) + CO2(g) + H2O(g)

  2. That change supplies 2 H atoms. One water molecule uses both. The 2 C atoms on the left match 1 in sodium carbonate plus 1 in carbon dioxide.
  3. Check the distributed O count. The left has 2 × 3 = 6 O atoms. On the right, 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 O atoms.
  4. Each side has 2 Na, 2 H, 2 C and 6 O atoms, and each solid formula unit and gas molecule is neutral overall. The coefficients 2, 1, 1, 1 have no common divisor greater than 1.

Put state labels after formulas

Read the conditions for each substance. Use (s) for solid, (l) for liquid and (g) for gas. Use (aq) when the substance is dissolved in water. A state label follows the whole formula and contributes zero atoms to the count.

Given. In an overall description of methanol synthesis, carbon monoxide gas and hydrogen gas yield methanol vapor. Goal. Balance with these states. Then record the same overall material change with methanol collected as liquid.

CO(g) + H2(g) → CH3OH(g)

C and O already match at 1 atom each on both sides. The methanol molecule contains 4 H atoms (3 + 1). Two hydrogen molecules supply 2 × 2 = 4 H atoms on the left.

CO(g) + 2H2(g) → CH3OH(g)

The final counts are 1 C, 4 H and 1 O per side, with zero total charge and the simplest ratio 1 to 2 to 1. With liquid methanol as the collected product, change its state label to (l).

CO(g) + 2H2(g) → CH3OH(l)

The coefficients and subscripts stay fixed. This second line includes collection after cooling. The equation summarizes the materials before and after, leaving out the cooling step.

Compare your edit on paper

Choose among 2I2(g), I2(g) and I(g). The correct edit is I2(g). It keeps the formula and coefficient for iodine. The first choice doubles the iodine count by adding a molecule. The third choice uses a single atom per particle. Try explaining why the other labels stay the same.

Balance on your own

For each case, write the balanced equation using the given formulas, then rewrite it with a state label after each formula. Use the smallest positive whole-number coefficients. Explain your first decision, and give the evidence you used to check the finished equation. Work in the response spaces or on paper before opening a comparison.

Case A · Making synthesis gas

Methane and steam react to produce carbon monoxide and hydrogen. In this description all four substances are gases.

CH4 + H2O → CO + H2

Compare case A

CH4 + H2O → CO + 3H2

CH4(g) + H2O(g) → CO(g) + 3H2(g)

One of each formula already matches C and O. The left supplies 4 + 2 = 6 H atoms, so 3 hydrogen molecules match the right to the left. Both sides have 1 C, 1 O, 6 H and zero total charge. The coefficients 1, 1, 1, 3 are the simplest ratio. Steam here is water vapor, so water takes (g).

If you used a subscript of 6 to fix H, retry using a count of whole hydrogen molecules.

Case B · Roasting an ore

For one pathway of pyrite roasting, solid iron disulfide and oxygen gas yield solid iron(III) oxide and sulfur dioxide gas. Use this pathway as given.

FeS2 + O2 → Fe2O3 + SO2

Compare case B

4FeS2 + 11O2 → 2Fe2O3 + 8SO2

4FeS2(s) + 11O2(g) → 2Fe2O3(s) + 8SO2(g)

One unit of iron(III) oxide needs 2 units of iron disulfide to match Fe. Those 2 units supply 4 S atoms, so use 4 sulfur dioxide molecules. That puts 3 + 4 × 2 = 11 O atoms on the right, while the oxygen gas on the left supplies atoms in pairs.

Double the linked Fe and S amounts together so the right needs 22 O atoms. Use 11 oxygen molecules. The result has 4 Fe, 8 S and 22 O atoms per side. Both sides have zero total charge, and 4, 11, 2, 8 share no divisor greater than 1.

If you changed only one product coefficient to make O even, retry from Fe and S and see which of those rows the change broke.

Check your next equation

Before opening this lesson again, recall what a number in front of a formula changes. For a fresh equation, choose a starting element and say why it is a useful first count. Check each atom count, total charge, the smallest ratio and each state label against the conditions.

If one row disagrees, trace it back to each formula that contributes atoms to that row. Ask about the first point where your counts diverge in the private chat.

Glossary

Reactants and products
In the written direction, the formulas before → are the reactants and the formulas after → are the products.
Balanced equation
An equation with the same total number of atoms of each element and the same total electric charge on each side.
Coefficient
The multiplier in front of the entire formula. A coefficient of 1 is usually left unwritten.
Subscript
The small lower number after an element symbol or group. It gives that element or group's count in the formula. An unwritten subscript is 1.
Molecule
A distinct group of bonded atoms. One oxygen molecule has formula O2 and contains two oxygen atoms.
Formula unit
The smallest whole-number ratio written for an ionic compound. An ion is an atom or group with electric charge. The formula counts a ratio of ions in the solid.
State label
A symbol in parentheses after the formula. (s) means solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas and (aq) dissolved in water.

Read a little more

See how letters and coefficients stand for an entire reaction in the IUPAC Gold Book entry on stoichiometric numbers. A stoichiometric number uses a minus sign for reactants to show they are consumed. The coefficients written in the equation are positive.
