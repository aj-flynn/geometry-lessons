Chemistry · Chemical equations

Balance a chemical equation

Find the smallest whole-number ratio that accounts for every atom. Then show the physical state of each substance when it is given.

Read one formula first

In Fe2O3, how many iron atoms and oxygen atoms does one unit of the formula count?

Check the small numbers

A subscript is the small number after an element symbol. One Fe2O3 formula unit counts two iron atoms and three oxygen atoms. A symbol without a subscript counts one atom per unit.

Use the glossary if a symbol or term is unfamiliar.

01 · Count

Scale the whole formula

A coefficient is the number in front of a formula. It multiplies the count for every element in that formula. When no coefficient is written, use 1. Changing a subscript changes the substance described by the formula.

02 · Balance

Follow a change across the equation

Given. In a simplified model of roasting pyrite, the reactants are the starting substances, FeS2 and O2. The products are the substances formed, Fe2O3 and SO2. The arrow points from reactants to products. Keep these formulas and their sides fixed.

Goal. Change coefficients until each element has the same atom count on both sides. A chemical reaction rearranges atoms and preserves the count of each element. Use the smallest whole-number ratio, with no whole number greater than 1 dividing all coefficients.

  1. Match iron and sulfur first. Start with one Fe2O3. Its two Fe atoms require 2FeS2. That gives 2 × 2 = 4 S atoms on the left, so use 4SO2 on the right.

  2. Add oxygen from both products. Fe2O3 contributes 3 O atoms and 4SO2 contributes 4 × 2 = 8 O atoms. That gives 3 + 8 = 11 O atoms on the right.

Test one change

2FeS2 + 5O2 → Fe2O3 + 4SO2

Five O2 units give 10 O atoms. Predict the right-side Fe, S and O totals if you double just Fe2O3. Which row that already matches will need attention?

On the right, Fe changes from 2 to 4. O changes from 11 to 6 + 8 = 14. S stays at 4. This gives an even oxygen total, but Fe now differs across the arrow. If you chose 7O2 at this point, only oxygen and sulfur would match.

Before continuing, try raising Fe2O3 to 3 in the original trial. Which atoms does this add on the right?

Compare the retry

3Fe2O3 gives 6 Fe and 9 O from that formula. With the 8 O atoms in 4SO2, the right side has 6 Fe, 4 S and 17 O atoms.

  1. Scale the linked counts together. O2 supplies pairs, so no whole coefficient can supply 11 O atoms. Double the FeS2, Fe2O3 and SO2 coefficients together to keep Fe and S matched and make the oxygen total even.

  2. Set the oxygen coefficient last. Each O2 unit supplies two atoms. Match the 22 on the right with 22 ÷ 2 = 11 units of O2 on the left.

4FeS2 + 11O2 → 2Fe2O3 + 8SO2

Each side has 4 Fe, 8 S and 22 O atoms. All written species are neutral, so each side also has zero net charge. The coefficients 4, 11, 2 and 8 have no common divisor greater than 1. The equation describes amounts for the stated reaction. It does not show the reaction steps or how quickly the reaction occurs.

03 · Label

Attach the given states

A state symbol goes immediately after the formula it describes. Write (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas and (aq) for dissolved in water. Read the conditions in the prompt. The formula and coefficient alone do not tell you the state.

When carbon monoxide, oxygen and carbon dioxide are all gases, write (g) three times. The atom counts stay the same.

2CO(g) + O2(g) → 2CO2(g)

When solid calcium carbonate yields solid calcium oxide and carbon dioxide gas, put each state next to its own formula.

CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)

Try a different state

Solid mercury(II) oxide decomposes. In the product report, the collected mercury is liquid and the oxygen is gas. Write the full equation before checking the state of Hg.

2HgO → 2Hg + O2

Which notation describes the collected mercury?

Compare the reported states

2HgO(s) → 2Hg(l) + O2(g)

Use (l) for the liquid mercury. The solid label belongs only to HgO, and (aq) would require mercury to be dissolved in water. The states leave two Hg atoms and two O atoms on each side.

04 · Transfer

Write the equations yourself

Use paper. For each equation, write the smallest whole-number coefficients and check all atom counts. Include state symbols where the conditions give them.

  1. Ammonia can react with oxygen to form nitric oxide and water. Balance the given formulas. Mark your first coefficient change and explain which count you checked next.

    NH3 + O2 → NO + H2O

    Now rewrite the balanced equation for a report that tracks the same reaction amounts after cooling the products. In the report, every substance is a gas except the collected liquid water.

  2. Nitric oxide reacts with oxygen to form nitrogen dioxide. In this case, all substances are gases.

    NO + O2 → NO2

  3. Hydrogen peroxide dissolved in water decomposes to liquid water and oxygen gas.

    H2O2 → H2O + O2

Compare after trying all three

  1. One route starts with hydrogen. Two NH3 units supply 6 H atoms, so use 3H2O. Two N atoms require 2NO. The product oxygen count is 2 + 3 = 5, so double these linked amounts to make ten O atoms on the right. Use 5O2 on the left.

    4NH3 + 5O2 → 4NO + 6H2O

    Each side counts 4 N, 12 H and 10 O atoms. If you got 2O2 or 3O2 after the first H match, check oxygen from both products and make their combined count even.

    4NH3(g) + 5O2(g) → 4NO(g) + 6H2O(l)

    Keep the ratio for the report. Apply the gas rule to three formulas and the liquid exception to water alone.

  2. 2NO(g) + O2(g) → 2NO2(g)

    Each side counts 2 N and 4 O atoms. Include oxygen from both reactants. Each of the three formulas gets (g).

  3. 2H2O2(aq) → 2H2O(l) + O2(g)

    Each side counts 4 H and 4 O atoms. (aq) describes the peroxide dissolved in water. (l) describes the liquid water produced.

The species are neutral in all three examples. Each ratio has no common divisor greater than 1. An unwritten coefficient counts as 1.

Take the method back to your work

  1. Keep every given formula and its side of the arrow fixed.
  2. Count coefficient × subscript for each term. Add an element’s contributions on each side.
  3. Look for an element in just one formula on each side to start. When an element is supplied in pairs, check whether the opposite total is even. Recount every row a move touches.
  4. Check each element, net charge and the smallest whole-number ratio. Then append the states where given.

On your next visit, recall why doubling one coefficient can break a match. Then balance the equation below on paper and say which element you chose first.

CaO + HCl → CaCl2 + H2O

Compare the recall attempt

CaO + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O

One route starts with Cl. The two Cl atoms in CaCl2 require 2HCl, which also gives the two H atoms in H2O. Both sides now count 1 Ca, 1 O, 2 H and 2 Cl atoms and have zero net charge. The other coefficients can stay at 1, so the ratio cannot be reduced.

Glossary

Subscript
The small number giving the count of an element in one unit of the formula. With no written subscript, the count is one.
Formula unit
The group of atoms counted by the formula for a compound such as solid iron(III) oxide.
Coefficient
The number before a formula that scales all of its counts. An omitted coefficient means one.
Reactant
A starting substance written to the left of the arrow.
Product
A substance formed, written to the right of the arrow.
Smallest whole-number ratio
A set of whole-number coefficients with no common divisor greater than one.
State symbol
Parentheses after a formula indicating solid (s), liquid (l), gas (g) or dissolved in water (aq) under the given conditions.
