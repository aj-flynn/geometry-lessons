01 · Read the formula

Make an atom inventory

An atom inventory is a count of each kind of atom. Start by reading one formula. In NO2, the small 2 is a subscript that belongs to O. One molecule contains one nitrogen atom and two oxygen atoms. A coefficient goes in front of a whole formula. With 2 NO2, you have two molecules, so both atom counts double. A missing number in either position means 1.

On an equation’s left are the reactants, the substances that go into a reaction. On the right are the products, the substances it yields. We’ll use known formulas and adjust the coefficients. Editing a subscript changes what the formula describes. The letter in parentheses describes the state, such as (g) for gas. It never enters the count.

Watch one gas molecule split

Track the direction in which gaseous N2O4 splits into NO2. Nitrogen appears in just one formula on each side. One starting molecule has two N atoms, so the nitrogen count sets the product coefficient to 2. Then check what happens to the oxygen atoms.

N2O4(g) → 2 NO2(g)

Imagine a sealed sample. If the diagram showed only one NO2 afterward and no other product, which tiles from the starting sample would be unaccounted for? Why would you question that drawing even if the number of outlines on both sides matched?

Predict, then test a coefficient

Start with one NO2 molecule on the product side. Predict how changing its coefficient from 1 to 2 will affect both rows. Then predict what a third molecule would do. Which setting accounts for the entire starting inventory?

02 · Look inside parentheses

Track a group only while it stays intact

In Ca(OH)2, the outside subscript 2 applies to both symbols inside the parentheses. A formula unit of this ionic compound gives the ratio of ions, which are charged particles. Here, one calcium ion corresponds to two hydroxide ions. A solid crystal contains a repeating arrangement of ions. The formula gives a ratio within the solid.

Compare the same group in two reactions

Ca(OH)2(aq) + MgCl2(aq) → Mg(OH)2(s) + CaCl2(aq)

In this precipitation equation, dissolved ions form a solid, magnesium hydroxide. Each hydroxide group contains one O and one H, and those elements occur only in hydroxide on both sides. Count the group as a shortcut. There are 2 OH groups on each side. Then Ca, Mg and Cl give counts of 1, 1 and 2 on each side. All coefficients can stay at 1.

Ca(OH)2(s) + NH4Cl(s) → CaCl2(s) + NH3(g) + H2O(g)

In this equation for a heated solid mixture, the product side contains water and ammonia. There’s no intact OH group to track. Keep the formulas and balance their individual atoms.

  1. Start with 1 Ca(OH)2. One Ca atom calls for 1 CaCl2.
  2. One CaCl2 needs 2 Cl atoms, so use 2 NH4Cl.
  3. Those two units supply 2 N atoms, so use 2 NH3.
  4. The 2 O atoms on the left call for 2 H2O on the right.
  5. Check H across all its sources. The left has 2 + 2 × 4 = 10 H atoms. The right has 2 × 3 + 2 × 2 = 10 H atoms.

Ca(OH)2(s) + 2 NH4Cl(s) → CaCl2(s) + 2 NH3(g) + 2 H2O(g)

Final counts on each side are Ca 1, Cl 2, N 2, O 2 and H 10. The state labels describe the substances under the stated conditions. (s) is solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, and (aq) dissolved in water.

Check a new group

One formula unit of Al2(SO4)3 contains three sulfate groups. Sketch or list the groups in 2 Al2(SO4)3, then count its O atoms. Explain which numbers apply to O.

Compare your count

Two formula units contain 2 × 3 = 6 sulfate groups. Each has 4 O atoms, giving 6 × 4 = 24 O atoms. A count of 12 would cover just one formula unit. A count of 8 would omit the three groups within each formula. Retry with 3 Al2(SO4)3 and count S as well.

Three formula units give 9 S atoms and 36 O atoms.

03 · Choose what to fix first

Use an anchor, then solve the linked rows

An anchor is a row that ties coefficients together with few terms. With one formula on each side, it gives a direct ratio. When an element occurs in several formulas, it often helps to use other rows to determine some of those coefficients first.

When oxygen comes in pairs

C3H7OH(l) + O2(g) → CO2(g) + H2O(g)

Given the formulas for complete combustion of propanol, its reaction with enough oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water. Goal the smallest positive whole-number coefficients. Oxygen appears in all four formulas, so starting with it would give a row with four unknown counts.

  1. Try 1 propanol molecule. Its 3 C atoms set the CO2 coefficient to 3.
  2. Its 7 + 1 = 8 H atoms set the H2O coefficient to 4.
  3. The products need 3 × 2 + 4 = 10 O atoms. Propanol supplies 1, leaving 9 O atoms for O2.
  4. An odd number of O atoms can’t come from whole pairs. The trial ratio uses 9 ÷ 2 = 4½ O2 per propanol. Double every coefficient so this becomes a whole-molecule ratio and the C and H rows stay matched.

2 C3H7OH(l) + 9 O2(g) → 6 CO2(g) + 8 H2O(g)

Final atom inventory for propanol combustion

C2 × 3 = 66 × 1 = 6
H2 × 8 = 168 × 2 = 16
O2 × 1 + 9 × 2 = 206 × 2 + 8 × 1 = 20

The coefficients share no whole-number divisor greater than 1. If they did, dividing every coefficient by that number would leave all atom inventories matched at a smaller scale.

Now compare ethanol, C2H5OH, with the same complete combustion products. One ethanol sets 2 CO2 and 3 H2O. The products need 2 × 2 + 3 = 7 O atoms. Subtract the one in ethanol and 6 O atoms remain. Three O2 already gives a whole-number ratio. The cue for doubling is the number of atoms still needed from pairs.

When several rows constrain the same coefficient

Cl2(g) + KOH(aq) → KCl(aq) + KClO3(aq) + H2O(l)

In hot concentrated potassium hydroxide solution, chlorine forms two chlorine-containing products in this equation. Starting from chlorine alone leaves two product coefficients to choose. Use the O and H inventories to tie those choices down.

  1. Try 1 KClO3. Call the number of KOH units b and the number of water molecules w.
  2. H row gives b = 2w because each KOH supplies one H and each water contains two.
  3. O row gives b = 3 + w because the chlorate contributes three O and water contributes one per molecule.
  4. So 2w = 3 + w, giving w = 3 and b = 6. The two rows must hold at once.
  5. Six K atoms need six formula units containing K. One is KClO3, leaving 5 KCl. These two product terms together need 6 Cl atoms, supplied by 3 Cl2.

3 Cl2(g) + 6 KOH(aq) → 5 KCl(aq) + KClO3(aq) + 3 H2O(l)

The total atom counts on each side are Cl 6, K 6, O 6 and H 6. All written formulas here are electrically neutral overall. If an equation lists individual ions, compare their total electric charge on both sides in a separate row as well.

04 · Work from the formulas

Choose your own first move

For each case, mark a useful starting row or group. Write the complete equation with the smallest positive whole-number coefficients, and tally every element. Before comparing, explain how a change in your first coefficient would affect a second one.

A · Peroxide breakdown

H2O2(aq) → H2O(l) + O2(g)

Compare case A

The H row ties the first two coefficients together. One peroxide molecule makes one water molecule, using one of the two O atoms. The remaining one would give ½ O2. Multiply each trial coefficient by 2 to get 2 H2O2(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + O2(g). The counts are 4 H and 4 O on each side. If you tried 4, 4 and 2, divide all three coefficients by 2.

B · Sulfate precipitation

(NH4)2SO4(aq) + Ba(NO3)2(aq) → BaSO4(s) + NH4NO3(aq)

Compare case B

Starting with one BaSO4 fixes one barium nitrate by the Ba count and one ammonium sulfate by the sulfate count. Two ammonium groups and two nitrate groups on the left are matched by 2 NH4NO3 on the right. In order the coefficients are 1, 1, 1 and 2. The full totals are Ba 1, N 4, H 8, S 1 and O 10 on each side. Changing the subscript on nitrate would change the formula and also leave the other atom counts unchecked.

C · Carbonate and acid

Li2CO3(s) + HCl(aq) → LiCl(aq) + CO2(g) + H2O(l)

Compare case C

One lithium carbonate sets 2 LiCl by the Li row and 1 CO2 by the C row. Two chlorides call for 2 HCl, whose H atoms give 1 H2O. The coefficients in order are 1, 2, 2, 1 and 1. Each side has Li 2, C 1, O 3, H 2 and Cl 2. Carbonate splits across the products, so count the O in both carbon dioxide and water.

D · Sulfur recovery

In this simplified overall equation, report the recovered, cooled sulfur as S8.

H2S(g) + SO2(g) → S8(s) + H2O(g)

Compare case D

Start with one SO2. Its two O atoms set 2 H2O, whose four H atoms set 2 H2S. Now count S from both reactants. Three S atoms give a trial coefficient of 3/8 for S8. Multiply every coefficient by 8 to give 16, 8, 3 and 16 in written order. Each side has 32 H, 24 S and 16 O atoms. The 3 and 8 share no whole-number divisor greater than 1. Changing S8 to S3 would change the specified sulfur molecule.

Explain a choice. In which case would counting a group speed things up? In which would it conceal an atom split? Choose one equation and explain what your inventory would expose if a product coefficient were too small. If a row disagrees, revisit the first step that fixed one of its terms, then check every row again.

05 · Return to the method

Recall it with the page closed

Before reopening this lesson, write down which numbers you can change, how to expand a group, and what to do when one row needs an odd number from pairs. Try a different starting point. For the reaction between zinc sulfide and oxygen, start from two ZnS units instead of one. Can you get the final ratio without a fractional step? Reopen the zinc sulfide example to compare the steps.

When you return to your own equations, use a blank inventory with one row per element. Mark where each element occurs before choosing a coefficient. When explaining an equation, point to a specific count and say what would be unaccounted for if that count failed.

Glossary

Atom inventory
A separate total for each element, made by adding its counts from every formula on one side of an equation.
Chemical formula and subscript
A formula uses symbols and subscripts to show a substance’s composition. A subscript is the small number specifying how many atoms or groups of the preceding kind occur in one formula.
Coefficient
A number before a formula. It multiplies the count for the entire formula.
Molecule and formula unit
A molecule is a distinct set of bonded atoms. A formula unit of an ionic solid represents the smallest whole-number ratio of its ions.
Ion
An atom or group of atoms with a net electric charge.
Reactant, product, and state label
A reactant goes into the reaction and a product is formed. A state label tells how a substance is present, such as (g) for gas or (aq) for dissolved in water.
Anchor
A starting count that gives a useful restriction on one or more coefficients.
Precipitation
The formation of a solid from substances in solution.
