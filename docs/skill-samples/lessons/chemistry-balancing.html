Balance reactions with an atom ledger

Choose which atom count to start with, then track what each change does to the rest of the equation.

Before you start, read 3 CO2 aloud. Which number counts molecules, and which counts O atoms in each molecule? Check your reading in the first section.

Read the atom ledger

A coefficient is the number in front of a formula. It multiplies the whole formula. A subscript is the small number in a formula. It helps specify the composition of the substance. An unwritten coefficient or subscript counts as 1. In 3 CO2, three molecules contribute 3 × 2 = 6 oxygen atoms and 3 × 1 = 3 carbon atoms. The counts here are atoms, grouped by element.

The arrow points from reactants, the substances used, to products, the substances formed. Keep the formulas fixed when you balance a supplied skeleton equation, which lists the reactants and products before the quantities are settled. Track each element on its own row in an atom ledger, a table of left and right atom counts. The state symbol (g) means gas. Other state symbols are listed below.

2 O3(g) → 3 O2(g)

Predict before testing. If the right side changes from three O2 molecules to four while the left stays fixed, which side will show an oxygen surplus? Would replacing O2 with O3 keep the same product?

Compare the predictions

Two molecules give 4 O atoms, three give 6, and four give 8. The left keeps 6. At four molecules the right has two O atoms beyond what the reactants supply. O3 would specify ozone in place of oxygen gas.

Try again with two O2 molecules. Say what changes and what stays fixed. Replacing O2 with O3 would specify a different product.

Match the packs

Start with an element that occurs in one term per side. A term is one formula and its coefficient. If one count is a multiple of the other, the larger pack gives you a trial total. If neither count divides the other, use a common multiple, a number both pack sizes divide into evenly.

One pack size fits inside the other

Given lithium metal and fluorine gas forming solid lithium fluoride, find the smallest whole-number coefficients.

Li(s) + F2(g) → LiF(s)

  1. Fluorine comes in pairs on the left and one F per formula unit on the right. For one F2 molecule use two LiF formula units. A formula unit represents the simplest composition ratio of an ionic solid. Here that ratio is one Li to one F.
  2. Two LiF units require two Li atoms. Write 2 in front of Li.

2 Li(s) + F2(g) → 2 LiF(s)

Follow atoms through groups

A subscript outside parentheses multiplies each atom count inside. For example, one Al2(SO4)3 formula unit contains two Al, three S and twelve O. If every S is accounted for in SO4 groups on both sides, count those groups together in a trial row. Any additional O atoms still belong in the O check. When a group splits across formulas, keep separate element rows. This shortcut is bookkeeping for the written formulas.

Open a group that splits across products

Given the skeleton for heated lead(II) nitrate, find the smallest whole-number coefficients.

Pb(NO3)2(s) → PbO(s) + NO2(g) + O2(g)

  1. Start with one lead nitrate unit. The two nitrate groups supply two N and six O atoms. There are no nitrate groups in the product formulas.
  2. One Pb atom sets one PbO unit. Two N atoms set two NO2 molecules.
  3. Those products account for 1 + 2 × 2 = 5 O atoms. The remaining one O atom per trial unit would require a coefficient of ½ for O2.

Solve linked counts

When an element appears in several formulas on one or both sides, total its contributions. Keep track of whether a coefficient can move on its own or must change other counts. These two skeletons differ in the oxygen source. State symbols are omitted for the hot reaction mixtures.

Oxygen comes from a term you can adjust on its own

Given the first skeleton, find the smallest whole-number coefficients.

MnO2 + KOH + O2 → K2MnO4 + H2O

  1. Try one K2MnO4. The Mn row sets one MnO2.
  2. The two K atoms set two KOH. The two H from those KOH set one H2O.
  3. Count 4 O from MnO2 and KOH together, and 5 O in the products. O2 contributes only to O, so assign it ½ in the trial.
  4. Double the entire ratio to get integers and verify the linked rows.

The oxygen source also changes K and Cl

Given the second skeleton, find the smallest whole-number coefficients.

MnO2 + KOH + KClO3 → K2MnO4 + KCl + H2O

Predict whether changing only the KClO3 coefficient can fix a deficit of O while leaving K and Cl balanced. Follow the linked counts to test your prediction.

  1. Let x be the K2MnO4 coefficient. Mn sets the MnO2 coefficient to x as well.
  2. Let y be the KClO3 coefficient. Cl sets the KCl coefficient to y. Changing KClO3 alone would disturb Cl and K.
  3. There are y K atoms in KClO3 and y in KCl. Those contributions cancel in the K equality, so KOH must supply the remaining 2x K atoms. Its coefficient is 2x.
  4. Those KOH units supply 2x H atoms. Water accounts for two H per molecule, so its coefficient is x.
  5. In O atoms the ledger gives 2x + 2x + 3y = 4x + x. Subtract 4x from both sides to get 3y = x. Choose y = 1 and x = 3 for the smallest positive integers.

Mixed transfer

Write the smallest whole-number coefficients for the given sulfur recovery skeleton. Use S8(s) for the collected sulfur after cooling. Give the final equation and a short explanation of the first count you chose and how it constrained another coefficient. Test your own method before opening the hints.

H2S(g) + SO2(g) → S8(s) + H2O(g)

Positive whole-number coefficients
Hint after an attempt

H and O are each present in one formula per side. Can the water coefficient satisfy both constraints at once?

Compare the reasoning after an attempt

Write a, b, c and d for the four coefficients in order. In H atoms, 2a = 2d, so a = d. In O atoms, 2b = d. Thus a = d = 2b. The left S count is a + b = 3b, so the S row requires 3b = 8c. The least positive total divisible by both 3 and 8 is 24 S atoms. It gives b = 8 and c = 3, then a = d = 16.

16 H2S(g) + 8 SO2(g) → 3 S8(s) + 16 H2O(g)

Recall the decision

On a later visit, before opening the lesson, sketch a ledger with an element that appears in two products. Explain what you multiply and what you add. Then write how you would tell whether changing one coefficient can fix only that row. Afterward, check the method on the carbon capture or sulfur recovery example.

If you get stuck on a different equation, ask about the row where a change starts to disturb another count.

Glossary

Atom ledger
A tally of the atoms of each element represented on the two sides of a reaction arrow.
Coefficient
The number in front of a formula. In these equations it scales the amount of the entire substance and all of its atom counts.
Subscript
A small number that specifies a count in a formula. When it follows a parenthesis, it multiplies the whole parenthesized group.
Skeleton equation
The specified reactant and product formulas before their amounts are balanced.
Reactant and product
A reactant is used up to the extent the reaction proceeds. A product forms as the reaction proceeds.
Molecule and formula unit
A molecule is a distinct bonded group of atoms. A formula unit for an ionic solid gives the simplest ratio of ions in the extended solid.
State symbols
(s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas and (aq) dissolved in water. They describe the form of the substance and contribute no atoms.
Common multiple
A number that each of the given whole numbers divides evenly.
