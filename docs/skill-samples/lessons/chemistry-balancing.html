Balance a chemical equation

Count each kind of atom on both sides. Find the smallest whole-number ratio that makes the counts agree.

Before you start. In CO2, how many C atoms and O atoms are in one molecule? What about two molecules?

Use the small number, called a subscript, to read one molecule. An element symbol with no subscript counts as one. Then count another copy of the same molecule. Compare your counts.

1. Change the number of copies

Starting equation

NO(g) + O2(g) → NO2(g)

Reactants, the starting substances, go on the left. The arrow points toward the products, the substances formed. The state label (g) means gas. Keep the formulas and state labels as given.

An atom count starts with copies. A coefficient in front of a formula counts copies of that whole formula. A blank coefficient means one. The starting equation has one N atom on each side, but the left has three O atoms and the right has two.

Predict on paper. If the coefficient of NO changes from 1 to 2, how many N and O atoms will be on each side? Which counts will change?

Next, also add one NO2 on the product side, keeping O2 at one. Record the totals for both moves. Use the trial controls when available, or calculate each trial on paper. Then compare both moves.

2. Follow atoms when a group splits

Making ammonia by heating two solids

Ca(OH)2(s) + NH4Cl(s) → CaCl2(s) + NH3(g) + H2O(g)

Goal: find the smallest whole-number coefficients. The same five elements must match on both sides.

  1. Expand the group. Start with one Ca(OH)2. It supplies one Ca, two O and two H. Track O and H separately because no product contains the OH group.
  2. Follow Ca, then Cl, then N. One Ca needs one CaCl2. Its two Cl atoms require two NH4Cl. Those bring two N atoms, so make two NH3.
  3. Find what H remains for water. The left supplies 2 + (2 × 4) = 10 H atoms. The two NH3 use 2 × 3 = 6. That leaves 10 − 6 = 4 H atoms, which fill two H2O.
  4. Check O against the same water count. Two H2O need two O atoms. Both came from Ca(OH)2. Changing just the water now would change both O and H.

Ca(OH)2(s) + 2 NH4Cl(s) → CaCl2(s) + 2 NH3(g) + 2 H2O(g)

Compare the two moves

Adding one NO adds one N and one O on the left. Left is now N 2, O 4. Right is still N 1, O 2. The left now has enough O to make two copies of NO2. Adding one NO2 adds one N and two O on the right, giving N 2, O 4 there too.

2 NO(g) + O2(g) → 2 NO2(g)

Each element matches. The written charge is zero on each side. The coefficients 2, 1, 2 have no common divisor greater than 1. The same N and O atoms are accounted for in the products, so the equation represents a rearrangement that conserves mass.

A. Burning ethane

One ethane calls for two CO2 and three H2O. Those products need 4 + 3 = 7 O atoms. Oxygen arrives in pairs, so double the amounts on both sides to work with whole O2 molecules. If you doubled only the oxygen, revisit C and H.

2 C2H6(g) + 7 O2(g) → 4 CO2(g) + 6 H2O(g)

Both sides have C 4, H 12 and O 14. Written charge is zero on each side. The four coefficients have no common factor greater than 1.

Return to the ethane equation

B. Heating nickel(II) nitrate

One Ni(NO3)2 has two N and six O. The nitrate group splits. One NiO and two NO2 account for all the Ni and N, and use 1 + 4 = 5 O. That leaves one O atom, but O2 comes in pairs. Start with two nickel nitrate formula units and double the NiO and NO2 counts as well. The two remaining O atoms fill one O2.

2 Ni(NO3)2(s) → 2 NiO(s) + 4 NO2(g) + O2(g)

Both sides have Ni 2, N 4 and O 12. Written charge is zero on each side. The O2 coefficient of 1 makes the ratio simplest.

Glossary

Subscript
A small lower number showing how many atoms or groups are in one copy of a formula.
Coefficient
The number before a formula. It multiplies the entire formula and expresses a ratio in the equation.
Reactants
The substances consumed in the reaction, written to the left of the arrow.
Products
The substances formed in the reaction, written to the right of the arrow.
State label
A label after a formula: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, and (aq) dissolved in water. The label supplies physical information and adds no atoms to the count.
Formula unit
The smallest whole-number ratio represented by the formula of an ionic compound. Counting formula units uses that ratio and does not require separate molecules in the solid.
