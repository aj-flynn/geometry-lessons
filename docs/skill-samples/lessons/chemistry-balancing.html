Balance a chemical equation

Count the atoms. Choose a number to change. Check what else moves with it.

Which formula shows two oxygen atoms in one molecule, CO or CO2? Say how you know, then read the next line.

The subscript, a small number after a symbol, makes CO2 a group of one C and two O atoms. A symbol without a subscript counts once. See the glossary for other notation.

Follow the atoms

The arrow runs from reactants, the starting substances, to products, the substances made. Here all three substances are gases, shown by (g).

CO(g) + O2(g) → CO2(g)

Read the blank spaces in front as ones. With one molecule of each, the left has three O atoms and the right shows two. Which picture would account for the extra O?

Change a coefficient, the number of copies of the whole formula. A reaction can regroup the original atoms. Changing CO2 to CO3 would change the substance you were given.

Predict, then change one coefficient

Predict the right-side C and O counts if CO2 goes from one copy to two. Which element would then need more on the left?

1 CO(g) + 1 O2(g) → 1 CO2(g)

Compare your prediction

Trial 1 CO(g) + 1 O2(g) → 2 CO2(g) shows 2 C and 4 O atoms on the right. The left still shows 1 C and 3 O atoms. Try changing just the left CO count next.

  1. Trial CO(g) + O2(g) → 2 CO2(g). Doubling the right side gives 2 C and 4 O atoms there.
  2. Double CO on the left to bring C to 2 there too. The left now has 2 O from CO plus 2 O from O2, or 4 O atoms. See the complete line below.
  3. Final 2 CO(g) + O2(g) → 2 CO2(g). The C and O rows match. Each written substance is neutral, so the total charge is zero on each side. The coefficient of 1 means there is no common whole-number divisor greater than one.

In your own words, compare the one-copy picture with the final inventory. Which could describe where all the oxygen atoms went, and what would be missing from the other picture?

Check a different trial

NH3(g) → N2(g) + H2(g)

What is the total number of H atoms on the left after changing NH₃ to two copies?

Compare your count

Two NH3 molecules contain 2 × 3 = 6 H atoms and 2 × 1 = 2 N atoms. Two counts the copies. Three counts H in one copy.

Retry with four NH3 molecules. Count H and N separately.

Compare the retry

4 × 3 = 12 H atoms and 4 × 1 = 4 N atoms.

Pick a starting point

Start with a symbol that appears in just one formula on each side. If the same group stays together, you can count the group first. Save a symbol shared by several formulas for a later pass.

Al(OH)3(s) + H2SO4(aq) → Al2(SO4)3(aq) + H2O(l)

In Al(OH)3, the 3 applies to both O and H. For a solid such as this, the formula counts a formula unit, the composition ratio, rather than a separate molecule. Here (s) is solid, (l) is liquid and (aq) means dissolved in water. Read more about formula and state notation.

  1. Trial 2 Al(OH)3(s) + H2SO4(aq) → Al2(SO4)3(aq) + H2O(l). Start with Al. Two left units match the two Al atoms in one product unit.
  2. Trial 2 Al(OH)3(s) + 3 H2SO4(aq) → Al2(SO4)3(aq) + H2O(l). Three acid copies match the three sulfate groups in one product unit.
  3. There are now 6 H atoms from the solid and 6 from the acid. Put 6 before H2O(l) to match the total of 12 H atoms.
  4. Final 2 Al(OH)3(s) + 3 H2SO4(aq) → Al2(SO4)3(aq) + 6 H2O(l). Check all elements below, even those you handled as a group.

The written formulas are neutral on both sides. The ratio 2, 3, 1, 6 is already in smallest whole numbers. The equation tracks the overall changes. Dissolved substances can contain ions, charged particles, even when written as neutral formulas here.

When a group splits, count its elements separately. If the last element needs an even total but the other trials give an odd count, try doubling all the coefficients you already settled. Then match the last element and recheck every row.

Test the shortcut

ZnS(s) + O2(g) → ZnO(s) + SO2(g)

Suppose the trial uses one copy of each solid and one of SO2. Zinc and sulfur match. Explain why an O2 coefficient alone cannot finish this trial in whole numbers.

Compare your explanation

The right shows 1 + 2 = 3 O atoms. The left adds O in pairs. Retry with two copies of each solid and two of SO2. Find the new coefficient for O2.

Compare the retry

With 2 ZnS(s) + 3 O2(g) → 2 ZnO(s) + 2 SO2(g), each side has 2 Zn, 2 S, 6 O and zero net charge. The coefficients have no common factor above one.

Use two counts together

In hot potassium hydroxide solution, chlorine ends up in two products. No single product carries all the Cl, so a Cl count alone leaves too many numbers open.

Cl2(g) + KOH(aq) → KCl(aq) + KClO3(aq) + H2O(l)

  1. Try one KClO3(aq). It needs 3 O atoms. More O must go to H2O(l), so the 3 alone cannot tell you how many KOH copies to use.
  2. Every KOH supplies one H and one O. Each water uses two of those H atoms and one of their O atoms. So every two KOH copies contribute one O to water and leave one O for KClO3. Getting 3 O into KClO3 takes 6 KOH and gives 3 H2O.
  3. Now account for the 6 K atoms. One is already in KClO3, so the other 5 go to KCl. Those two products together need 6 Cl atoms, supplied by 3 Cl2.
  4. Final 3 Cl2(g) + 6 KOH(aq) → 5 KCl(aq) + KClO3(aq) + 3 H2O(l). Each side has 6 Cl, 6 K, 6 O and 6 H atoms, with zero net charge. The coefficient of 1 rules out further reduction.

Test a grouped formula

Ca(NO3)2(s) → CaO(s) + NO2(g) + O2(g)

Try one copy of the starting solid. How many copies of NO2 match its N count? Explain what happens to O when you also match Ca.

Compare your reasoning

One starting unit has 2 N and 6 O atoms. One CaO and two NO2 match Ca and N, but account for only 5 O atoms. The leftover O cannot make a whole O2 molecule. Double the trial and recount.

Compare the retry

2 Ca(NO3)2(s) → 2 CaO(s) + 4 NO2(g) + O2(g) gives 2 Ca, 4 N, 12 O and zero net charge on each side. The 1 in front of oxygen means the ratio is as small as possible.

Choose your own first move

P4(s) + NaOH(aq) + H2O(l) → PH3(g) + NaH2PO2(aq)

Balance this overall equation for white phosphorus and aqueous sodium hydroxide. Write the full equation on paper with the smallest whole-number coefficients. Explain your first move and show how you settled the most difficult coefficient.

Get a hint after trying

Look for two totals that use some of the same formulas. Write the contribution of each formula before choosing trial numbers.

Compare your work

Match Na by giving NaOH and NaH2PO2 the same coefficient. Each salt unit needs two O atoms, one from its matching NaOH and one from water, so give water that coefficient too. Together the NaOH and water supply 3 H per salt unit. The salt needs 2, leaving 1 H for PH3. Each PH3 needs three such H atoms, so try 3 of each of those matched formulas and 1 PH3.

P4(s) + 3 NaOH(aq) + 3 H2O(l) → PH3(g) + 3 NaH2PO2(aq)

The trial needs 1 + 3 = 4 P atoms, supplied by one P4. Verify 4 P, 3 Na, 9 H and 6 O atoms on each side. The charge shown is zero on both sides and the coefficients have no common factor above one.

Retry from the other end. Give P4 a trial coefficient of 2. Work out the product amounts, then explain whether you would keep the final list.

Compare the retry

Eight P atoms divide between two PH3 molecules and six salt units. That needs six NaOH and six H2O. Every coefficient is even, so divide the full list by 2 before keeping it.

Recall it later

Before reopening the lesson, sketch two empty inventory columns and label them. Explain when you would count a group as a unit and what would make you switch to counting its elements.

Glossary

Coefficient
Number in front of a formula that multiplies all its atom counts. A missing coefficient means 1.
Subscript
Small number that counts the preceding element or parenthesized group in one formula.
Reactant
A starting substance, written before the arrow.
Product
A substance made in the reaction, written after the arrow.
State symbols
(s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water.
Formula unit
A counting unit for a compound written as a ratio, such as an ionic solid. It represents the stated proportions rather than a separate particle.
