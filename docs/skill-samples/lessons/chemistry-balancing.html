01 / KEEP AN INVENTORY

What can the tiles make?

Imagine opening some tile packets and regrouping their contents. A packet is one complete set of tiles. There are two packets with one square and one dot apiece, plus one packet with two dots.

Warm-up · count the dots in the starting packets

Predict first. Two mixed packets give one dot each. The last packet gives two dots. That makes four dots altogether, along with two squares.

Predict. The new packets must each hold one square and two dots. How many complete new packets use exactly the starting tiles? What would be left over if you stopped after one packet?

Can a head count catch the error?

Another set starts with three squares and one dot. A draft picture ends with two squares and two dots. The draft uses four tiles.

What would you check before approving the draft?

Compare your choice

The grand total stays at four while the square count drops and the dot count rises. Compare each shape separately. Packet counts can change when tiles are regrouped.

Retry with a different inventory

Three squares and two dots are repacked into two square–dot packets plus one loose tile. Predict which loose tile completes the inventory.

Compare the retry

The two packets use two of each shape. The loose tile has to be a square to account for the third square.

02 / READ THE FORMULA

Count inside, then multiply

In chemistry, each element, or kind of atom, has a symbol. In Ca(OH)2, Ca is calcium, O is oxygen and H is hydrogen. A symbol starts with a capital letter and can have a lowercase letter, as in Ca.

A formula gives the contents of one molecule or, for solids such as calcium hydroxide, a simplest ratio called a formula unit. The solid is an extended array of ions, which are particles with electric charge. Use one Ca(OH)2 formula unit as a counting unit.

3 Ca(OH)2
Outside the formula
The coefficient 3 copies the entire unit three times.
Inside the formula
The subscript 2 copies the whole group in parentheses. Each unit has one Ca, two O and two H.
The counts
Multiply each count by 3 to get 3 Ca, 6 O and 6 H atoms.

An unwritten coefficient or subscript means 1. To change how many copies you have, change the front number. Editing a subscript changes the formula itself, like changing what goes in a tile packet.

Read a new label

Al means aluminum. How many oxygen atoms are represented by 4 Al(OH)3?

Hint · unpack one unit first

Write out the group inside the parentheses as many times as its subscript says. Then account for the four full units.

Compare your count

Each Al(OH)3 unit contains three O atoms. Four units contain 4 × 3 = 12 O atoms. A count of three leaves out three units. A count of four gives units, so it answers a different question.

Retry in reverse

Several units of Mg(OH)2 contain four Mg atoms and eight O atoms. Mg means magnesium. Write the formula with a coefficient for this batch. Predict the number of H atoms.

Compare the retry

One Mg per unit gives 4 Mg(OH)2. Both O and H occur twice per unit, so this batch also has eight H atoms.

03 / REPAIR A REACTION

Start where a change is easy to track

A chemical equation lists reactants, the starting substances, to the left of the arrow and products, the substances formed, to the right. The arrow means “forms” and plus separates substances on one side. The formulas below are given. Your job is to decide how many copies of each belong in one batch.

Ca(OH)2 + HBr → CaBr2 + H2O

Calcium hydroxide reacts with hydrobromic acid to form calcium bromide and water. Br means bromine. One calcium bromide unit needs two Br atoms, while one unit of acid provides only one. If the draft used one of each formula, the second Br atom would have no source.

  1. Anchor on a simple row. Br occurs in one formula on each side, so you can see what changing the HBr coefficient will do. Use 2 HBr to supply the two Br atoms.
  2. Follow the changes. With that edit, the left has 2 O and 4 H. The only product carrying either of those elements is water. Two water molecules use both O atoms and all four H atoms.
  3. Recount every row. A coefficient affects every element in its formula. A row that matched earlier can change while you fix another row.

Ca(OH)2 + 2 HBr → CaBr2 + 2 H2O

The front numbers describe a ratio, the amounts relative to one another. Two identical batches would double every coefficient and keep the same ratio. Use the smallest positive whole-number set. If every coefficient shares a divisor greater than 1, divide all of them by it. A coefficient ratio counts formula units or molecules. It does not give a ratio in grams or say how fast the reaction happens.

Some formulas include a state label. (s) means solid, (l) means liquid, (g) means gas and (aq) means dissolved in water. These labels describe the substance and add zero atoms. The examples here use neutral formulas and leave the state labels out so you can focus on the atom counts.

A row can have two sources

A model of carbon monoxide reacting with oxygen shows 4 CO + 2 O2 → 4 CO2. C means carbon. Imagine you need the smallest whole-number set for the same reaction.

Hint · check before shrinking

First audit oxygen on both sides. Include oxygen from both left-hand formulas. Then look for a common divisor across all three coefficients.

Which check and next step fit this draft?

Compare your reasoning

Oxygen has two sources on the left, 4 from CO and 4 from O2. Carbon and oxygen both match across the arrow. Halve every coefficient to get 2 CO + O2 → 2 CO2. Halving just the product would change both right-hand counts and break both matches.

Retry · spot a change that will fail

Start with 2 CO + O2 → 2 CO2. Someone wants three copies of CO and writes 3 CO + O2 → 3 CO2. Which row catches the problem?

Compare the retry

Carbon still matches at 3. Oxygen is 3 + 2 = 5 on the left and 3 × 2 = 6 on the right. There is no whole number of oxygen molecules that can supply the three O atoms needed to fill the gap left by the three CO molecules. A whole-number batch must use an even number of CO molecules here.

04 / BUILD A WHOLE BATCH

What if oxygen comes in pairs?

In the complete combustion, or burning, of propene, the fuel reacts with oxygen to make carbon dioxide and water. Build the smallest positive whole-number set. Keep the given formulas fixed.

C3H6 + O2 → CO2 + H2O

Predict. With one propene molecule, start with C and H. How many O atoms would the products require? Can the oxygen molecules supply exactly that many?

Hint · choose where to start

C appears in one formula on each side. So does H. Oxygen appears in two products, and the only oxygen source delivers it in pairs. That makes oxygen a good row to check after C and H.

Hint · when a pair leaves an odd gap

Write out the required oxygen count with one fuel molecule. If it is odd, try two fuel molecules and keep the carbon and hydrogen proportions. Now revisit oxygen.

Compare a worked ledger

One propene molecule supplies 3 C and 6 H, calling for 3 CO2 and 3 H2O. Those products use 6 + 3 = 9 O atoms. Whole O2 molecules bring an even number of O atoms.

Start with two fuel molecules. That makes 6 CO2 and 6 H2O. The products now use 12 + 6 = 18 O atoms, supplied by 9 O2 molecules.

2 C3H6 + 9 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O

Final check. both sides have 6 C, 12 H and 18 O. The coefficients 2, 9, 6 and 6 have no common divisor greater than 1.

Retry · take apart a mineral

On heating, malachite can break down into copper(II) oxide, water and carbon dioxide. Its formula is Cu2(OH)2CO3. Cu means copper. Start with one formula unit and the three product formulas CuO, H2O and CO2. Which element would you leave for the final check, and why? Then check that row.

Compare the retry

O shows up in all three products, so start with Cu, H or C. One starting unit has 2 Cu, 2 H and 1 C, so use 2 CuO, 1 H2O and 1 CO2. The starting unit has 2 + 3 = 5 O atoms. The products contain 2 + 1 + 2 = 5 O atoms.

The ledger assumes the listed substances are the ones that react and form. An atom count alone cannot tell you whether the reaction will happen, which products to expect under new conditions, or how much fuel a real burner actually uses.

05 / EXPLAIN YOUR CHECK

Take the ledger with you

Exit explanation. A lab group copies the one-fuel propene draft with three molecules of each product, then writes 4 O2. What would their record require the equipment to provide that the starting molecules did not? Explain using a single ledger row. Then explain why changing the formula of oxygen would describe a different starting substance.

Compare the exit explanation

The O row has 8 atoms on the left and 9 on the right. The draft assumes one extra O atom is available. Writing a different subscript changes how many O atoms are in each particle, so it changes what the gas formula describes. Scale the fuel and both products so the oxygen demand can be met in pairs.

At your next equation. Keep the formulas visible. List the elements once down the left side of your ledger. Start with a row you can track easily, follow the effect of each change, and explain what a mismatch would mean in a batch of the actual substances. Before you stop, audit all rows and see whether the entire set can be reduced.

Later recall. Before opening this lesson again, sketch two boxes showing what a front number changes and what a small number changes. Then ask yourself how many O atoms are in 3 Fe(OH)3, where Fe means iron.

Compare after you recall

The front number repeats a whole formula. The subscript changes its contents. Three iron(III) hydroxide formula units have three O atoms each, so there are nine O atoms in total.

Glossary

Element and symbol
A kind of atom and its shorthand. O stands for oxygen.
Molecule
A group of atoms held together by chemical bonds.
Ion and formula unit
An ion is a particle with electric charge. A formula unit gives the simplest whole-number ratio in an ionic substance.
Subscript and parentheses
A small lower number counts the symbol just before it, or the entire group just before it if the group is in parentheses.
Coefficient and ratio
A coefficient is the number in front of a formula. The coefficients together give a ratio, the amounts relative to one another.
Reactants and products
Starting substances are on the left of →. Substances formed are on the right.
Atom ledger
A table that records counts of atoms, with one row per element and a column for each side of the arrow.
State labels
(s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas and (aq) dissolved in water.
