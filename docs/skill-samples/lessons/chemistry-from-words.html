Introductory chemistry · Reactions

From reaction words to equations

Turn a description into a balanced molecular equation, with a whole formula for each substance. Use the words to fix the substances and their states. Then count atoms to find the numbers in front.

First, read the numbers

In 2NH3(g), how many N atoms and H atoms are represented? What does (g) tell you? A subscript is a small number inside a formula that counts atoms or groups in one unit. An element with no subscript counts as one per unit. Use the state symbols below if you need them.

Use a notebook for your counts. The short checks have their own comparisons at the end.

Compare the starting check

There are 2 N atoms and 6 H atoms. The coefficient, 2, counts whole molecules. The subscript, 3, counts H atoms in each molecule. With no subscript, there is one N in each molecule. The state symbol (g) means gas.

See how the numbers work in an equation or look up a term.

Read the sentence in two passes

  1. Mark the reactants, the starting substances, and the products, the substances formed. Words such as “yields” and “forms” mark the change. Use + between substances on the same side and → toward the products.
  2. Write each formula and state. The skeleton equation is this first draft with no balancing numbers added. Freeze the formulas. To balance the equation, change only the coefficients (the numbers in front) until the two sides have equal counts of every element. Reduce to the smallest positive whole-number ratio and leave any 1 unwritten.

State symbols: (s) solid · (l) liquid · (g) gas or vapor · (aq) aqueous, meaning dissolved in water. A liquid such as pure water uses (l). A substance dissolved in that water uses (aq).

In a molecular equation, keep every substance as a whole formula, including dissolved salts. For a salt, count by formula unit, the smallest whole-number ratio of its ions. The unit is a counting convention: the ions in a solid form a larger repeating structure, and the label (aq) tells you the salt is in solution, where its ions can be separated.

1. Fix the substances before balancing

What happens when silver oxide is heated

On heating, solid silver(I) oxide breaks down, leaving solid silver and giving off diatomic oxygen gas.

For this example, use Ag+ for the silver ion and O2− for the oxide ion. Diatomic means two atoms in one molecule.

Ion charges from the UW–Madison ion chart.

“Breaks down” puts the single starting substance on the left. Heating describes a condition. If shown in the equation, the symbol Δ goes over the arrow. Heat adds no atoms.

Two silver ions at +1 each balance the −2 of one oxide ion, so the solid’s neutral formula is Ag2O. The silver metal uses Ag, while diatomic oxygen gas uses O2.

Ag2O(s) → Ag(s) + O2(g)

Oxygen leaves in pairs. One Ag2O unit supplies just one O atom. A second whole unit supplies the second O atom and brings two more Ag atoms with it.

Put 2 before Ag2O, then 4 before Ag. Keep one O2 on the right.

2Ag2O(s) → 4Ag(s) + O2(g)

Both sides have 4 Ag atoms and 2 O atoms. The written formulas are neutral and the coefficients 2, 4, 1 have no common divisor larger than 1.

3. When one gas supplies oxygen to two products

A step in making nitric acid

Ammonia gas, NH3, reacts with diatomic oxygen gas over a hot catalyst. The products in this step are nitrogen monoxide gas and water vapor.

A catalyst speeds a reaction without being used up overall. The catalyst is a condition here. In molecular names, mono- means one and di- means two. Nitrogen monoxide has one N and one O.

Write the fixed skeleton. Both products get (g), including water because it is described as vapor.

NH3(g) + O2(g) → NO(g) + H2O(g)

  1. Match H first. NH3 supplies H in threes and H2O uses H in twos. A shared total of 6 H requires 2NH3 and 3H2O.
  2. Match N. The 2 N on the left require 2NO on the right.
  3. Count both sources of O on the right. The two NO molecules use 2 O and the three H2O molecules use 3 O. Five O atoms would require 5/2 O2 molecules in the trial ratio. To clear the half, double all coefficients.

4NH3(g) + 5O2(g) → 4NO(g) + 6H2O(g)

Check N: 4 = 4. H: 12 = 12. O: 10 = 4 + 6. The written charge is zero on both sides, and 4, 5, 4, 6 cannot all be divided by a common factor larger than 1.

4. Build neutral salt formulas before counting

A solid appears when two solutions mix

Mixing aqueous lead(II) nitrate and aqueous potassium iodide makes solid lead(II) iodide and aqueous potassium nitrate.

The solid made from a solution is a precipitate.

Ion charges from the UW–Madison ion chart.

Find lead(II) and nitrate in the lookup. One Pb2+ needs two NO3− ions for a neutral unit.

Charge: +2 + 2 × (−1) = 0
Formula: Pb(NO3)2
Inside the two groups: 2 × 1 = 2 N; 2 × 3 = 6 O.

The outer 2 multiplies everything inside the parentheses.

Use the same charge test to get KI, PbI2 and KNO3. Now each written compound is neutral. A coefficient changes the number of formula units while keeping each unit neutral.

Pb(NO3)2(aq) + KI(aq) → PbI2(s) + KNO3(aq)

One PbI2 needs two I atoms. Put 2 before KI, then 2 before KNO3 to match the two K atoms. This also carries both nitrate groups across.

Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI2(s) + 2KNO3(aq)

Each side has Pb 1, I 2, K 2, N 2 and O 6. Net written charge is zero. A coefficient of 1 already makes this the simplest ratio.

5. Count hydrogen in both products

Hydrogen from aluminum and liquid water

In a process that exposes fresh solid aluminum to liquid water, the products under the conditions described here are solid aluminum hydroxide and hydrogen gas.

Use Al3+ and hydroxide, OH−, for the salt. Elemental hydrogen gas is diatomic.

Ion charges from the UW–Madison ion chart.

One Al3+ needs three OH− ions. Write Al(OH)3. The subscript 3 applies to the whole group, giving 3 O and 3 H per formula unit.

Al(s) + H2O(l) → Al(OH)3(s) + H2(g)

  1. Try one Al(OH)3 unit. It needs 1 Al and 3 O, so start with one Al and three H2O.
  2. The three water molecules supply 6 H. The solid already accounts for 3 H. That leaves 3 H for the gas, which holds H in pairs.
  3. Double the trial. Two units of the solid account for 6 of the 12 H. The remaining 6 H make three H2 molecules.

2Al(s) + 6H2O(l) → 2Al(OH)3(s) + 3H2(g)

Check Al: 2 = 2. O: 6 = 6. H: 12 = 6 in the solid + 6 in the gas. Charge is zero on both sides, and the ratio 2, 6, 2, 3 is in lowest terms.

Glossary

Reactant
A starting substance. Write it to the left of the arrow.
Product
A substance formed. Write it to the right of the arrow.
Molecular equation
An equation written with each substance as a whole chemical formula, even for ionic compounds in solution.
Skeleton equation
A first draft showing the correct formulas on their correct sides, before balancing.
State symbol
A parenthetical label after a formula. Use (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas and (aq) for aqueous.
Aqueous
Dissolved in water.
Subscript
A small number in a formula. It counts the atoms or groups in one molecule or formula unit.
Coefficient
A number in front of a formula. It multiplies every atom in that formula. When no coefficient is written, the value is 1.
Formula unit
The smallest whole-number ratio of ions in an ionic compound.
Balance
Adjust coefficients so both sides account for the same number of atoms of each element. A complete equation must also conserve total charge.
Diatomic
Made of two atoms per molecule. Elemental oxygen gas and hydrogen gas use O2 and H2.
Precipitate
A solid that forms from dissolved substances in a solution.
Catalyst
A substance that speeds a reaction without being consumed overall.
