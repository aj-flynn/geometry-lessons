From reaction words to a balanced equation

Translate the description into formulas and state symbols. Then use atom counts to find the smallest whole-number ratio.

Glossary of terms and symbols

Read the description before you balance

  1. Put the reactants (starting substances) left of the arrow and the products (substances formed) on the right. Use a plus sign between substances on the same side. In “Y is produced when X reacts,” X goes on the left.
  2. Write each substance’s formula and state. Copy a formula when it is given. Look up an element’s symbol for a solid metal, use the given molecular formula or its name for a molecular substance, and use ion charges to write a neutral formula for an ionic compound.
  3. Keep those formulas fixed. Balance by changing only coefficients, the numbers in front. A subscript, the small number after a symbol or group, tells you what one molecule or one ionic formula unit contains. A missing coefficient or subscript means 1.
  4. Check the number of atoms of every element on both sides. Divide all coefficients by any shared factor greater than 1. Omit coefficients of 1 in the final equation.

Use (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas or vapor, and (aq) for a substance dissolved in water. A molecular equation writes each compound as a whole formula, even for dissolved ionic compounds. An ionic formula unit gives an ion ratio. It represents neither a separate molecule in a crystal nor a cluster that stays together in water.

A balanced equation conserves atoms and total charge. The substances here all have neutral formulas, so the total charge is zero on each side. Balancing uses the products given in the description and cannot establish which products will form.

One coefficient changes every count in its formula

Gaseous nitrogen monoxide and diatomic oxygen gas form gaseous nitrogen dioxide. A diatomic molecule has two atoms. The names give NO, O2, and NO2, each labeled (g). Start with one of each. Predict what happens to both product counts if you double only nitrogen dioxide. Which reactant would you adjust next?

Final 2NO(g) + O2(g) → 2NO2(g). The reactant O2 has coefficient 1, so the coefficients have no shared factor greater than 1.

A draft has 3N₂O(g). How many atoms does that term contribute?

Compare your choice

Three molecules give six nitrogen atoms and three oxygen atoms. If you missed a multiplier, trace both element counts in each molecule and try the question again.

When is the first draft already balanced?

In a decomposition, one reactant forms two or more products. Compare two heated solids. The first equation needs no adjustment. The second has an oxygen-pair constraint.

Copper(II) hydroxide gives copper(II) oxide and water vapor

Given Heated solid copper(II) hydroxide forms solid copper(II) oxide and water vapor. Goal Write its balanced molecular equation.

  1. Read “copper(II)” as the copper ion with charge 2+. The symbol for copper is Cu. Hydroxide is OH−, so one Cu2+ needs two OH− groups. The charges add to +2 − 2 = 0. Write Cu(OH)2 with parentheses around the repeated group.
  2. Oxide is O2−. One oxide ion balances one Cu2+, giving CuO. Use H2O for water and (g) for vapor.
  3. Draft Cu(OH)2(s) → CuO(s) + H2O(g). The left has Cu 1, O 2, H 2. The right has Cu 1, O 1 + 1 = 2, H 2. All counts match at the smallest possible coefficients.

Silver(I) oxide gives silver and oxygen gas

Given Heated solid silver(I) oxide forms solid silver and diatomic oxygen gas. Goal Write its balanced molecular equation.

  1. Silver(I) is Ag+. Two Ag+ and one O2− give neutral Ag2O. Use Ag for silver metal and O2 for the gas.
  2. Draft Ag2O(s) → Ag(s) + O2(g). The draft supplies one oxygen atom on the left per formula unit and two on the right per molecule. Two formula units supply the two atoms needed for one oxygen molecule.
  3. Two formula units supply four silver atoms. Use a 4 in front of Ag. The final equation is 2Ag2O(s) → 4Ag(s) + O2(g).
  4. Check Ag 4 and O 2 on each side. One coefficient is 1, so the ratio is already simplest.

Heat can be shown with Δ over the reaction arrow. It is a reaction condition. The equation includes only the substances when you count atoms.

Liquid hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂, decomposes into liquid water and diatomic oxygen gas. Which edit balances the draft and keeps those substances?

Compare your choice

Two H₂O₂ and two H₂O balance hydrogen at four atoms per side. The two water molecules account for two of the four O atoms on the left. One O₂ accounts for the remaining two. If you edited the formula or balanced only one element, restore the formulas and retry both rows.

Find how much oxygen the pairs must supply

When a fuel burns completely, the description may give carbon dioxide and water as products. In these examples, carbon appears in just one product and hydrogen in just one product. Their counts fix the two product coefficients for one fuel molecule. Oxygen appears in both products and in the fuel, so count it last.

One ethanol molecule needs a whole number of oxygen molecules

Given Gaseous ethanol, C2H6O, burns in diatomic oxygen gas to produce carbon dioxide gas and water vapor. Goal Write the balanced molecular equation.

  1. Copy the fuel formula. Use O2 for diatomic oxygen, CO2 for carbon dioxide, and H2O for water. All four terms have (g).
  2. For one fuel molecule, put 2 in front of CO2 for the two carbon atoms and 3 in front of H2O for the six hydrogen atoms.
  3. The products contain 2 × 2 + 3 × 1 = 7 oxygen atoms. The fuel supplies one. O2 must supply 7 − 1 = 6 atoms, or 3 molecules.
  4. Final equation C2H6O(g) + 3O2(g) → 2CO2(g) + 3H2O(g). Both sides have C 2, H 6, and O 7. The coefficient of the fuel is 1.

One propan-2-ol molecule leaves an odd oxygen demand

Given In a complete burn, gaseous propan-2-ol, C3H8O, reacts with diatomic oxygen gas to form gaseous carbon dioxide and water vapor. Goal Write the balanced molecular equation.

  1. Write the same product formulas and (g) labels as before. Per fuel molecule, use 3CO2 for the three carbon atoms and 4H2O for the eight hydrogen atoms.
  2. The products contain 3 × 2 + 4 × 1 = 10 oxygen atoms. The fuel supplies one. The remaining 10 − 1 = 9 atoms require a temporary ratio of 9/2 O2 per fuel molecule.
  3. Double every coefficient so all are whole numbers. Final equation 2C3H8O(g) + 9O2(g) → 6CO2(g) + 8H2O(g).
  4. For C, 2 × 3 = 6 on the left and 6 on the right. For H, 2 × 8 = 16 on the left and 8 × 2 = 16 on the right. For O, 2 + 9 × 2 = 20 on the left and 6 × 2 + 8 = 20 on the right. No number greater than 1 divides all four coefficients.
After accounting for oxygen in a fuel, its products need 11 more oxygen atoms per fuel molecule. What preserves the carbon and hydrogen counts?

Compare your choice

The temporary oxygen coefficient is 11/2. Multiply the whole ratio by two. If you rounded or scaled only the reactants, redo the carbon, hydrogen and oxygen counts before retrying.

Keep whole formulas when an aqueous mixture forms a solid

A precipitate is a solid that forms in a solution. The description tells you which product is solid and which remains dissolved. In a molecular equation, write each dissolved compound with its full neutral formula and (aq).

Given Mixing aqueous barium nitrate and aqueous potassium sulfate produces solid barium sulfate and aqueous potassium nitrate. Goal Write the balanced molecular equation.

  1. Look up the ion symbols and charges. Barium is Ba2+, potassium is K+, nitrate is NO3−, and sulfate is SO42−. Nitrate and sulfate are polyatomic ions, charged groups made of several atoms.
  2. One Ba2+ needs two NO3− groups, so +2 + 2(−1) = 0. Write Ba(NO3)2. One SO42− needs two K+, so 2(+1) − 2 = 0. Write K2SO4. The products pair +2 with −2 in BaSO4 and +1 with −1 in KNO3.
  3. Draft Ba(NO3)2(aq) + K2SO4(aq) → BaSO4(s) + KNO3(aq). Sulfate matches, but potassium and nitrate do not. Since nitrate stays NO3 in the formulas on both sides, count those groups together. Two nitrate groups and two potassium ions on the left require 2KNO3 on the right.
  4. Final equation Ba(NO3)2(aq) + K2SO4(aq) → BaSO4(s) + 2KNO3(aq). Expand the groups to check Ba 1, K 2, N 2, S 1, and O 10 on each side. All formulas are neutral. The coefficients include 1.

Count an intact group as a unit only when its composition is unchanged across the equation. In the copper hydroxide example, oxide and water carry the oxygen and hydrogen separately, so count those elements individually.

Zinc nitrate contains Zn²⁺ and NO₃⁻. What does one neutral formula unit contribute?

Compare your choice

One Zn²⁺ needs two nitrate groups. The outer 2 in Zn(NO₃)₂ multiplies the nitrogen and the oxygen. If you missed either count, rewrite the two nitrate groups separately and retry.

Count every place hydrogen ends up

Compare the role of water in two reactions. In the first, every hydrogen atom ends up in the gas. In the second, hydrogen also stays in a solid product.

With magnesium and steam, hydrogen goes to one product

Given Solid magnesium reacts with steam to produce solid magnesium oxide and hydrogen gas. Goal Write the balanced molecular equation.

  1. Use the element symbol Mg for magnesium metal. Steam is H2O(g). One Mg2+ and one O2− make neutral MgO. Hydrogen gas is diatomic H2(g).
  2. Draft Mg(s) + H2O(g) → MgO(s) + H2(g). One water molecule provides one oxygen for the oxide and two hydrogen atoms for the gas. Both sides have Mg 1, O 1 and H 2. All coefficients are 1.

With calcium hydride, hydrogen goes to two products

Given In a water reaction with solid calcium hydride, liquid water is consumed, solid calcium hydroxide forms, and hydrogen gas is released. Use the stated solid product. Goal Write the balanced molecular equation.

  1. Look up Ca2+ and hydride H−. Two hydride ions balance one calcium ion, giving CaH2. Two hydroxide groups, OH−, also balance one calcium ion, giving Ca(OH)2. Liquid water has (l), and H2 gas has (g).
  2. Draft CaH2(s) + H2O(l) → Ca(OH)2(s) + H2(g). Start with one unit of the hydroxide. Its two O atoms require two water molecules.
  3. Count hydrogen after setting the water coefficient. The hydride provides two H atoms and water provides four, for six on the left. The solid product accounts for two. The remaining four H atoms give two H2 molecules.
  4. Final equation CaH2(s) + 2H2O(l) → Ca(OH)2(s) + 2H2(g). Check Ca 1, O 2 and H 6 on both sides. The coefficients include 1.
A set of reactants supplies eight H atoms. The solid product accounts for two. How many hydrogen gas molecules fit the rest?

Compare your choice

Eight H atoms minus two in the solid leaves six. Six atoms make three H₂ molecules. If you used all eight or counted six gas molecules, draw the six leftover atoms in pairs and retry.

Write complete equations from descriptions

Use paper for each attempt. Write a molecular equation with formulas, state labels and the smallest whole-number coefficients. Choose an element or intact group to balance first and explain why. Leave each comparison closed until you have a full draft and have checked every element.

Use this lookup strip as needed. Aluminum is Al3+, nitride is N3−, hydroxide is OH−, and ammonia is NH3. Strontium is Sr2+, potassium is K+, phosphate is PO43−, and nitrate is NO3−. Hydrobromic acid is HBr(aq), zinc forms Zn2+, and bromide is Br−.

1. A material reacts with water

In a simplified description of aluminum nitride reacting with liquid water, solid aluminum hydroxide forms and ammonia is released as a gas. Solid aluminum nitride is the other starting material.

Compare equation 1

Al3+ and N3− give AlN. One Al3+ and three OH− groups give Al(OH)3. Three water molecules supply the three oxygen atoms. Their six hydrogen atoms go into both products, three to the solid and three to the gas.

AlN(s) + 3H2O(l) → Al(OH)3(s) + NH3(g). Each side has Al 1, N 1, O 3, H 6. A coefficient of 1 makes the ratio simplest.

If you sent all six H atoms to ammonia, start again at the oxygen row, then subtract the three H in the solid before counting NH3.

2. Two solutions make a solid

Solid strontium phosphate forms when aqueous potassium phosphate and aqueous strontium nitrate are mixed. Potassium nitrate stays dissolved.

Compare equation 2

For strontium phosphate, +6 and −6 cancel using three strontium ions and two phosphate groups. Potassium phosphate needs three K+ per phosphate. Strontium nitrate needs two nitrates per Sr2+. Potassium nitrate needs one of each ion.

For one unit of Sr3(PO4)2, use two units of potassium phosphate and three of strontium nitrate. Those supply six potassium ions and six nitrate groups, so six units of potassium nitrate complete the equation.

2K3PO4(aq) + 3Sr(NO3)2(aq) → Sr3(PO4)2(s) + 6KNO3(aq). Each side has K 6, Sr 3, P 2, N 6, O 26. Every compound formula is neutral and the coefficients include 1.

If you split dissolved substances into ions, write each neutral formula first and use (aq). If you balanced only the solid, retry the dissolved product by counting both potassium and nitrate.

3. A vapor burns

Ethanal vapor, C2H4O, and diatomic oxygen gas react completely. The two products are carbon dioxide gas and steam.

Compare equation 3

One fuel molecule calls for two carbon dioxide and two water molecules. The products have 2 × 2 + 2 × 1 = 6 oxygen atoms. The fuel supplies one of the six, so oxygen gas supplies five. Use 5/2 O2 temporarily and multiply the whole ratio by two.

2C2H4O(g) + 5O2(g) → 4CO2(g) + 4H2O(g). Each side has C 4, H 8, and O 12. No number greater than 1 divides all four coefficients.

If you used three O2 per fuel molecule, count the oxygen already in the fuel and retry. If you used (l) for water, read the word “steam” again.

4. A heated solid releases a gas

When solid anhydrous copper(II) nitrate is heated, solid copper(II) oxide remains. Nitrogen dioxide gas and diatomic oxygen gas are released. Anhydrous means the crystal formula contains no water.

Compare equation 4

Two nitrates balance one Cu2+, so write Cu(NO3)2. Oxide is O2−, so write CuO. Per nitrate formula unit, one CuO uses the copper and two NO2 use the nitrogen. Those products account for five of the six oxygen atoms. The remaining atom calls for 1/2 O2, so double all coefficients.

2Cu(NO3)2(s) → 2CuO(s) + 4NO2(g) + O2(g). Each side has Cu 2, N 4, O 12. The coefficients include 1.

If you kept nitrate together, find nitrogen and oxygen in the product formulas. The nitrate group has changed, so try the copper and nitrogen rows first, then return to oxygen.

5. A solid metal reacts with an acid

Hydrogen gas forms as solid zinc reacts with aqueous hydrobromic acid. Zinc bromide remains dissolved.

Compare equation 5

Use Zn for the metal, HBr for the acid, and H2 for the gas. Neutral zinc bromide takes two Br− per Zn2+, giving ZnBr2. A coefficient of two on HBr supplies two bromine atoms for the salt and two hydrogen atoms for the gas. Keep the acid in molecular-equation notation.

Zn(s) + 2HBr(aq) → ZnBr2(aq) + H2(g). Each side has Zn 1, H 2, Br 2. The coefficients include 1.

If you wrote H as the gas, use the diatomic formula. If you wrote ZnBr, use the charges to build a neutral formula and then rebalance.

Glossary

Aqueous (aq)
Dissolved in water. (l) is the label for a liquid substance, (s) for a solid, and (g) for a gas or vapor.
Coefficient
Number in front of a formula that multiplies all of its atom counts. The final equation uses the smallest positive whole-number ratio.
Decomposition
A reaction in which one reactant forms more than one product.
Diatomic
Made of two atoms per molecule. A sample of diatomic elemental gas is counted in pairs.
Formula unit
The set of atom or ion counts represented by an ionic compound’s formula. An ionic solid is an extended array, with the formula describing the ion ratio.
Ion and polyatomic ion
An atom or bonded group with a net charge. A polyatomic ion contains multiple atoms, as in nitrate.
Molecular equation
An equation written with whole formulas for all substances, including dissolved ionic compounds.
Precipitate
A solid formed from a solution.
Reactants and products
Starting substances and substances formed. The arrow points from reactants to products.
Subscript
Small number in a formula that counts atoms of the preceding element or repeats a preceding parenthesized group.
Δ over the arrow
The reaction is carried out with heat.
