Introductory chemistry

From formula to name and back

A chemical name tells you what to count. Learn to read and write names using the charges of ions (charged particles) or the atom counts in a molecule (a distinct group of bonded atoms).

Read the small numbers

What does the 2 tell you in Br2? What does it tell you in Mg2+?

Compare the two positions

A subscript is a small lowered number. Here it counts two bromine atoms. The raised 2+ gives the charge of one magnesium ion. A symbol with no subscript counts as one.

01 / Read an ionic formula

Find the charge of one metal ion

An ionic compound contains positive and negative ions in a ratio that gives zero total charge. A metal paired with a single nonmetal is a useful clue for the ionic examples here. The metal is written first and the nonmetal gets an -ide ending.

If the metal can form ions with different charges, the name includes a Roman numeral in parentheses. It tells you the charge of one metal ion. Use the count in the formula to work backward from the total negative charge.

Numeral key +1 → I +2 → II +3 → III +4 → IV +5 → V +6 → VI

Name Cu2O

Given: Two copper ions for each oxide ion, O2−. Find: The charge of each copper ion, then the name.

  1. One oxide ion contributes 1 × (−2) = −2.
  2. The two copper ions together must contribute +2 to make zero.
  3. Divide by the number of copper ions. (+2) ÷ 2 = +1 per copper ion.
  4. Write copper(I) oxide. The I records +1 per copper ion. The lowered 2, a subscript, counts copper ions.

Here and below, charge numbers are in units of the elementary charge, the size of a proton’s charge.

Try a different formula

Name Cr2S3. Use S2− for sulfide. Write the total negative charge, the charge of each chromium ion and the full name before opening the comparison.

Compare your reasoning

Three sulfide ions contribute 3 × (−2) = −6. Two chromium ions together supply +6, so (+6) ÷ 2 = +3 per chromium ion. Write chromium(III) sulfide. If you wrote (VI), you used the charge of both chromium ions. Check by adding two +3 charges to −6.

Retry. What name fits SnF4 with F−? How would the tin numeral change if there were only two fluoride ions per tin ion?

Compare the retry

Four fluoride ions contribute −4, so one tin ion must supply +4. The name is tin(IV) fluoride. With two fluoride ions per tin ion, the total negative charge would be −2 and the numeral would be (II).

02 / Build an ionic formula

Keep each group together

A polyatomic ion is a group of bonded atoms with one overall charge. Match the whole formula in a reference, including its small numbers and charge. Similar names can belong to different groups.

Ion key for this lesson

  • SO42− sulfate
  • SO32− sulfite
  • HPO42− hydrogen phosphate
  • PO43− phosphate
  • NH4+ ammonium
  • C2O42− oxalate

Compare the four oxygens in sulfate with the three in sulfite. Both have charge −2, so charge alone cannot tell you which name to use.

Write a formula and name from two ions

Given: Al3+ and SO42−. Find: The smallest neutral ratio, then the formula and name.

  1. Match SO42− in the key. The full formula matches sulfate.
  2. Positive charge comes in threes and negative charge in twos. The smallest shared charge size is six. Two aluminum ions give 2 × (+3) = +6. Three sulfate ions give 3 × (−2) = −6.
  3. Write the positive ion first and leave out the charge marks. In Al2(SO4)3, the 3 outside the parentheses counts whole sulfate ions. The 4 still counts oxygen atoms in each sulfate ion.
  4. Write aluminum sulfate. In this naming convention, aluminum has a fixed +3 charge, so its name needs no Roman numeral.

Test a count before writing a formula

Pair Mg2+ with phosphate, PO43−. Predict which trial count gives the simplest neutral ratio. The charges of the ions and the composition of phosphate are fixed. Change only how many of each ion you count.

No trial selected.

Compare the three trial counts
  • One to one gives 1 × (+2) + 1 × (−3) = −1. The trial has extra negative charge.
  • Three to two gives 3 × (+2) + 2 × (−3) = 0. The ratio is neutral and already smallest.
  • Six to four gives 6 × (+2) + 4 × (−3) = 0. The ratio is neutral and both counts can be divided by two.

Use the smallest neutral trial to write the formula. Check what your outside subscript counts.

Compare your formula

Mg3(PO4)2 is magnesium phosphate. The outer 2 counts two phosphate ions, for a total of two phosphorus atoms and eight oxygen atoms per ratio written.

Let the ion key settle the name

Given NH4+ and HPO42−, write a formula and a name. Use the ion key for both labels. Mark which group repeats and explain how you know.

Compare your reasoning

Two ammonium ions supply +2 and one hydrogen phosphate supplies −2. The formula is (NH4)2HPO4 and the name is ammonium hydrogen phosphate. The H at the start of HPO42− is part of that ion. If you bracketed the negative ion, check the counts. Only the positive group occurs more than once.

Retry. Given Mg2+ and SO32−, write a formula and a name. Which parts of the ion key row helped?

Compare the retry

One ion of each kind balances the charges. Write MgSO3, magnesium sulfite. The three oxygens identify sulfite. No parentheses are needed because just one sulfite ion is counted in the ratio.

03 / Read and write molecular names

Count atoms in one molecule

A binary molecular compound contains exactly two different elements per molecule. Use the prefix method when the problem identifies a molecule. These compounds often combine two nonmetals and can also include elements such as boron. A prefix at the start of an element name tells you the count of that element in one molecule. Keep the element order. Give the second element an -ide ending.

Prefix key 1 mono- 2 di- 3 tri- 4 tetra- 5 penta- 6 hexa- 7 hepta- 8 octa- 9 nona- 10 deca-

Formula to name

Cl2O has two chlorine atoms and one oxygen atom. Write dichlorine monoxide. Keep mono- for one atom of the second element. Mono- and oxide combine as monoxide.

When there is one atom of the first element, leave off mono-. For SF4, one sulfur and four fluorine atoms give sulfur tetrafluoride.

Name a molecule from its formula

Name SeF6. Mark where each count comes from. Then explain which position would take mono- if both atom counts were one.

Compare your reasoning

There is one selenium atom, so start with selenium. Six fluorine atoms give hexafluoride. Write selenium hexafluoride. If both counts were one, only the second element would take mono-.

Retry. Name P4S10, and say whether either count is implied.

Compare the retry

Tetraphosphorus decasulfide states both counts. Use tetra- for four phosphorus atoms and deca- for ten sulfur atoms. Sulfur changes to sulfide in second position.

Name to formula

For disulfur decafluoride, di- sets sulfur to two and deca- sets fluorine to ten. Keep the order and write S2F10. Dividing the subscripts by two would give a different atom count per molecule.

Read an unprefixed first element as one. Boron trifluoride gives BF3. The symbol B has no subscript because one is implied.

Write a formula from a molecule’s name

Write the formula for tetraphosphorus trisulfide. Match each prefix to the element that follows it.

Compare your reasoning

Tetra- sets phosphorus to four, and tri- sets sulfur to three. The formula is P4S3. If you switched the 3 and 4, read the names in order and place each subscript right after its element.

Retry. Write a formula for disulfur dichloride. Explain whether you should reduce any subscripts.

Compare the retry

S2Cl2 has two atoms of each element in a molecule. Keep both subscripts to preserve those counts.

04 / Choose your method

Three labels to complete

Imagine putting these labels into a chemical inventory. For each, write the requested label and explain which part of the clue told you what to count. Try all three before checking.

  1. Cr3+ and C2O42−. Write one formula and the full name for the compound.
  2. CuCl2. Write its name. Use Cl− for chloride.
  3. Diphosphorus tetrafluoride. Write its formula.
Compare the three labels
  1. The charges mark ions. Several rows in the ion key have charge −2 and four oxygens. Only oxalate has two carbon atoms. Two chromium ions give +6 and three oxalate ions give −6. Write Cr2(C2O4)3, chromium(III) oxalate. The (III) is the charge on each chromium ion. The outside subscript 3 counts whole oxalate ions.
  2. Copper paired with a nonmetal suggests the charge method here. Two chlorides contribute −2, so one copper contributes +2. Write copper(II) chloride.
  3. The prefixes give the counts. Write P2F4. A ratio of one to two would lose the number of atoms in one molecule.

Take the check with you

For an ionic formula, account for all charges and verify the smallest ratio. For an ionic name, check the charge per metal ion. For a group you have not seen before, look in your course’s polyatomic ion table and match the entire formula and charge. For a molecular formula or name, read back the actual count of each kind of atom.

Tomorrow, before reopening the lesson, recall what a Roman numeral, a subscript outside parentheses and a molecular prefix each tell you. Then write the formula for silicon tetrafluoride using Si for silicon and explain what the prefix counts.

Check on your return

Write SiF4. Tetra- counts four fluorine atoms in one molecule. The first element has no prefix, so it contributes one atom.

Glossary

Subscript
A lowered number that counts the preceding atom or the group in preceding parentheses.
Ion
An atom or bonded group with an electric charge. A raised sign and number show that charge. A lone sign means a charge of +1 or −1.
Ionic compound
A substance made of positive and negative ions with zero total charge. Its formula gives the simplest neutral ratio of those ions.
Roman numeral
A number written with letters, such as I, II, III or IV. In the ionic names here it gives the positive charge of a single metal ion.
Polyatomic ion
A bonded group of atoms that acts as one charged unit when you balance charges and write a formula.
Molecule
A distinct group of bonded atoms. Its molecular formula gives the number of atoms of each element in one such group.
Binary compound
A compound containing exactly two different elements. A molecule with four atoms of one element and three of another is still binary.
Prefix
A beginning such as di- or tri- that records an atom count in the molecular names in this lesson.
