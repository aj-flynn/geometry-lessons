Total and net ionic equations

Start with a formula or a name. A species is one kind of particle or substance in the mixture. Write a total ionic equation that includes every species in the reaction record, then a net ionic equation that shows what changes.

In 3Ca(OH)2, how many calcium, oxygen, and hydrogen atoms are represented? Which number could you change to balance an equation?

Check the starting count

There are 3 Ca, 6 O, and 6 H atoms. One formula unit is one count of the simplest neutral ion ratio written in the compound’s formula. The coefficient 3 counts three of those units. A subscript gives a count within a formula. The 2 after the parentheses applies to the whole OH group. Change the coefficient when balancing.

1. Build the formulas

Given · A mixture of copper(II) chloride and sodium hydroxide, both aqueous (dissolved in water), gives solid copper(II) hydroxide and aqueous sodium chloride. Use these stated forms throughout.

Goal · Write a neutral formula for each named compound, then carry its state label into the equation.

An ion is a charged atom or bonded group. Use each ion’s charge to build a neutral formula.

Look up ions for the lesson examples
Positive ions
Copper(II) Cu2+, sodium Na+, calcium Ca2+, zinc Zn2+, rubidium Rb+, cobalt(II) Co2+, potassium K+, aluminum Al3+, lithium Li+, barium Ba2+
Negative ions
Chloride Cl−, hydroxide OH−, fluoride F−, acetate C2H3O2−, iodide I−, phosphate PO43−, bromide Br−, sulfate SO42−, chromate CrO42−

For more on reading ion formulas and charges, see OpenStax Chemistry 2e, Ionic and Molecular Compounds, Table 2.5.

Find hydroxide in the lookup. One OH− contributes one negative charge. The II in copper(II) gives a 2+ charge per copper ion. Two hydroxide ions give a combined charge of −2, so one copper ion and two hydroxide ions make a neutral ratio.

Cu(OH)2(s) · The outer 2 counts two intact OH groups. OH− is a polyatomic ion, a bonded group with one overall charge. The (s) marks a solid.

Write an intermediate

Use the lookup to write the formula and state of copper(II) chloride. Then write the two sodium compounds from the same givens.

Compare the formulas

CuCl2(aq) needs two Cl− per Cu2+. Cl is one atom, so its 2 needs no parentheses. NaOH(aq) and NaCl(aq) each pair a 1+ ion with a 1− ion in a 1:1 ratio.

Repair a formula with a charge mismatch

Test one written formula by multiplying each ion’s charge by its subscript count and adding. CuOH gives +2 − 1 = +1, so it cannot represent a neutral formula unit. Retry with aqueous calcium acetate. Write its formula using the lookup.

Compare calcium acetate

Ca(C2H3O2)2(aq). Each of two acetate ions contributes −1. Their combined −2 balances calcium’s +2.

2. Balance before splitting

Write the compound formulas on the sides given by the names. This is the molecular equation format, even for salts. The formulas and states are fixed now. Change only coefficients so each element has the same count before and after the arrow.

CuCl2(aq) + NaOH(aq) → Cu(OH)2(s) + NaCl(aq)

Predict · Keep one Cu(OH)2(s). What coefficient should go before NaOH(aq)? First count the intact OH groups on each side.

Compare the balance

Two OH groups are needed on the left. Put 2 before NaOH. That brings in two Na, so put 2 before NaCl. It also gives the two Cl already present on the left.

CuCl2(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Cu(OH)2(s) + 2NaCl(aq)

Both sides have Cu 1, Cl 2, Na 2, O 2, and H 2. The coefficients 1, 2, 1, 2 share no factor greater than 1.

3. Split, compare, and cancel

Use the balanced equation. A dissolved strong electrolyte is present almost entirely as separate ions. In these examples, split the soluble salts and soluble ionic strong bases such as NaOH and KOH into their ions. Keep each solid formula together. Use both the (aq) label and the identity of the substance to decide what to split.

Predict · Write the ion terms for 2NaOH(aq). Which product stays in its formula form? Then write the full equation with the ion terms and the solid.

Compare the total ionic equation

2NaOH contributes 2Na+(aq) and 2OH−(aq). Cu(OH)2(s) is a precipitate, a solid formed in the mixture. Keep its formula together.

Cu2+(aq) + 2Cl−(aq) + 2Na+(aq) + 2OH−(aq) → Cu(OH)2(s) + 2Na+(aq) + 2Cl−(aq)

Atom counts still match the balanced formula equation. In units of elementary charge, the left is +2 − 2 + 2 − 2 = 0 and the right is 0 + 2 − 2 = 0.

Predict · Which species can you match across the arrow one for one? Match each formula, charge, and state. Cancel equal counts of matching species from both sides. An ion that stays unchanged is a spectator ion.

Trace the particles and compare the net equation

Cu2+(aq) + 2OH−(aq) → Cu(OH)2(s)

Na+ and Cl− remain in solution after mixing. Canceling their terms only changes the written record. The net equation has Cu 1, O 2, H 2, and total charge 0 on each side. Its coefficient ratio is 1:2:1.

Try the split on a new equation

Ca(C2H3O2)2(aq) + 2NaF(aq) → CaF2(s) + 2NaC2H3O2(aq)

Write the total and net ionic equations, then account for the charge and every element on both sides of your net equation.

Compare the fluoride case

Ca2+(aq) + 2C2H3O2−(aq) + 2Na+(aq) + 2F−(aq) → CaF2(s) + 2Na+(aq) + 2C2H3O2−(aq)

Ca2+(aq) + 2F−(aq) → CaF2(s)

The acetate group stays intact as an ion and cancels twice on each side. The net counts are Ca 1 and F 2. The net charge is +2 − 2 = 0 on the left and 0 on the right. Both equations are in their smallest whole-number ratios.

Repair a split that erases a solid

Start with this balanced equation.

ZnSO4(aq) + 2KOH(aq) → Zn(OH)2(s) + K2SO4(aq)

A draft lists these products in the total ionic equation. Which terms should you replace? Which ions can still cancel?

Zn2+(aq) + 2OH−(aq) + 2K+(aq) + SO42−(aq)

Compare the repair

Keep Zn(OH)2(s) as a single product term. Splitting it would create a false match for the reactant zinc and hydroxide ions. Cancel only the two potassium ions and one intact sulfate ion on each side.

Zn2+(aq) + 2OH−(aq) → Zn(OH)2(s)

Both sides contain one Zn, two O, and two H, with total charge 0.

Retry · Write the total and net ionic equations for this new case.

BaCl2(aq) + K2CrO4(aq) → BaCrO4(s) + 2KCl(aq)

Compare the retry

Ba2+(aq) + 2Cl−(aq) + 2K+(aq) + CrO42−(aq) → BaCrO4(s) + 2K+(aq) + 2Cl−(aq)

Ba2+(aq) + CrO42−(aq) → BaCrO4(s)

Match two K+ and two Cl− on each side. Chromate stays one bonded group in the aqueous ion and is part of the solid product. The net counts are one Ba, one Cr, and four O on each side, with total charge 0.

When every ion has a match

Suppose the proposed exchange is shown below. The state labels apply under the given conditions. Predict whether any term remains after splitting and matching. Write the complete list for each side before deciding.

Zn(C2H3O2)2(aq) + 2RbI(aq) → ZnI2(aq) + 2RbC2H3O2(aq)

Compare the two ion lists

Zn2+(aq) + 2C2H3O2−(aq) + 2Rb+(aq) + 2I−(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + 2I−(aq) + 2Rb+(aq) + 2C2H3O2−(aq)

Both sides have Zn 1, C 4, H 6, O 4, Rb 2, and I 2, with charge 0. Every species has the same count, charge and state on both sides. Report no reaction for this proposed exchange in the model. The ions still mix in the solution.

The examples use an idealized model of dissolved strong electrolytes. Their states are given. In an experiment, whether a solid forms can depend on concentrations and conditions.

Your turn

For each fresh case, write the balanced formula and total ionic equations. Then report the net ionic equation or no reaction, and justify the result with a species match. Use the ion lookup when needed. Work both before opening either comparison.

Case A

Aqueous cobalt(II) acetate and aqueous potassium phosphate give solid cobalt(II) phosphate and aqueous potassium acetate.

Case B

Aqueous aluminum sulfate and aqueous lithium bromide are mixed. The proposed new pairings are aqueous aluminum bromide and aqueous lithium sulfate.

Compare case A

Three Co2+ and two PO43− give +6 and −6. Build that ratio into the solid formula first. Then supply three cobalt and two phosphate groups from the reactants, which leaves six potassium ions and six acetate ions in solution.

3Co(C2H3O2)2(aq) + 2K3PO4(aq) → Co3(PO4)2(s) + 6KC2H3O2(aq)

3Co2+(aq) + 6C2H3O2−(aq) + 6K+(aq) + 2PO43−(aq) → Co3(PO4)2(s) + 6K+(aq) + 6C2H3O2−(aq)

3Co2+(aq) + 2PO43−(aq) → Co3(PO4)2(s)

Cancel six K+ and six acetate ions per side. The total equation has Co 3, C 12, H 18, O 20, K 6, and P 2 on each side, with net charge 0 on each side. The net equation has Co 3, P 2, and O 8, with charge +6 − 6 = 0 on the left and 0 on the right. The ratio 3:2:1 cannot be reduced.

Compare case B

Two Al3+ balance three sulfate ions. That makes the formula Al2(SO4)3. It supplies two Al and three sulfate groups, so the products need two aluminum bromide units and three lithium sulfate units.

Al2(SO4)3(aq) + 6LiBr(aq) → 2AlBr3(aq) + 3Li2SO4(aq)

2Al3+(aq) + 3SO42−(aq) + 6Li+(aq) + 6Br−(aq) → 2Al3+(aq) + 6Br−(aq) + 6Li+(aq) + 3SO42−(aq)

Both sides have Al 2, S 3, O 12, Li 6, and Br 6. The charge sum is +6 − 6 + 6 − 6 = 0 on either side. Match equal quantities of all four ions. Report no reaction for the proposed exchange.

Return to your work

  1. Check that each named salt has a neutral formula. Keep its given state.
  2. Balance with coefficients. Count every element on both sides and check the smallest whole-number ratio.
  3. Split soluble salts into ions. Multiply the coefficient by the number of each ion per formula unit.
  4. Match identical species and cancel equal counts. Check atoms, total charge, and the ratio again. If every term cancels, report no reaction for the proposed exchange.

Compare the later recall

Three Ca2+(aq) and six I−(aq) give +6 − 6 = 0. The subscript gives two iodides per unit, the coefficient gives three units, and each iodide carries one negative charge.

Glossary

Species
A chemically distinct kind of particle or substance represented by a term in the equation. Identify it by its formula, charge, and state.
Formula unit
One count of the simplest neutral ratio of whole ions in an ionic compound. A formula unit of Ca(OH)2 counts one calcium ion and two hydroxide groups.
Ion
An atom or bonded group with a net electric charge. A raised charge applies to one ion.
Polyatomic ion
A bonded group of atoms with an overall charge. A subscript outside parentheses repeats the entire group.
Coefficient
A number in front of a formula that scales the amount of the entire species. An omitted coefficient is 1.
Subscript
A lowered number that gives the count of the preceding atom or parenthesized group in one formula unit.
State label
(aq) means dissolved in water, (s) means solid, (l) means liquid, and (g) means gas.
Strong electrolyte
A solute represented as essentially fully dissociated or ionized in water under the conditions used.
Molecular equation
The equation written with whole compound formulas, including ionic compounds.
Total ionic equation
Also called the complete ionic equation. It shows dissolved strong electrolytes as ions and retains the other species in their given forms.
Spectator ion
An ion represented with the same charge, state, and amount before and after the reaction.
Net ionic equation
The equation after equal amounts of unchanged species are canceled from both sides.
Precipitate
A solid that forms from a solution during a reaction.
