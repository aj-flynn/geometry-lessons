Start with the charge on one ion

An ion is a charged atom or group of atoms. Find acetate in the group-ion table in the UW–Madison ion charge charts. Its formula is CH3CO2−, also written C2H3O2−. One whole acetate ion has charge −1.

Try it. How many acetates balance the charge of one Zn2+ ion? Write a neutral formula and explain what its parentheses tell you to count.

Compare the charge count

Two acetate ions supply −2, so the charges add to zero. Write Zn(C2H3O2)2. The outer subscript, a small number below the line, counts two copies of the whole group. Each acetate still contains two C, three H and two O atoms.

Work one reaction from start to finish

Given names and states

Before
lead(II) acetate (aq) + rubidium iodide (aq)
Proposed products
lead(II) iodide (s) + rubidium acetate (aq)

State labels give physical form. (aq) means dissolved in water. (s) means solid.

Use the given states and treat dissolved salts and strong bases as separated ions in these examples. Look up rubidium and iodide in the UW–Madison ion charge charts.

1. Fix the formulas, then balance the equation

An ionic compound’s formula must have zero total charge. Lead(II) specifies Pb2+. It needs two −1 ions, so use Pb(C2H3O2)2 and PbI2. The chart gives Rb+ and I−. Rubidium needs one −1 ion, giving RbI and RbC2H3O2.

The molecular equation lists substances by formula. The arrow separates what you mix from what is proposed to form.

Pb(C2H3O2)2(aq) + RbI(aq) → PbI2(s) + RbC2H3O2(aq)

Balance by changing coefficients, the numbers in front of formulas. A coefficient multiplies the entire formula. Subscripts inside formulas and states are fixed once the substances and states are known.

Predict before checking. Start with a coefficient of 1 on every term. If you double only RbI(aq), which atom counts will change? Which counts will still differ across the arrow?

Compare the prediction

Double RbI and the left side has 2 Rb and 2 I. This fixes iodine but gives 2 Rb on the left and 1 on the right. Pb, C, H and O counts do not change when you double RbI. C, H and O still mismatch because the left side has two acetates and the right side has one. Use 2 rubidium acetates on the right to fix Rb. That also gives two acetates on the right, matching the two on the left.

Worked reaction · Finish balancing

Balanced molecular equation

Pb(C2H3O2)2(aq) + 2 RbI(aq) → PbI2(s) + 2 RbC2H3O2(aq)

Each side has 1 Pb, 2 Rb, 2 I, 4 C, 6 H and 4 O. The coefficients have no common factor larger than 1.

2. Count the dissolved particles

A formula unit gives the simplest ion ratio in an ionic compound. One lead acetate formula unit represents one lead ion and two intact acetate ions. Two rubidium iodide formula units give two rubidium ions and two iodide ions. The complete ionic equation, also called the total ionic equation, lists these ions individually.

Lead iodide is the precipitate, a solid formed from solution. Keep its solid formula whole.

Pb2+(aq) + 2 C2H3O2−(aq) + 2 Rb+(aq) + 2 I−(aq) → PbI2(s) + 2 Rb+(aq) + 2 C2H3O2−(aq)

Before mixing, total charge is +2 − 2 + 2 − 2 = 0 in elementary-charge units. The solid is neutral, so the right side is 0 + 2 − 2 = 0.

3. Match what stayed the same

Read the inventories before comparing. Trace the two iodides and the two acetates. Of those two ion types, which remains dissolved in the same quantity and form? Explain why an iodide in the solid cannot match a free iodide.

Compare what matched

Both acetates and both rubidium ions match in formula, charge, state and count. These spectator ions remain dissolved and cancel in the net ionic equation, which shows the change.

A tempting mistake is to cancel free iodide against the I in PbI2(s). Match whole species. The left has dissolved iodide and the right has a solid containing iodide. Keep both terms. Revise your cancellation using the state of each species.

Pb2+(aq) + 2 I−(aq) → PbI2(s)

Both sides have 1 Pb and 2 I. The left has charge +2 − 2 = 0 and the right has charge 0. The coefficients 1, 2, 1 are in the simplest ratio.

Keep positive groups intact too

The ion lookup gives ammonium NH4+ and sulfate SO42−. Two ammonium ions give 2(+1) = +2, balancing one sulfate ion’s −2. Write (NH4)2SO4 for neutral ammonium sulfate.

One formula unit of dissolved ammonium sulfate gives 2 NH4+(aq) + SO42−(aq). The outside 2 counts two whole ammonium ions with 2 N and 8 H atoms in total.

If the other side of a complete ionic equation also has 2 NH4+(aq), cancel those terms as spectator ions. The formula, charge, state and amount all match.

What if nothing changes?

This proposed swap is already atom-balanced. Before opening the comparison, write the complete ionic equation and decide whether any species changes.

RbC2H3O2(aq) + LiI(aq) → RbI(aq) + LiC2H3O2(aq)

Compare the two inventories

Rb+(aq) + C2H3O2−(aq) + Li+(aq) + I−(aq) → Rb+(aq) + I−(aq) + Li+(aq) + C2H3O2−(aq)

Each ion has the same formula, charge, state and count on both sides. The total charge is 0 on either side. The proposed formula partners differ, but the dissolved inventory is identical. Write no net reaction. The complete ionic line is evidence for your conclusion.

Case A comparison

Molecular

3 CuBr2(aq) + 2 K3PO4(aq) → Cu3(PO4)2(s) + 6 KBr(aq)

Complete ionic

3 Cu2+(aq) + 6 Br−(aq) + 6 K+(aq) + 2 PO43−(aq) → Cu3(PO4)2(s) + 6 K+(aq) + 6 Br−(aq)

Net ionic

3 Cu2+(aq) + 2 PO43−(aq) → Cu3(PO4)2(s)

Three +2 ions match two −3 groups. In the complete line, expand 2 K3PO4(aq) to 6 potassium ions and 2 whole phosphate groups. If you wrote 3 potassium ions, multiply the subscript by the coefficient and revise the line. Cancel 6 potassium and 6 bromide ions. The molecular and complete lines each have 3 Cu, 6 Br, 6 K, 2 P and 8 O per side. The net line has 3 Cu, 2 P and 8 O per side, with charge +6 − 6 = 0 on the left and 0 on the right. The complete line has zero charge on each side as well. Try Case B after the repair. Check how many perchlorate groups each coefficient represents.

Glossary

Ion
An atom or bonded group of atoms with an electrical charge. A polyatomic ion is one such group.
State label
The notation after a formula that describes its physical form. (aq) means aqueous, or dissolved in water, and (s) means solid.
Subscript
A small number below the line that counts an element or parenthesized group in one copy of a formula.
Coefficient
A number placed before a formula. It counts copies of the whole formula in the equation ratio. An omitted coefficient is 1.
Molecular equation
An equation that lists each substance by its formula, including dissolved salts written as neutral formulas.
Formula unit
The smallest whole-number ratio of ions represented by an ionic compound’s formula.
Complete ionic equation
An equation that lists dissolved, fully separated ions individually and retains intact species such as solids. Also called a total ionic equation.
Precipitate
A solid that forms from substances dissolved in solution.
Spectator ion
An ion present in the same form on both sides of the equation. Equal amounts cancel.
Net ionic equation
An equation that shows the chemical change after matching spectator terms are removed.

Ion lookup

The UW–Madison ion charge charts supply the ion formulas and charges used here. Roman numerals in names specify the charge of the named metal ion.

The examples use a model of salts and strong bases in water. Aqueous means dissolved in water. For other kinds of solutions, check how the solute behaves in water before deciding how to write it.
