Chemistry · Isotope data

Two calculations from an isotope table

Build an average mass from a mixture of atoms. Then check how far a lab reading is from a reference.

Start with “out of 100”

In one sample, 40% of the atoms are of one type. Write 40% as a decimal fraction. Now go the other way: what percent is a decimal fraction of 0.08?

Check the two conversions

40% means 40 out of 100, so its decimal fraction is 40 ÷ 100 = 0.40. A share of 0.08 means 8 out of 100, so it is 8%. Move between these forms by asking “how many out of 100?”

01 / Average massCount each atom’s contribution

A five-atom model

These are two isotopes: atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. J is a made-up element.

Masses and counts in the model
IsotopeMass (u)Count
J-2020.004
J-2222.001

u is the unified atomic mass unit. The 20 in J-20 is a mass number, a count of protons and neutrons. Use the mass column for calculations; real isotope masses need not equal their mass numbers.

Goal: Find the average atomic mass, the mass per atom averaged across this sample.

  1. Add all five atoms’ masses.Total mass = 4 × 20.00 u + 1 × 22.00 u
  2. Share that total across five atoms.Average mass = (4 × 20.00 u + 1 × 22.00 u) ÷ 5
  3. Divide each count by five instead. Each mass is now paired with its fractional abundance, the share of atoms in its row. Here 4 out of 5 is 80%, or 0.80, and 1 out of 5 is 20%, or 0.20. Average mass = (0.80 × 20.00 u) + (0.20 × 22.00 u)
    = 20.40 u

Why not 21.00 u? Averaging the two row masses alone would give each row half the atoms. This sample has more lighter atoms, so the average sits nearer 20.00 u. The average describes a mixture; it need not equal any one atom’s mass. A table for natural samples and a prepared sample can use different isotope shares.

Return to the five-atom data

Predict where the average moves

The heavier isotope starts at 20% of the atoms. If its share rises to 70%, which way will the average move? Will it end up below or above the halfway mark? Write or say a prediction before checking it.

20% heavier · 20.40 u average
Compare your prediction (also works without the slider)

At 70% heavier, the average moves right, past the halfway mark at 21.00 u. The masses stay fixed; only the number of atoms contributing each mass changes.

Average mass = (0.30 × 20.00 u) + (0.70 × 22.00 u)
= 21.40 u

02 / Percent errorSize the gap against its reference

Two instrument checks

For the same isotope, two test mixes have different accepted values, the reference values for those mixes.

Abundance readings for the two mixes
MixAccepted (%)Measured (%)
A10.08.0
B40.038.0

Goal: Compare the signed percent errors. The sign shows whether a reading was high or low. Compare the reading and the reference for the same quantity in the same mix.

  1. Subtract the reference from the reading. The subtraction of two percentages gives a gap in percentage points. A: 8.0% − 10.0% = −2.0 percentage points
    B: 38.0% − 40.0% = −2.0 percentage points
  2. Divide by the accepted value. A gap has to be sized against what should have been measured. Both numbers are on the same percentage scale, so the ratios have no units. A: −2.0 ÷ 10.0 = −0.20
    B: −2.0 ÷ 40.0 = −0.05
  3. Express each ratio as a percent.A: −0.20 = −20%
    B: −0.05 = −5%

Both readings were low by the same number of points. But the gap is one fifth of A’s reference and only one twentieth of B’s. This lesson uses the signed convention; when a lab asks for the size alone, report the magnitude without a minus sign.

Return to the instrument readings

A calibration sample

Nq is fictional. The reference column describes the accepted composition of the sample. A test run reads 22.2% Nq-41 and 21.8% Nq-44.

Nq-4140.9624.0

Nq-4443.9420.0

Write both entries on paper before checking.

  1. Write a numerical setup for the average atomic mass of Nq in the reference sample. Leave it as an expression with numbers and units.
  2. Calculate the signed percent error of the test run’s abundance reading for Nq-41. Show your calculation and report the result as a percent.
Return to the calibration data

Compare both lab report entries

1. Numerical setup: (0.24 × 40.96 u) + (0.56 × 41.95 u) + (0.20 × 43.94 u). You can stop there. If you evaluate it, the model gives 42.1104 u before rounding.

If you divided the three masses by three, you gave each isotope the same share. Replace each equal share with the fraction from that isotope’s own row. If you used 41, 42 and 44 as masses, replace the labels’ mass numbers with the masses in u.

2. Percent error: Nq-41 is the target: the measured abundance is 22.2%, and its reference abundance is 24.0%.

22.2% − 24.0% = −1.8 percentage points
−1.8 ÷ 24.0 = −0.075 (same percentage scale)
−0.075 = −7.5%

If you got −1.8%, your subtraction is right. Label that gap in percentage points, then divide it by the accepted abundance and express the ratio as a percent. Check that the negative sign matches the direction of the gap. Revise your work before trying the next case.

Reference vial V

Vial V contains fictional isotope Zq-33 (32.97 u; 73.0% accepted share) and Zq-31 (30.98 u; 27.0% accepted share). A test run reports 29.7% Zq-31.

Write the numerical setup for the vial’s reference average atomic mass, then calculate the signed percent error for the Zq-31 reading.

Return to the vial data
Compare your retry

Mass setup: (0.27 × 30.98 u) + (0.73 × 32.97 u). The 73% share belongs with the larger mass, despite being listed first.

Error: (29.7 − 27.0) ÷ 27.0 = 0.10 = +10.0%. Both inputs are abundances on the same percentage scale. The reading is above the reference, so the result is positive.

Come back tomorrow

Before looking at the examples, compare two abundance tests for an isotope. One reads 6% with a 5% reference; the other reads 21% with a 20% reference. A classmate says both percent errors are the same. Are they? Use the two reference shares to explain.

Compare your explanation

The gap is 1 percentage point in both tests, but the reference shares differ. The first signed percent error is (6 − 5) ÷ 5 = +20%; the second is (21 − 20) ÷ 20 = +5%. The first is larger because the same gap is a larger fraction of a smaller reference.

Returning to your own work? For an average, match each mass to its share and check that all shares total 100%. For percent error, match the quantity, identify the reference, and check that the sign agrees with high or low.

Glossary

Isotope
A form of an element with a particular number of neutrons. Isotopes of one element have the same number of protons.
Unified atomic mass unit (u)
A unit for very small masses. One u is one twelfth of the mass of a neutral carbon-12 atom at rest in its ground state.
Mass number
The number of protons plus neutrons in one atom’s nucleus. It is a count with no mass unit.
Average atomic mass
The average mass per atom in a specified mixture. Each isotope’s mass contributes in proportion to its share of the atoms.
Fractional abundance
The share of all atoms of an element in the sample that belong to an isotope, written as a decimal. Percent abundance expresses the same share out of 100.
Accepted value
The reference value agreed on for the quantity being measured.
Signed percent error
Measured value minus accepted value, divided by the accepted value and expressed as a percent. It needs a nonzero accepted value. For positive references like these, a negative result means the reading is low.
Percentage point
The unit for a difference between percentages on the same scale, before dividing by a reference.
