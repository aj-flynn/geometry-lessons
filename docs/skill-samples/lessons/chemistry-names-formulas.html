Names and formulas

Use clues in a chemical name to write its formula. Use a formula to name a compound when its metal can have several charges.

In Br−, does the raised minus sign give a charge or count atoms?

Check the symbol

The raised sign gives the charge of one bromide ion (a charged atom or group). A subscript, written low, gives a count. An omitted count means one.

Glossary · Ion lookup

1. Balance known charges

An ionic compound contains positive and negative ions. The formula of an ionic solid gives the simplest whole-number ratio of those ions. Together they must be neutral (total charge zero).

Write the formula for sodium sulfide

Sodium has a fixed charge in these compounds. The ion lookup gives Na+ and S2−.

  1. One sulfide gives a charge of −2.
  2. Two sodium ions supply 2 × (+1) = +2.
  3. Check the total charge: (+2) + (−2) = 0.
  4. Write Na2S. The simplest ratio is two sodium ions per sulfide ion.

Try aluminum phosphide

Use the lookup. Write the formula and explain why the ratio cannot be reduced before checking.

Which formula did you write?

Compare your formula

AlP. One Al3+ and one P3− give zero charge. A 3 to 3 ratio also balances but reduces to 1 to 1. Two aluminum ions and one phosphide would leave a charge of +3.

Retry with a new compound. Write the formula for aluminum sulfide.

Compare the retry

Al2S3. Two Al3+ give +6 and three S2− give −6. The 2 to 3 ratio cannot be reduced.

2. Read molecular prefixes

A molecular compound contains separate molecules. In the names here, a prefix at the start of an element’s name gives its atom count in one molecule. Keep those counts even if they share a factor.

Write the formula for carbon monoxide

  1. Carbon comes first. With no prefix on the first element, count one C.
  2. Monoxide is mono- (one) combined with oxide. Count one O.
  3. Write CO. Each molecule has one carbon atom and one oxygen atom.

Try a molecule

Write the formula for tetraphosphorus decasulfide. Use the prefix list if needed. Explain whether you can divide the two counts by two.

Which formula did you write?

Compare your formula

P4S10. Tetra- belongs to phosphorus and deca- belongs to sulfur. Dividing the subscripts would change the number of atoms per molecule.

Retry with a new compound. Write the formula for xenon tetrafluoride.

Compare the retry

XeF4. With no first prefix, use one Xe. The prefix tetra- gives four F atoms.

3. Use the metal’s Roman numeral

A Roman numeral after the metal gives its oxidation state, a number used to assign charge in a compound. For a metal ion made of one atom, the oxidation state equals its charge.

Write the formula for copper(II) bromide

From the name, one copper ion has charge +2. In the bromide row, read the charge for one ion, −1. ChEBI calls that field “Net Charge.”

Predict before testing. Start with one Cu2+ and one Br−. How would adding a second Br− change the total charge?

Trial: one Cu²⁺ and one Br⁻. Total charge +1.

Compare the two trials

One bromide gives (+2) + (−1) = +1. Two give (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0.

Write CuBr2 for the balanced 1 to 2 ratio. The II gives the charge on each copper ion. The subscript 2 counts bromide ions.

Try lead(II) sulfide

Write the formula. Explain where the II affects your work.

Compare your formula

PbS. The II gives Pb2+. One S2− balances it. If you wrote Pb2S, two lead ions give +4 and leave a charge of +2 after adding sulfide.

Retry with a new compound. Write the formula for gold(III) bromide.

Compare the retry

AuBr3. One Au3+ needs three Br− to balance the charge.

4. Find the charge from a formula

Read the negative ion first. A polyatomic ion is a charged group of atoms. Use the charge of the whole group. An outer subscript after parentheses counts groups.

Name Cr2(SO4)3

  1. Match SO4 to sulfate in the lookup. Each sulfate ion has charge −2. The 4 counts oxygen atoms inside each group.
  2. Three sulfate ions give 3 × (−2) = −6.
  3. The two chromium ions together must give +6 for the solid to be neutral.
  4. Divide the total among the two chromium ions: +6 ÷ 2 = +3 per ion.
  5. Write chromium(III) sulfate. Keep the name sulfate for the intact group.

For a negative ion made of one element, use its -ide name, such as sulfide.

Use the same accounting for OsO4

For the metal oxides here, assign each oxygen an oxidation state of −2. Four oxygens give 4 × (−2) = −8. One osmium accounts for +8, giving osmium(VIII) oxide. Here +8 is a bookkeeping value. The formula represents a molecule with bonded atoms.

Try Cu2S

Write the name and explain what the subscript tells you.

Compare your name

Copper(I) sulfide. One sulfide contributes −2, so the two copper ions share a total of +2. If you wrote copper(II), divide that total between the two copper ions.

Retry with a new compound. Name CuSO4.

Compare the retry

Copper(II) sulfate. There is one sulfate group with charge −2, so one copper ion has charge +2. A single polyatomic ion needs no parentheses in the formula.

Choose your method

Work on paper. Write each answer and name the clue that decided your method before opening any comparison.

Name Fe3(PO4)2

Explain what each subscript counts. Show the total charge of each kind of ion and find the charge of one iron ion.

Compare your name

Iron(II) phosphate. The lookup gives PO43−. The 4 counts oxygen atoms in one phosphate. The outer 2 counts phosphate groups, so the negative charge is 2 × (−3) = −6. The 3 counts iron ions, so each has charge +6 ÷ 3 = +2. Using −3 once would miss an entire group.

Write the formula for diboron tetrafluoride

Compare your formula

B2F4. Di- gives two boron atoms and tetra- gives four fluorine atoms. Use both counts as written in the name.

Write the formula for titanium(III) fluoride

Compare your formula

TiF3. The III gives +3 for one titanium ion. Three fluoride ions give −3. The 3 belongs on fluorine because it counts fluoride ions.

Write the formula for calcium sulfide

Compare your formula

CaS. Calcium has a fixed charge of +2. One sulfide has charge −2. One of each gives the simplest neutral ratio.

Name Re2O7 with a Roman numeral

Compare your name

Rhenium(VII) oxide. The seven oxygens contribute 7 × (−2) = −14 in the oxidation-state calculation. The two rhenium atoms account for +14, giving +7 each. VII records the oxidation state.

Later recall. On your next visit, explain from memory why the II in a metal name and the di- in a molecular name tell you different things. Then name Au2S and justify your choice.

Compare after recall

Gold(I) sulfide. One sulfide supplies −2. The two gold ions supply +2 altogether, so each is +1. A Roman numeral gives the charge per metal ion. A molecular prefix gives the atom count.

Glossary

Ion
An atom or group of atoms with an electric charge.
Subscript
A low number that counts atoms or groups. If the number is missing, count one.
Ionic compound
A compound made of positive and negative ions. Its solid formula gives the simplest ion ratio.
Neutral
Having total charge zero. The signed charges cancel.
Molecular compound
A compound made of separate molecules. A molecular formula gives the number of each kind of atom in one molecule.
Prefix
A word part at the beginning. Common counts include mono- (1), di- (2), tri- (3), tetra- (4), hexa- (6), and deca- (10).
Roman numeral
A symbol such as I, II, III, or IV. In these metal names it represents an oxidation state such as +1, +2, +3, or +4.
Oxidation state
The charge assigned to an atom by a set of bookkeeping rules. For an ion made of one atom, it equals the ion charge.
Polyatomic ion
Two or more bonded atoms that together have a charge. Parentheses keep repeated groups intact in a formula.
