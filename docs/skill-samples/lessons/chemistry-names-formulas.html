Start with two small numbers

A subscript sits low and counts atoms or groups. A superscript sits high and can show the charge on an ion, an atom or group with an electric charge.

H2 Which number counts atoms?

Zn2+ Which number tells you the charge?

Compare the two numbers

H2 has two hydrogen atoms in one molecule. Zn2+ shows a single zinc ion with charge +2. Read the number’s position and look for a charge sign. A symbol starts with a capital letter, and its second letter, if it has one, is lowercase.

For the names here, a metal followed by an ion name sends you to ionic charge balance. A name with two nonmetals and a prefix, a word part such as di-, sends you to molecular atom counts. These cues fit this lesson’s names. Use the compound type given by your course when the name or formula is unfamiliar.

Goal. Write the smallest whole-number ratio of ions with total charge zero.

Find potassium in the lookup. K+ means each potassium ion contributes +1. Find sulfide in a different row. The name ends in -ide because it represents one nonmetal atom here, with a charge of −2.

Predict, then change the count

You start with one K+ and one S2−. If you add one potassium ion, will the total charge be negative, zero, or positive? What if you add two? Test both predictions with the trial or open the comparison.Write both predictions before turning to the worked steps on the next page.

One potassium ion (+1) and one sulfide ion (−2). Total charge −1.

One potassium ion (+1) and one sulfide ion (−2). Total charge −1.

Compare both predictions

One added K+ gives 2(+1) + (−2) = 0. Two added K+ give 3(+1) + (−2) = +1. Adding changes the number of ions. Each ion keeps its own charge.

Worked example · Potassium sulfide

  1. One sulfide ion needs a total of +2, so use two potassium ions.
  2. 2(+1) + 1(−2) = 0. The ratio 2:1 is already as small as it can be.
  3. Write K2S, positive ion first. Leave out the implied subscript 1 and the individual charge signs in the neutral formula.

Given a name

Symbols and fixed charges checked against IUPAC and UW–Madison Netorial.

Some metals can have different charges in different compounds. In these names, a Roman numeral such as (III) gives the charge of one metal ion. It tells you to use Bi3+ here.

  1. One bromide ion contributes −1. To cancel +3, use three bromide ions for each bismuth ion.
  2. 1(+3) + 3(−1) = 0.
  3. Write BiBr3. The 3 lands on bromine because that is what you need three of.

  3. Keep both counts in P2I4. Reducing to PI2 would change the number of atoms in one molecule.

If the first element has no prefix, count one and leave its subscript blank. In xenon tetrafluoride, xenon has no prefix, so the formula is XeF4. The second element can use mono- to say one, as in monoxide.

03 Find the metal’s charge

Given a formula

Cu3(PO4)2

Cu
Copper, variable charge
Phosphate ion
PO43−

Phosphate entry and copper charge category from UW–Madison Netorial.

Goal. Name the compound, including the charge of one copper ion in Roman numerals.

Phosphate is a polyatomic ion, a charged group made of more than one atom. Keep PO4 together. The 4 counts oxygen atoms inside each group. The 2 outside the parentheses counts whole groups. A single group has no outside subscript or parentheses.

  1. Find phosphate in the lookup by matching PO4. Two groups contribute 2(−3) = −6.
  2. Since the compound is neutral, the three copper ions together contribute +6.
  3. Divide by the number of copper ions. +6 ÷ 3 = +2 per copper ion.
  4. Write copper(II) phosphate. Keep the name of the whole group. With a single-atom ion, use its -ide name from the lookup.

The outside 2 happens to match (II). The numeral comes from the division. For example, two groups of charge −2 would need a charge of +4 from a single metal ion, so the numeral in that case would be (IV).

Compare and repair the name

Three hydroxide ions give 3(−1) = −3. The single gold ion supplies +3. The name is gold(III) hydroxide.

If you counted oxygen and hydrogen separately, circle each OH as one unit, then multiply the group charge by the outside 3. The chromium entry in the practice set gives you another group and more than one metal ion.

Compare the recall case

Chromium(II) sulfate. There is one sulfate ion with charge −2 and one chromium ion, so the chromium charge is +2. Here the 4 stays inside the sulfate group. It counts oxygen atoms in that group.

Comparisons for the practice

  • Two small numbers. H2 has two atoms. Zn2+ is one ion with charge +2.
  • Potassium trial. Add one K+ for total charge 0. Add two for total charge +1.
  • Aluminum sulfide. Al2S3. One of each gives +1. The smallest equal totals in magnitude are 6 and 6.
  • Selenium hexafluoride. SeF6. The absent first prefix gives one Se. Hexa- counts F.
  • Au(OH)3. Gold(III) hydroxide. Three hydroxide ions supply −3. One gold ion supplies +3.
  • A. SrS. Charges of +2 and −2 cancel in a 1:1 ratio.
  • B. P4S6. The name fixes four P and six S atoms per molecule.
  • C. PbI2. Two iodide ions balance one lead ion of charge +2.
  • D. Copper(I) sulfide. Divide +2 among two copper ions.
  • E. Chromium(III) sulfate. Divide +6 among two chromium ions.
  • Later recall. Chromium(II) sulfate. One sulfate ion needs one chromium ion of charge +2.

Glossary

Subscript
A small lower number that counts the element or group immediately before it. A missing count means one.
Superscript
A small raised label. In ion formulas here, it shows charge, as in Zn2+.
Ion
An atom or group of atoms with a net electric charge.
Ionic compound
A compound made of positive and negative ions. Its formula gives the smallest whole-number ratio of ions with no net charge.
Prefix
A word part placed at the beginning. In the molecular names here, a prefix gives the number of atoms of the element it modifies.
Molecular compound
A substance made of molecules. Each molecule is a bonded group of atoms. A molecular formula counts the atoms of each element in one molecule.
Roman numeral
A number written with letters. In these simple ionic names, it shows the charge of one metal ion. I, II, III, IV, V and VI represent 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6.
Polyatomic ion
A bonded group of atoms with one net charge for the whole group, such as PO43−.
