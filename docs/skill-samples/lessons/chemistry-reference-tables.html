Reading chemistry tables

Predict when a solid metal can replace a dissolved metal. Then use the amount of dissolved charge to compare how well two solutions carry current.

Quick start

Which is the solid metal in a reaction, Ni(s) or Ni2+(aq)? Which is an ion, a particle with an electric charge?

Check the notation

Ni(s) is solid nickel. The 2+ in Ni2+(aq) marks a positive ion. Aqueous, written (aq), means dissolved in water. Look for the charge and state label when you compare two reaction setups.

01 · Relative position

Can the metal swap places?

An activity series orders metals by how readily they give up electrons. For a metal replacing a dissolved metal ion, Table J predicts a displacement when the solid metal is higher than the metal named by the ion. The course calls that direction spontaneous, meaning the reaction is favored on its own under the model’s conditions.

Switch the solid and the ion

Find Zn and Ni in the metal column. Zinc is higher.

Zinc is solid. Zn atoms give up electrons and enter the water as ions. Nickel ions gain those electrons and become solid nickel.

Zn(s) + Ni2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Ni(s)

Nickel is solid instead. Ni is below Zn, so Table J predicts no displacement of Zn2+(aq) by Ni(s).

This is a prediction about the favored direction under the course model. Table J gives no reaction speed or guarantee of a visible change.

Predict what changes

Start with solid cobalt in a solution containing Ni2+(aq). In a second setup, keep the cobalt solid and switch the solution to Mn2+(aq). Find Co and the two ion metals on the strip. Predict which setup should produce a new solid metal.

Which setup should produce a new solid metal?

Compare the metal prediction

Only the Ni2+(aq) setup is predicted to produce a new solid metal. Co is higher than Ni, so solid Co can supply electrons to nickel ions. Co is below Mn. Switching the solution to Mn2+(aq) changes the direction of the comparison.

Retry on paper. For Sn(s) and Pb2+(aq), predict whether a new solid metal forms. Name it if one does.

Compare the metal retry

Sn is above Pb. The model predicts that lead ions gain electrons and become Pb(s).

02 · Rule and exception

Which solution carries more current?

Quick formula check. A polyatomic ion is a group of bonded atoms with an overall charge. In Zn(ClO3)2, which group stays together as one ion, and how many copies of that ion are in the formula?

Compare the grouped formula

Table E lists the group inside the parentheses as chlorate, ClO3−. The 3 counts oxygen atoms in one chlorate ion. The 2 outside the parentheses counts two whole chlorate ions for each zinc ion. When using Table F, look up chlorate as one ion.

A saturated solution holds as much dissolved substance as it can at that temperature. Different substances can reach saturation at very different concentrations.

Solubility describes how much of a substance can dissolve. Table F groups ionic compounds by how readily they dissolve in water. Find the ions in the formula, then read the matching rule and check the partner ion for exceptions. Even compounds listed as insoluble may dissolve a little.

Table F · Selected rules for this lesson
Ion or familyRule in water
Halides, negative ions such as Cl−, Br− and I−Usually soluble. One exception is when paired with Pb2+.
Phosphate PO43− or sulfide S2−Usually insoluble. Soluble with a Group 1 ion or ammonium, NH4+.
Acetate C2H3O2−Soluble in Table F.

This excerpt omits other rules and some halide exceptions. Use the full Table F for other ion pairs.

Same chloride, different partners

  1. Find the shared ion. Both RbCl and PbCl2 contain chloride, Cl−.
  2. Check the partners. With Rb+, the chloride follows the soluble rule. With Pb2+, the chloride falls under an insoluble exception.
  3. Follow the charge. In separate saturated water solutions at the same temperature, RbCl supplies many more mobile ions and is expected to conduct better. Dissolved ions can move and carry electric charge.

Use this comparison when one salt is soluble and the others are very slightly soluble. Table F does not report conductivity, how readily a solution carries current. An exact ranking needs ion concentrations and how fast the ions move in an electric field.

Predict after a partner switch

Two separate water samples at the same temperature are saturated with K3PO4 and Sr3(PO4)2. Potassium is in Group 1. Strontium is in Group 2. Read the selected rules and predict which solution contains many more dissolved ions.

Which solution should conduct better?

Compare the solution prediction

K3PO4 is soluble because K+ is a Group 1 ion. Sr2+ does not meet either phosphate exception, so far less Sr3(PO4)2 dissolves. The saturated potassium phosphate solution should conduct better because it has many more mobile ions.

Retry on paper. Which would you choose for a better conductor, a saturated PbBr2 or ZnBr2 solution at the same temperature?

Compare the solution retry

ZnBr2 should give the better conductor. Bromide is a halide. Pb2+ is an exception and Zn2+ follows the usual soluble rule.

03 · Choose the evidence

Two decisions at a lab station

For each decision, choose a table, make a choice, and write one sentence using evidence from the table.

Pick a strip

A bath contains Co2+(aq). A lab group wants cobalt metal to form. The available strips are tin, lead and zinc. Which strip should the group pick?

Compare your strip choice

Pick zinc. Table J puts Zn above Co. Solid zinc can supply electrons to cobalt ions so they become solid cobalt. Sn and Pb are both below Co.

Pick a sample

A conductivity meter tested three saturated water solutions at the same temperature. One sample gave a much higher reading than the other two. The labels fell off. The possible labels are ZnS, PbI2 and Zn(C2H3O2)2. Which label belongs on the highest-reading sample?

Compare your sample choice

Choose zinc acetate, Zn(C2H3O2)2. Table F puts acetates in the soluble group. ZnS follows the insoluble sulfide rule, and PbI2 is an exception to the soluble halide rule. Zinc acetate supplies far more dissolved ions at saturation.

Back to your work

  • For a metal in an ion solution, mark the solid first. Then compare its position with the ion’s metal.
  • For saturated salt solutions, separate each formula into ions. Read the rule and test the partner for exceptions. Then link the amount dissolved to the charge that can move.

Glossary

Ion
An atom or group of atoms with a charge. Positive ions are cations. Negative ions are anions.
Polyatomic ion
Two or more bonded atoms that together carry a charge. In an ionic formula, a subscript after parentheses counts copies of the entire ion.
Aqueous (aq)
Dissolved in water. In contrast, (s) labels a solid.
Activity series
A ranking used to predict which metals more readily lose electrons in displacement reactions. In Table J, more active metals are higher.
Metal displacement
A reaction in which a solid metal becomes ions and another metal’s dissolved ions become solid metal.
Spontaneous
A process favored under the stated conditions. A spontaneous process may still be slow.
Halide
A negative ion formed from a halogen atom. The halide row of Table F covers chloride, bromide and iodide.
Saturated solution
A solution with the equilibrium amount of dissolved solute at its temperature. Any extra solid can remain undissolved.
Solubility
How much of a substance can dissolve under specified conditions. Table F uses broad categories for water solutions.
Conductivity
How readily a material carries electric current. In water solutions, moving ions carry the charge.
