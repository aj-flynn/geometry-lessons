From table to prediction

Use two kinds of reference table to predict what happens when metals meet dissolved ions (charged particles) and which salt solution will carry current better. For each prediction, point to the evidence that supports it.

Help with state and charge labels

(s) means solid, and (aq) means aqueous, or dissolved in water. A raised charge such as 3+ means three positive units of charge.

01 · Worked example

Can the solid supply electrons?

Two trials, same pair of metals

Start: manganese strip, Mn(s), in water containing nickel ions, Ni2+(aq).

Swap: nickel strip, Ni(s), in water containing manganese ions, Mn2+(aq).

More active at the top ↑

  1. Mn Manganese
  2. Ni Nickel

Relative order: NYSED, 2011, Table J (selected metals).

The activity series ranks metals by how readily they give up electrons in this comparison. In displacement, atoms from the solid give electrons to dissolved ions of a different metal. The ions that gain electrons become solid metal.

  1. Start with the solid. The solid is Mn, so find Mn first. Then find Ni, the element in the dissolved ion.
  2. Compare their positions. Mn is above Ni. The table predicts Mn can supply electrons to Ni2+.
  3. Track what changes. Mn becomes dissolved Mn2+; Ni2+ becomes solid Ni.

Mn(s) + Ni2+(aq) → Mn2+(aq) + Ni(s)

The table predicts direction without an outside power source, not speed. A coating on the strip can slow a real trial.

Predict the swap

Will Ni(s) displace Mn2+(aq)? Say which element now has to give electrons, and use the order to justify your prediction.

02 · Worked example

How many ions can get into the water?

Two chloride solutions

Each sample is saturated: it holds as much dissolved salt as it can at that temperature. Compare the liquids at the same temperature.

LiClPbCl2

Solubility guide
IonRuleException
Cl−SolubleWith Pb2+: low solubility

NYSED, 2011, Table F (rule narrowed to these choices).

Solubility is how much of a substance can dissolve under given conditions. Conductivity is how readily current flows. In a salt solution, moving ions carry the charge; ions locked in undissolved crystals cannot travel through the water.

  1. Find a rule. LiCl contains Li+ and Cl−. Look up Cl−: its salts are usually soluble.
  2. Check the partner ion. Li+ does not trigger the listed exception; Pb2+ does. PbCl2 has low solubility, even though it is also a chloride.
  3. Compare what can move. Saturated LiCl is the better conductor predicted by this guide. Much more LiCl can dissolve and supply mobile ions.

The guide separates soluble salts from salts with very low solubility. It cannot reliably rank two soluble salts. Actual conductivity also depends on ion concentration, charge, mobility and temperature.

Formula-reading help

A polyatomic ion is a bonded group with a charge. Match its full formula to a row; don’t split the group. A subscript outside parentheses counts whole copies of the group. For example, K2C2O4 contains two K+ for each C2O42−; the subscripts in oxalate stay part of the ion.

Compare after you try

Read one comparison at a time. If your reasoning differs, revise your sentence and check again.

Label check

Co(s) is the solid metal; Co2+(aq) is the dissolved cobalt ion. The raised 2+ shows charge. The parentheses tell you the state. Back to the label check.

Predict the swap

No displacement is predicted. The nickel strip would now have to give electrons to Mn2+. Ni is below Mn. If you predicted “yes” both times, underline which is solid in each case and redo the comparison from that element. Back to the swap.

Screen A

C. Zinc. Zn is the only solid in the list above Co, so it can supply electrons to Co2+. Ni is close to Co, but it’s below Co, as are Sn and Pb. If you wrote “Ni is close,” replace distance with direction: is the solid above or below the element in the ion? Revise Screen A, then test your reasoning with the fresh pair.

Screen B

D. Ammonium acetate. Its ions are NH4+ and C2H3O2−. The acetate row classifies the salt as soluble, so the saturated liquid can contain many mobile ions. For PbI2, the lead ion triggers the iodide exception. The zinc phosphate and manganese sulfide do not meet their rows’ exceptions. If you chose the formula with the most ions per unit, first ask how many units can actually dissolve. Revise Screen B, then try a new partner ion.

Same phosphate, new partner

Zinc phosphate fits the usual low solubility rule; potassium phosphate is soluble because K+ is a Group 1 ion. Expect saturated potassium phosphate to conduct better. In your revised sentence, name the ion that triggers the exception. Back to the retry.

Later recall

Setup 2. Solid Zn is above Sn and can give electrons to Sn2+. Tin starts in the liquid as ions and becomes Sn(s). Setup 1 reverses the roles and is not predicted to displace zinc. Back to the two setups.

Glossary

(s)
Solid state.
Aqueous, (aq)
Dissolved in water.
Ion
An atom or bonded group of atoms with a net electric charge.
Activity series
A ranked list used here to compare how readily solid metals give up electrons.
Metal displacement
A reaction in which a solid metal becomes ions and the dissolved ions of another metal become solid metal.
Saturated solution
A solution holding the equilibrium amount of dissolved substance at the given conditions. Different substances can have very different amounts dissolved.
Solubility
How much of a substance dissolves at equilibrium in a specified solvent under given conditions.
Electrical conductivity
How readily a material conducts electric current. In these liquids, moving ions carry the charge.
Spontaneous reaction
A reaction favored under the stated conditions without a continuing input such as an outside power source. It may be slow.
Polyatomic ion
A bonded group of two or more atoms with an overall charge, such as PO43−.

Where the evidence comes from

The rank and solubility excerpts adapt selected entries from the New York State Education Department’s Reference Tables for Physical Setting/Chemistry (2011), Tables J and F. Each excerpt is limited to the choices on that card.

OpenStax: Electrolytes explains how dissolved ions move and carry current, including the difference between how much dissolves and what happens to the portion that dissolves.
