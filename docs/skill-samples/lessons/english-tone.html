When a poem changes tone

Find the words that make a voice sound different as a poem goes on. Then use two details to explain the change.

Jot down your thinking for each task before turning to its comparison on a later page.

Hear the difference

A poem’s speaker is the voice we hear in it. Its tone is the attitude that voice shows. Which line makes the speaker sound more eager? Pick one word that helps you tell.

  • “I waited for the doors to open.”
  • “I ached for the doors to open.”
Compare the two door lines

In both lines, the doors are still closed. “Ached” makes the speaker’s wish for them to open sound especially strong.

Follow the words through a change

The Parcel

  1. Another box from the shop.
  2. I dragged it up the stairs.
  3. More tape to saw through.
  4. Inside, my old lunch tin,
  5. its handle fixed at last.
  6. I traced the little stitches
  7. of wire where you mended it.
  8. I set it by my bed.

A tone shift happens when the attitude changes. Read the whole poem. In lines 2–7, where does the speaker start to sound different? Pick an early and a later phrase that help you hear the change.

After your first attempt, open the model comparison and complete the response-repair task inside it.

Compare a model, then repair a response

At first the speaker carries a box upstairs. “Dragged” makes carrying it sound like a chore. Finding the repaired tin is the event. Look at how the speaker describes what happens next.

One supported reading is that the speaker goes from sounding bothered by the parcel to sounding appreciative, or thankful for the care behind the repair. The early word sets up the attitude and the later phrase shows how it changes.

Compare line 6 with “I saw the wire.” The same repair would be there. “Traced the little stitches” lets us hear the speaker’s interest in the work.

Make the detail do some work

Here’s a first try about “The Parcel.”

The speaker goes from annoyed to thankful. The poem says “dragged” and “traced the little stitches.”

Both phrases are evidence, or details that can support an idea. Add a reason for each choice. What does the speaker do, and what does each phrase make that action sound like?

Reread “The Parcel”
Compare your revision

“Dragged” makes taking the box upstairs sound like a chore, so the speaker sounds annoyed. When the speaker “traced the little stitches,” the speaker took time to feel the tiny signs of someone’s work. That close attention makes the speaker sound thankful for the repair.

Read your revision without the two quoted phrases. If all that’s left is the claim or a list of events, add what each phrase suggests about the attitude.

Try the move again

The Tower

  1. I crowned my tower
  2. with the last wooden block.
  3. It leaned into my shadow.
  4. One block slid,
  5. then the whole stack folded
  6. like a cheap paper fan.

Someone says the speaker sounds proud all the way through. Choose one detail from the start and one from the end. Explain what each detail makes the speaker sound like.

Compare your retry

“Crowned” makes placing the last block sound like putting a crown on something grand. The speaker sounds proud. “Cheap paper fan” makes the tower sound flimsy and ordinary as it collapses. By the end the speaker sounds let down, or even amused at how easily the tower fell.

“It leaned into my shadow” could warn us that the tower will fall. Use the comparison to a “cheap paper fan” to explain how the speaker talks about that fall. Revise your answer if it only tells what happened.

Write your own response

Sound Check

  1. My name was still on the song list.
  2. The crew unrolled the cables.
  3. Someone set out two folding chairs.
  4. The bass player waved me over.
  5. I found the new drummer
  6. sitting at my drum set.
  7. My thumb found a dent
  8. in the wood of a stick.
  9. I counted every dent again
  10. before I handed over the pair.
  11. A cable tapped against the floor.
  12. The first beat came in crooked.
  13. Then the next beat tucked itself
  14. into a gap I always left.
  15. I heard the beat I’d taught the band
  16. with a whole new room inside it.
  17. My hands took up the beat
  18. on my knees.
  19. When the beat stopped,
  20. the bass player lifted a hand.
  21. In the hall a cart rattled past.
  22. We were up in five minutes.

Read the whole poem. On paper, write a short paragraph explaining how the speaker’s word choices in lines 7–18 shift the tone. Support your response with two details from the poem.

Reread “Sound Check”
Compare after you write

The speaker goes from sounding reluctant to lend the sticks to sounding impressed by what the new drummer adds. In “I counted every dent again,” the word “again” shows that the speaker checks the same marks a second time. Taking that extra time before passing over the sticks makes the speaker sound slow to let go of them. Later, “a whole new room inside it” makes the familiar beat seem bigger and full of new possibilities. The speaker now sounds interested in what the drummer can do with it.

“Crooked” could make you think the speaker stays doubtful. Reread the next few lines. The new beat fills a gap, and soon the speaker’s hands join in. If you chose a different tone word, make sure it fits those later details too.

Take the method to another poem

Next time · Repair Shop

  1. I carried in my clock,
  2. a dead weight in my bag.
  3. On the counter it ticked.
  4. Such a small, stubborn heart.

Before looking back at the method, explain the change in the speaker’s attitude. Use two details from the poem.

Check your later recall

“Dead weight” makes the clock sound like a useless burden. “Small, stubborn heart” makes its ticking sound like something alive that refuses to give up. The speaker shifts from sounding fed up with the clock to sounding fond of it or impressed by it.

Before you go back to your own poem

  • Read the whole poem and find the lines the question names.
  • Name how the speaker sounds before and after the change.
  • Pick two details that help show the change. Explain what each choice makes you hear in the speaker’s voice.

Glossary

Speaker
The voice that speaks in the poem. It can be a voice the poet made up.
Tone
The attitude the voice shows toward a subject. The words can suggest that attitude without naming it.
Tone shift
A change in the attitude we hear within a poem.
Evidence
A detail from the text used to support an idea about it.
