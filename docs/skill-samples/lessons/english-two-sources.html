The writing desk

Build one argument from two readings

Choose a detail from each text. Use them together to explain why something matters, then develop that argument in an essay.

Before you read

Which sentence gives a reason for opening the market at dusk? Explain what specific detail could support that reason.

  1. The article describes a market that opens at dusk.
  2. Opening the market at dusk can make it easier for people on day shifts to shop there.
Check your starting point

The second sentence offers a claim, a point a writer can support. Look for evidence, a specific detail that helps support it, such as what time the shoppers finish their shifts. The first sentence only reports what the article covers.

01 · Read & choose

What is worth keeping?

Essay question Why is it important to keep a record of unsuccessful attempts?

Read both pieces. Before opening the model, choose one detail from each and use them to draft a provisional thesis, the main claim your essay will develop. Jot down what the second reading helps you explain that the first leaves open. Work on paper or in your own document.

Reading A · Reflective essay

The Sheets Behind the Print

By Maya Ferrer

  1. For a long time I kept only the prints I was willing to frame. The others curled into a wastebasket under the worktable. In the drawer I could trace the changes in my taste: the year I preferred thick borders, the winter I printed everything in gray. What I could never find in that tidy record was the moment I changed my mind. Improvement looked so steady there that I began to remember it that way.
  2. One afternoon I pulled six prints from the same carved block. The first had a pale corner. By the sixth, even the finest lines in that corner were dark. I remembered trading my soft work mat for a firmer board, and for weeks I credited the board. Then I found the numbered sheets under a stack of catalogs. Number three still had the pale corner, though I had marked the switch to the board on its back. The corner did not darken until after I moved the block closer to the center of the press.
  3. The clean print could show me where I had arrived, but it could not tell me what I had changed. Even the numbered sheets could not establish which change mattered most. I might have turned the handle differently the last time. Still, they ruled out a story I had already begun to trust. The board alone had left the corner pale that day. If I wanted a better explanation, I could now design a trial in which I moved the block and kept the other conditions as steady as I could.
  4. I keep fewer sheets now than I did in the months when I saved everything. A stack of unmarked paper can be as hard to read as an empty drawer. I keep the attempts whose differences I can describe, along with enough notes to tell one from the next. When I pull out an old print, I want to know what I still have grounds to doubt.

Reading B · Expository article

What a Seed Library Keeps

By Elias Cho

  1. At the Linden Seed Library, the catalog lists varieties that borrowers can take home. A separate trial log records batches that staff and volunteers attempted to grow. Entries include the batch label, the trial dates, and the conditions under which seeds started to grow or failed to start. The catalog answers the immediate question of what is available. The trial log serves people making decisions after the growers who ran the trials have moved on.
  2. Each spring, the library has to decide which older batches to offer. A failed trial can justify another look, but it can have several explanations. Seeds may be too old, or the conditions of the trial may have been unsuitable. A short entry such as “failed” leaves those possibilities tangled. The library asks volunteers to note departures from the usual procedure, even when they seem unrelated to the outcome.
  3. One incoming coordinator nearly discarded a batch of beans after reading that few had sprouted. The notes showed that a second, newer batch had also done poorly on the same shelf and that the warming mat under both trays had been unplugged during the trial. The coordinator scheduled a new trial rather than discarding the older batch. The notes gave someone who had never seen the trays a reason to reconsider an otherwise reasonable decision.
  4. Recording an unsuccessful trial can also keep a small program from spending its limited space on the same test twice. Yet an exact repeat can be worth doing if the first result was surprising or might have happened by chance. A log lets a coordinator distinguish a repeat with a purpose from one that uses a shelf simply because nobody remembers what occupied it last year.
  5. A trial log can complicate the case for buying new seed. When the library orders a replacement, it must decide whether to keep the same variety or spend the money on a different one. Some varieties that did poorly in late outdoor trials had grown well in earlier indoor trials. The library kept those results beside each other so that buyers would weigh the conditions across years before replacing a variety. There was still room to choose a different variety for other reasons, including what borrowers asked for.

All four readings on this page are original practice texts. Authors and organizations in the readings are fictional.

02 · Compare & revise

Give each detail a job

Open the annotated model after choosing your details

First draft. Keeping records helps people learn from mistakes.

That point could fit almost any story about a mistake. Read each detail at three levels. Identify what the text actually says, put its meaning in your own words, then make an inference, a conclusion the wording supports. The inference is where you explain why that detail matters to your thesis.

Revised thesis. Records of unsuccessful attempts matter because they let people test plausible explanations and give later decision-makers enough context to act on what remains uncertain.

From notes to a paragraph

A quotation uses exact words from a text. A paraphrase puts a detail in your own words while keeping its meaning and limits. In both cases, name the source so the reader can follow your reasoning.

A record of an unsuccessful attempt can reopen a decision that seemed settled. In The Sheets Behind the Print, Ferrer reflects, “The clean print could show me where I had arrived, but it could not tell me what I had changed” (par. 3). The numbered sheets showed her that the corner stayed pale after she switched to the firmer board, giving her a reason to test another explanation. Cho’s article shows that those reasons can travel beyond the original observer. The new coordinator scheduled another trial after the log showed that both bean batches had done poorly and that their warming mat had been unplugged (par. 3). That information justified a retest before discarding the older batch. Together, the readings show why recording the conditions of an attempt matters. The record preserves grounds for a better decision even while its outcome is still uncertain.

From one paragraph to an essay

  1. Open with the revised thesis. Introduce the difference between knowing that an attempt failed and knowing what to do next.
  2. Develop the first reason. Use Ferrer’s third sheet to show how records can disprove a plausible explanation. Explain why the next trial can test a more specific question.
  3. Develop the second reason. Use Cho’s example to show how records carry useful context to a new decision-maker. The combined paragraph above could develop that reason by bringing Ferrer’s case back briefly to show what changes when the person deciding was absent.
  4. Close on the consequence. Explain what gets harder when only successful outcomes survive. Keep each paragraph focused on a reason, even when it draws from more than one source.

Try it on your own thesis. Name the role of each detail. If both roles read “shows it helps,” return to the two readings and name the specific decision each record makes possible.

Swap a detail. Does the claim still fit?

Keep Ferrer’s evidence about the board. Try replacing the bean retest with either of the following details. Reread Cho’s full article and trace each detail back to what the log records in paragraph 1. Which detail better supports the specific idea that old trial conditions matter to a new decision? If you choose the other detail, revise the thesis to give it a clear job.

  • Replacement order. The library kept indoor and outdoor results together for varieties that had performed differently under those conditions.
  • Repeat trial. A log helps a coordinator tell a purposeful repeat from one done because no one remembers the last trial.
Compare both choices

Replacement order. This is a strong fit. Buyers can compare results under different conditions before deciding what to order. Cho gives a reported comparison across trials. It gives a reason to weigh the conditions, but it leaves the exact cause and the buyers’ final choice open. Try revising the thesis to make the later decision more specific.

Repeat trial. This is also relevant. Cho explains a principle rather than giving an example of a particular repeat. It better supports an argument about using limited space wisely. Keep the claim about testing explanations, then revise its second part to say that records help teams decide which trials are worth repeating. Preserve Cho’s condition that a repeat can have a purpose.

Keep the source’s limits

Test the sentence “Cho says the coordinator discovered why the older beans failed.” Which words go beyond the text, and how would you revise them?

Compare and retry

“Discovered why” turns a possible explanation into a confirmed cause. Cho reports what the coordinator learned about the trial conditions and what the coordinator did next. A tighter version is “The coordinator found grounds to retest the older beans.” Now check your own paraphrase for a word that changes who acted, when, how often, or how certain the source was.

03 · Write independently

Two new readings, one essay

Essay question Why is it important to have more than one route to a public destination?

Read both pieces and write an essay answering the question. Present and develop a thesis, support it with accurate details from both readings, and explain how those details build your argument. Use correct grammar, spelling, and punctuation. Write privately on paper or in your own document.

Reading C · Reflective essay

The Long Way to the Baths

By Leona Velez

  1. On the map in the station, the bathhouse was a thumb’s width away. The short route climbed a flight of steps built into the hillside. I knew the number of steps because I used to count them before races at the pool, as though the climb were a warm-up designed for me.
  2. After my knee surgery I took the road that curved behind the laundry. It added nine minutes. At first I described that road to friends as my detour, as if it were a temporary correction to the real route. On the curve I passed a father pushing a stroller and a swimmer pulling her oxygen cart. The steps had always been open, but I had mistaken a route that worked for me for a route that worked in general.
  3. In the winter the street crews blocked the foot of the stairs while they replaced a drain. For two weeks the long way served people who could have climbed without a second thought. I had assumed the choice mattered mainly to people whose bodies made the stairs difficult. The drain made me reconsider who depended on the road, and for how long. It also made me wonder what would happen if both routes began at the same blocked corner.
  4. I still use the stairs when I am late and the weather is dry. The road has a crossing that can feel hurried in traffic, and I would never trade the whole stairway for it. What changed was my sense of the distance on the map. A short line tells me very little until I know who is expected to travel it.

Reading D · Expository article

A Park With Two Approaches

By Arjun Bell

  1. The west path into Quarry Park runs beside a creek. The east path approaches over higher ground. In dry weather the east path carries fewer visitors and costs the parks department almost as much to inspect as the west path. On an ordinary week, it can look like an expensive duplicate.
  2. When the creek rose during a spring storm, staff closed the west path and kept the park open from the east. The two paths differed at the point that mattered during the closure: the east path bypassed the flooded bank. Adding a second gate beside the west entrance would have cost less, but both gates would have depended on the same path.
  3. Two routes can offer a practical choice before any closure. The east approach is longer from downtown but shorter from the senior apartments on Lark Street. It also has a gentler slope, although the crushed-stone surface can be difficult to cross after rain. A map that shows only the busier route hides those trade-offs from visitors deciding how to get to the park.
  4. Separate approaches only help if people can find the one that still works. During the closure, a notice at the west entrance directed visitors back to the main road. Staff later added a sign at the earlier junction so walkers could turn before descending to the creek. A second path offers a choice in practice when people have the information to use it.
Review your draft after writing
  1. Underline the thesis. Could a reader tell why the routes matter from that sentence alone?
  2. Mark your evidence by source. Find at least one accurate detail from each reading. Check quoted words against the original, and check whether any paraphrase silently broadens a condition or drops an exception.
  3. Read the sentence after each detail. Does it explain what that detail establishes? Temporarily remove one source. If the argument loses only an example, check whether a different detail could add a reason, a condition, or a consequence.
  4. Retry and edit. Replace one weak detail or revise the thesis to fit the evidence. Read your new sentence aloud to check sentence boundaries, spelling, and punctuation.

Take the method back

On your own assignment, decide whether to quote a phrase or paraphrase the detail. Explain why your choice preserves what matters. For each source, complete this thought in your notes: “Without this reading, my argument would lose…” Use that check before you revise.

Recall tomorrow. Without looking here, explain the difference between adding another example and adding another reason. Use a new pair of short readings to test your explanation.

Glossary

Claim
A point that a writer asks readers to accept and can support with reasoning or evidence.
Evidence
A specific detail that supports a point when the writer explains the connection.
Thesis
The central claim developed across an essay. A provisional thesis is a first version you expect to test.
Inference
A conclusion drawn from details. Its strength depends on what those details allow you to conclude.
Quotation
Exact words taken from a source and identified as that source’s words.
Paraphrase
A restatement of a specific part of a source in your own words, preserving the original meaning and crediting the source.
