Make two sources support one argument

Write a claim that answers a question, then explain what each reading adds to your case.

Which note tells you if a bench will be shady at noon?

One note says a courtyard bench is green. Another says it sits in full sun at noon. Pick the detail that helps you decide whether you’ll need shade.

Check your pick

The sun note answers a question about shade. The paint color could matter for another decision. A detail is useful when it fits the question.

01 · Read for the question

What can this detail help prove?

Evidence is a specific detail you use to support a point. As you read, mark a few details that might help. For each one, jot what it shows and where it came from so you can find it again.

Try it with this question. Should a school test movable shade in its courtyard? The two readings below are invented for practice.

A · Lunch-duty log

1. The wall shaded the west tables during first lunch. By second lunch the shade had moved off the tables.

2. On Tuesday we rolled a canopy, a shade cover on a frame, beside those tables before second lunch. Six students sat there through the period. On Monday those seats had been empty at second lunch.

3. The tables were repainted over the weekend.

B · Facilities walk-through

1. The gate path must stay clear for deliveries. At noon, the shade from a canopy beside the west tables would cover the seating, but two of the canopy’s feet would sit on that path.

2. The north seating bay, an area set aside for tables, is wider. The crew could test a canopy there with all four feet outside the path.

Predict. Which line helps explain why a canopy might be useful? Which line helps decide where to test it? Give a reason for each pick. Switch questions below and see whether the most useful detail changes.

Compare your choice for the need for shade

A1 shows that the same tables lose their shade between lunch periods. A2 adds a clue about use, but comparing two days leaves room for other explanations. A3 says little about the need for shade.

Compare your choice for where to test

B1 identifies a problem with the west location. B2 suggests testing at the north seating bay without putting canopy feet in the path. A2 describes students using shaded seats and leaves the question of access open.

Try the move on a new question

A rink manager asks if the skate-return station needs more room at closing. Notes from a Friday shift show that the rink used 22 cones, the return rack held 12 pairs, and the return line blocked an exit at closing.

Which detail best supports testing a roomier return station?

Compare and retry on paper

The line blocking an exit shows a reason to test a roomier layout. The rack’s capacity could matter if it filled, but the note does not say that. The number of cones does not tell you what happened at the return station.

Retry. If the question changed to what size rack the rink already uses, which detail would now matter most?

Check the retry

Use the 12-pair capacity. The line tells you where people waited, while the rack count answers a question about size.

02 · Give each source a job

Build one case from both readings

A thesis is your essay’s main claim. It should answer the question and make a point the reader can test against the evidence. “These readings discuss the courtyard” names the topic. “The school should test movable shade in the north seating bay to make lunch seating more usable while keeping the gate path clear” takes a position with reasons.

Analysis is the explanation that connects a detail to your point. Try writing what the reader can conclude from the detail and why. A paraphrase restates a detail accurately in your own words. Use a short quotation when the exact words matter. Name the source for either one.

Read a model paragraph and see how it works

The school should test a movable canopy in the north seating bay. The lunch-duty log reports that the west tables lose their shade by second lunch. That timing gives the school a reason to try shade it can place where students need it. The facilities walk-through explains that a canopy at the west tables would put two of its feet on the gate path. The north bay has room for the canopy feet outside that path, so it is a better candidate for the trial.

The paragraph names each source and connects its detail to a specific part of the decision. For a longer essay, you could develop the need and the location in separate paragraphs, then connect them back to the same thesis.

Find the missing link

A community center is considering using a second entrance for a clothing swap. The event log says the check-in line reached the street. A floor plan shows that the second entrance opens into a room with space for both the check-in tables and a line indoors. A draft gives both details and claims the second entrance is worth testing.

Which sentence best explains how the details work together?

Compare and retry on paper

The third sentence turns the log into a reason to act and the plan into a possible way to act. The first goes beyond what the plan can prove. The second gives the facts again without connecting them.

Retry by writing one sentence about why an actual trial would still help.

Check the retry

A trial would show where visitors really wait and whether the new room can handle the crowd.

03 · Draft and revise

Try a new pair on your own

Why is saving usable paper offcuts worthwhile? An offcut is a scrap left after cutting a larger sheet. Use both invented notes to make your case.

A · Supply inventory

On Thursday, the club put paper scraps from the banner project in a tray. The next group made all eight of its small label cards from that tray. It opened no new sheets for the cards.

B · Worktable cleanup note

Clean, flat pieces are easy to sort in the tray. Glue-covered strips stuck to the flat pieces on Friday. Two pieces tore when a club member tried to pull them apart.

Write a thesis and a short paragraph supporting it. Give each source a job. As you revise, choose where you need a more precise detail, a clearer explanation, or a narrower claim. Read your paragraph aloud once to catch missing words and incomplete sentences.

Use this space or write on paper. This page has no draft submission or saving feature.

Compare after drafting

One defensible thesis is “Saving clean, flat paper scraps is worthwhile because those pieces can replace new paper for small jobs.” The inventory’s eight label cards show what “small jobs” means. The torn pieces in the cleanup note give a reason to sort scraps before storing them. A claim about how much paper the club would save in a year would need more records.

Check your draft. Box the thesis, underline a precise detail from each note, then circle the explanation that ties each detail to the claim. If you can only circle a repeat of the underlined words, add what the detail shows and why that matters. Check source names, spelling, and where each sentence ends.

Exit explanation. What would your argument lose if you removed one source? Say what that source adds in a single sentence.

Use this check if both sources seem to say the same thing

Try finishing a separate sentence for each source that starts “This matters because…” If both sentences do the same job, look for a condition, example, or limit that only one source can show.

Take the method back to your reading

  1. Circle the action the prompt asks for. Write a one-sentence answer that takes a position.
  2. Mark a promising detail in each reading. Beside each one, write why it fits. Change your position if your strongest details point to a narrower claim.
  3. Plan paragraphs by reason. For each detail, name the source, explain what the detail shows, and tie it back to the reason. End by explaining what the reasons add up to.
  4. Check that a reader can tell which ideas came from each source and which explanations are yours. Reread for sentence endings, spelling, and punctuation.

Tomorrow, before opening this page, recall the difference between giving evidence and explaining it. Find one sentence in a different article and say what claim it could support. If you get stuck, start by asking what the sentence lets you conclude.

Glossary

Analysis
The explanation of what a detail shows and why it matters for your claim.
Claim
A point you want the reader to accept based on your reasons and evidence.
Evidence
A fact, example, or piece of wording you use to support a point.
Paraphrase
A detail accurately restated in your own words, with its source named.
Quotation
A source’s exact words, marked with quotation marks when used in a sentence.
Thesis
The main claim that the essay develops.
