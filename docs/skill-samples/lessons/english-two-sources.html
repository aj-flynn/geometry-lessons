Reading into writing

Build one argument from two readings

Choose a detail from each reading. Use each detail to explain a different part of the same argument.

Before you start

The workshop stayed open until eight. It should stay open late again.

Which sentence makes a claim someone could disagree with? What would you need to know to support it?

Check your reasoning

The second sentence makes a claim. The first gives a fact about the hours. Knowing how many people used the workshop after six would help you argue whether the extra hours were worth offering.

01Choose

Find two details that do different work

A thesis is the central claim your essay will defend. Evidence is a detail you use to support that claim. First get the plain meaning of the detail right. Then ask what it lets you argue.

Where should a town plant its first shade trees?

The town can afford one round of planting near its bus stops. Read these fictional accounts. Before looking at the map below them, choose one detail from each and jot down a tentative answer. Explain what each detail adds. Find a condition in the readings that might make you narrow your answer.

A · Survey memo

An empty stop can still be hot

Our team mapped the shade at six bus stops on three clear weekdays, visiting each at the start and end of the afternoon rush. The stop by the depot looked like the obvious place to plant. Its concrete pad had virtually no shade all afternoon, and the pavement there registered our highest surface reading.

At the clinic, the pavement was cooler, but riders who missed a bus often waited half an hour in direct sun. At the depot, most riders moved straight from one bus to another. Our maps captured the heat of each location more easily than the length of anyone’s exposure. The clinic deserves a closer look even though it came second in the temperature rankings.

B · Grounds crew column

Room beneath the sidewalk

Residents often ask us for a tree that will grow fast. Last spring our crew replaced two trees that had grown quickly for a few seasons and then begun to decline. Both stood in little wells of soil cut into the pavement. When we dug them up, their roots had doubled back against the walls of those wells.

On Market Lane, we joined several small planting wells by opening a continuous strip of soil. It cost more to dig, but the roots had somewhere to spread. Before promising shade at a particular stop, the town should check for room under the pavement. Buried service lines can rule out a longer planting strip even where a sidewalk looks wide.

“The depot had the hottest pavement” is a true reading of A. It would help rank pavement temperatures. The temperature reading alone leaves open how much time riders spend in the sun. Similarly, B’s Market Lane example shows one way to make room for roots. Its last sentence explains why that fix may be impossible at some stops.

Quick check · A detail can change your claim

A food pantry is considering a second pickup site. Its delivery log reports that the usual truck cannot enter narrow alleys. A map shows that residents of the Ridge apartments have the longest walk to the current site and that several wide streets border the complex. The map was printed last spring.

Which proposal can you defend with the most useful detail from each reading?

Compare after choosing

Look near the Ridge apartments for a site the truck can reach. A search across the whole district ignores who walks farthest. A stop at every entrance assumes the truck can reach each one. The printing date might matter if the streets have changed, but by itself it gives no reason to prioritize one location.

Retry with one change. Suppose the Ridge residents’ walk has shortened since the map was drawn. Which part of the proposal would you now need to support with fresh evidence?

Check the retry

Recheck which residents travel farthest before giving the Ridge area priority. The delivery log still gives a reason to check the truck’s route.

02Develop

Turn the map into an essay

Plan by reasons. A paraphrase restates a specific detail in your own words. Reasoning explains how that detail helps defend your claim. A qualification sets a limit on when the claim holds.

  1. Introduction. The town has limited trees. Argue for starting at stops with long waits in direct sun where trees have room to grow.
  2. Body, first reason. Waiting time helps reveal who stands to benefit. Contrast the clinic’s long waits with the short transfers at the hotter depot. Explain why a single heat measurement can miss riders’ exposure.
  3. Body, second reason. The site needs to sustain a tree. Use the crew’s observations about the confined roots and the service lines. Explain why the clinic is a candidate and what the town still needs to investigate there.
  4. Conclusion. Explain the value of screening for both need and growing conditions before spending the budget. State the next practical step, checking the clinic site against both criteria.

Revise a paragraph that stops at the facts

First draft. The crew dug up two trees that were in little wells. Their roots had folded back. A wide sidewalk can have service lines underneath. These details show that space is important.

That last sentence repeats the topic. To revise, identify the decision the evidence changes. The crew describes what it found under some sidewalks. The memo tells us about a place worth checking. Together they let you make a limited recommendation.

Revised body paragraph

The town should check underground growing space before choosing a stop, even if riders there would benefit from shade. In the grounds crew’s column, the two trees in small planting wells began to decline, and their roots had doubled back against the walls of the wells. Their early growth could make the wells seem adequate for a few seasons. Since the crew also explains that buried service lines can prevent crews from expanding a planting strip, sidewalk width alone cannot tell the town whether a tree can grow there. The clinic’s long waits make it a candidate, as the survey memo suggests, but the town should check below the pavement before committing a tree to the site.

Try the same revision with a new example

A school’s courtyard closes after rain. In the building manager’s log, standing water remained beside a door after the paving had dried elsewhere. An access audit says this door is the only step-free entrance from the courtyard. Improve the last sentence of this argument in your own words. “Water gathers near one of the doors. The audit calls that door the courtyard’s only step-free entrance. These details show that drainage matters.”

Compare your revision

One way to connect them is “Repairing drainage at that door would help keep the courtyard accessible after rain, because students who need a step-free route have no other entrance from the courtyard.” If your sentence just says the water is inconvenient, explain who loses a usable route and why.

Retry by changing the evidence. If the audit finds a second step-free door across the courtyard, rewrite your sentence to keep the inconvenience without claiming all step-free access is lost.

Check the revised claim

You might argue that repairing drainage would restore the shorter step-free route after rain. That version needs evidence that the original route is shorter. With just these details, you can safely argue that the repair would give visitors a second usable step-free entrance after rain.

03Write

Write your own two-source essay

Read the two fictional texts below. Then write an essay arguing why a local museum might show repairs on the objects it exhibits. Develop a thesis, draw on both readings to develop your argument, and use correct grammar, spelling, and punctuation. Write the full essay on paper or in a document you control. Keep it beside the readings as you work.

Reading 1 · Workshop notebook

Under the handle

The delivery satchel arrived folded flat, its brass buckles wrapped in tissue. For the photograph we stuffed it to its original shape. There was a spare satchel in the collection, made to the same pattern and apparently unused. One member of the exhibit team favored showing it instead. Even the maker’s stamp was easier to read on the unused bag.

The photographer asked which side should face the camera. I turned the worn satchel to show the oval of darker leather beneath one handle, where irregular stitches cross a row of machine-made holes. From a distance the marks give the impression of careless workmanship. Under a lamp I could see that the irregular stitches passed through two layers, one of them a patch set against a section of leather worn almost as thin as paper.

Town records list the business that ordered the satchel and the year it closed. The preserved order form specifies the color of the leather and the price per bag. The form would be helpful if we wanted to reconstruct what the company sold. We found no record of what happened to this particular bag after it left the shop.

The idea that we can recover an object’s whole history by returning it to its original appearance is tempting. An object has a history after it is made, however, and some objects survive precisely because they were altered. When I set a small weight in the worn satchel on the workbench, the handle’s pull traveled through the patched area. I could see how the extra layer took some of that strain. I still cannot tell which worker mended it or how much longer the bag lasted.

Another patch, at the bottom, raises a different problem. Its adhesive is newer than the rest of the bag. A volunteer remembered a former curator routinely gluing loose seams, though there is no record that this bag received that treatment. We could show both patches. I would be reluctant to describe them as products of the same period.

For the case we chose a low stand. Visitors can look down far enough to see where the handle bends. The brass buckles reflect beautifully under the case light. We angled the light to the side so that the edge of the leather patch would cast a narrow shadow.

Reading 2 · Museum education bulletin

Two ways through a case

Two people can spend the same amount of time at an exhibit case and leave having done quite different things. Both may be able to name the object. Only one may have stopped to consider the problem its maker or owner faced. It is hard to tell those visits apart by counting people or timing how long they stand at the case.

In a trial display of kitchen utensils, we put out the same cracked wooden spoon on two different weekends. We kept it in the same case and left the dates and place of manufacture on the label. The first weekend, the label also asked visitors to find the two wire loops and trace with their eyes where the crack might spread if either loop came loose. On the second weekend, we replaced that part of the label with a brief description of the wood. Both versions drew visitors to the case, especially when the gallery next door was crowded.

There is a trade-off when a label directs a visitor’s attention. A person reading about the wire might miss the marks left by the tool that carved the bowl. Those marks would be worth pointing out in an exhibit on how utensils were made. We had room for a short label, and we wanted to see what people would take away when it drew their attention to an alteration made after the spoon left the workshop.

At the end of each visit, staff asked people to sketch one thing they remembered about the spoon. Several visitors from the first weekend drew the loops with the crack between them or included arrows that showed how the loops held the wood together. Most drawings from the second weekend showed the outline of the bowl and handle. The drawings were voluntary, and the two weekend groups may have come in with different interests. One family from the second weekend had brought a guidebook about antique utensils.

We tried a similar label beside a lantern whose soldered seam ran underneath its base. Few visitors could find the seam through the glass. We added a close-up image to show where the joining metal spread across the break. Afterward, a visitor pointed from the image to the dent in the lantern’s side and asked whether the dent and the break could have happened at the same time. The staff could explain why both marks were visible and why their order remained uncertain.

Use this only after a first attempt
  1. Underline your thesis. If it could describe any museum display, state what showing repairs adds. Find a phrase that makes your claim too broad and replace it with wording you can defend.
  2. Box a detail from each reading. Can you find each detail in the text as you wrote it? If one is just a topic or a summary of the whole reading, substitute a specific observation and identify its source.
  3. Circle the explanation after each detail. Cover each detail in turn. If your argument stays just as strong, explain what that detail supports that the other does not.
  4. Check limits and proofread. Mark each place you claim certainty, name a cause, or say “always.” Verify that the source justifies that strength. Check sentence boundaries, spelling, and punctuation. Read a revised paragraph back against your thesis.
Compare one possible evidence route

In Reading 1, stitches that hold a patch cross earlier machine holes, and the patch takes some of the handle’s pull. This suggests a later effort to keep the bag useful. The patch supplies a clue about use that the order form can’t give. In Reading 2, some visitors’ sketches show how the loops hold the spoon together after the label draws attention to the repair. These visitors used the repair to reconstruct a practical problem. Together the details can support showing carefully described repairs in ways visitors can see and interpret. Leave room for what the texts cannot establish, such as when the lower patch was added or how much the label alone changed what people drew.

Try a different route in your revision. Use the lantern and one precise detail about the satchel to explain when showing an object alone would leave visitors with too little evidence.

Take the method with you

What would you fix first if both readings appear in your essay but your reasons sound the same? Explain why you would change the evidence, the explanations, or the thesis.

When you return to a two-reading assignment, close this page and recall three questions. What is my claim? What does each reading contribute? Where does my claim need a limit? Use the questions to check your own plan privately.

Glossary

Evidence
A specific detail that supports a claim.
Paraphrase
A faithful restatement of a specific detail in your own words.
Qualification
A word, phrase, or condition that narrows a claim to what its evidence can support.
Reasoning
An explanation of how a detail supports the claim.
Thesis
The central claim an essay develops and defends.
