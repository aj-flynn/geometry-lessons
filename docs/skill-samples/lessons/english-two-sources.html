Before you read

A sports center installed a bottle filler and counted 60 refills on its opening day. Which claim (a position you can defend) does that count support?

  1. The filler was used repeatedly on opening day.
  2. The center now throws away fewer plastic bottles every week.

Pick one and name a piece of information the other claim would need. Write your thinking in a notebook.

Check this and the other short attempts in Short-check comparisons at the end.

Check what the count establishes

The first claim fits the 60 refills. To defend the second, the writer would need weekly bottle-waste counts from before and after installation. If you chose the second claim, circle its words fewer and every week, then ask what the opening-day count can tell you about either.

01 · Read and decide

What is repair worth?

Read both texts. Pick one detail from each to use as evidence (support for your claim). Jot down a first answer. Why is it worth knowing how to repair things?

After reading both, write what the second text adds that the first could not establish on its own. Use a notebook or a private document for your responses.

Both readings are original practice texts. The people, places, and incidents are fictional.

Reading A · Memoir excerpt

At the Back of the Bench

By Inez Mora

  1. My uncle used to keep a row of crooked objects at the back of his workbench. I remember a lamp whose shade leaned toward the wall and a picture frame shaped almost like a parallelogram. The lamp came on whenever he wanted it to. The frame held a photograph of his brother. Their defects interested me more than their service did. I wanted them to look new.
  2. Years later, at a theater where I built scenery, we had a small rolling platform that refused to run straight. I was about to order a new wheel when our shop lead set a ruler against the wooden base. The wheel was fine. Its bracket had been screwed into a split in the wood, and the split widened whenever we put weight on the platform. An expensive set of wheels would have taken us straight back to the same problem.
  3. I expected the shop lead to fill the crack. Instead, she removed the platform from use and showed me how to inspect the rest of the frame. The sound boards came off and went into another build. The damaged section went into the scrap bin. There was no ceremony. What impressed me was how neatly she separated two judgments I had been making as one. The platform had failed. Much of the wood still had work to do.
  4. Making a repair begins with a guess about what has stopped doing its job. Testing that guess is slow, and sometimes the answer is inconvenient. There are failures I leave to someone with training, especially when a wrong guess could hurt a person. There are tools I replace because their parts are no longer made. It would be wasteful to make a virtue of keeping everything.
  5. I have grown less interested in whether the objects at the back of the bench look finished. A surface can be tidy and conceal a weakness. A scratch can be deep enough to catch a fingernail and mean very little. The skill I wish I had learned earlier is how to tell a change in appearance from a change in what an object can do.
  6. When I fix a drawer now, I take its contents out before I put any pressure on it. Then I try to see where it catches. It is a small habit, and it saves me from my old habit of forcing everything. More often than I expected, I find I can keep the thing and change a small part of it.

Reading B · Reported feature

The Saturday Fix Table

By Felix Adeyemi

  1. The repair table at the Linden Row library opens at ten on Saturdays. Last month, a patron arrived early carrying a floor fan in a laundry bag. Behind him, someone set down a box of curtains with torn hems. Above the volunteer sign-in sheet hangs a photograph of the original team, posing with a blue metal toolbox.
  2. The table started as a drop-off service. Volunteers fixed what they could while owners did their shopping. At closing time, coordinator Nella Hart says, people would collect their things and hurry away. A month later, some returned with the same kind of failure. Hart started putting a second chair at each station. The chair was for the person who owned the object.
  3. On the morning I visited, a volunteer showed a man how to tell which threads were holding his backpack strap in place. He practiced on a scrap before sewing his own strap. He came back two Saturdays later with a length of webbing he had stitched to a second bag at home. The repair was rough, but he said it held the books he carried to work.
  4. Hart keeps a register of jobs. In April, volunteers helped with fourteen torn hems, split seams, and loose buttons. Most went home usable that day. Three people came back for advice about something they had started at home. Hart records visits and repairs, so the register cannot show how many replacements the project has prevented. She also says it matters whether a person has a needle, thread, and somewhere to sit down and use them.
  5. When I asked about the fan, Hart pointed to the service rules. Volunteers could clean accessible parts with the fan unplugged, but they could not work on wiring. The fan's plug had heat damage, so the owner left with a referral to a qualified technician. The rules keep the table from filling up with jobs that need equipment the library cannot provide.
  6. Near closing, the man with the backpack appeared with a spare pack of needles. The next owner to sit beside a volunteer was trying to repair a shopping bag. Hart slid the packet down the table toward her.

02 · Compare and revise

Make each detail do a job

Compare the repair argument after trying

From a first claim to a thesis

“Repair saves things” points in a direction, but it leaves out what a person must know to decide what is worth saving. If you paraphrase a detail (restate it in your own words), name the source and keep its meaning and limits.

Read a short model essay

Introduction and thesis. When an object breaks, what stopped working? What is still useful? Repair skills matter because a careful diagnosis can save usable parts, and learning a manageable fix can help people keep other objects in service later.

Diagnosing the problem. In At the Back of the Bench, Inez Mora recalls wanting to replace a wheel on a platform before the shop lead finds the split wood beneath the bracket (par. 2). After taking the platform out of service, the lead reuses the sound boards (par. 3). A new wheel would have cost money and left the widening split in place. Inspecting the frame lets the lead keep usable material while removing the part that can no longer carry weight.

Extending the benefit. Mora’s example shows what can be recovered from one failed object. Felix Adeyemi’s The Saturday Fix Table shows how learning a technique can help an owner care for another object later. After working with a volunteer on one backpack, a patron stitches webbing to a second bag at home and reports that it carries his books (par. 3). The value goes beyond a single visit because the patron now has a way to maintain another bag. That result still depends on having supplies and choosing safe work. At the same table, a fan with a damaged plug is referred to a technician (pars. 4–5).

Conclusion. Knowing how to diagnose wear and carry out manageable repairs gives people more control over what they can keep. Sometimes that control means knowing where to stop and what can still be saved.

The body paragraphs each develop a different reason that fits the thesis. If two possible reasons compete, choose the one the readings support better, or explain when each applies. Look back at your draft claim. If a detail only repeats a point the other reading already supports, replace it or explain what new example, consequence, or condition it brings. Rewrite your claim to make room for that contribution.

Reading C · Nonfiction book excerpt

The Other Side of the Blue Line

By Mira Okafor

  1. The first map I bought with my own money hung above my bed for six years. It showed the Bluefin River and the streets that fed into it. I liked that the print was small enough to hide details from across the room. At night the map turned into a pattern of blue and brown, and the river looked as solid as a plank.
  2. I carried a copy of that map when I took a summer job inspecting culverts, the pipes or channels that let water pass under roads. We followed a thin brown line until it ended at a chain and a sign for a closed quarry. On the map, the line was a street. In the heat that afternoon, it was a strip of grass and stones behind a gate. I thought the mapmaker had made a mistake.
  3. My supervisor showed me the small type beneath the title. The city had made the map to record where it owned strips of land for roads. Some strips were still waiting for roads to be built. Others, including the one at the quarry, held routes that had fallen out of use. The thin line had told us something true about the city’s records. We had taken it to say something about whether our truck could get through.
  4. There is a temptation to think that a map is a little piece of the world. It may be closer to a record of questions someone asked about the world. Is that ground public? Will rain run downhill toward this house? Where can a person cross without paying? The map begins by giving certain facts the right to appear. The rest can seem to vanish, even if they matter a great deal to the person who stands there.
  5. A few years after that summer, a shop owner at the quarry drew another map for me on the back of a receipt. It showed two routes to the river, neither marked on my road map. One ran across a yard with the owner’s permission. The other wound past a shed where a dog spent the afternoon. The sketch could not tell me where the city’s property ended, but it helped me choose a way down to the water.
  6. At city meetings I still see somebody put a finger on a map and say there is nothing here. Sometimes they mean there are no buildings. Sometimes they mean the place has no entry in the category the map was made to show. Once, a gray space on a property map was used to propose a spot for equipment storage. A parks worker who recognized the corner asked the group to look at the footpath map. A route used by walkers crossed the proposed site.
  7. I used to blame such mistakes on the size of the print. Give the reader a bigger key, I thought, and the problem goes away. But people at that meeting could read the property map perfectly. They knew what each kind of line meant. What they had not noticed was that a blank space on one map can contain the very thing another map was designed to show.
  8. Sometimes leaving a thing unmarked is a decision I would defend. A map for volunteers counting a rare plant might give them a rough area to search. A map with dots precise enough to find every plant could also guide someone who wanted to dig one up. There is a difference between choosing to conceal a location and forgetting that a place has users. A blank patch alone gives me little basis for deciding which has happened.
  9. Even a map made for a familiar purpose can change the way a person imagines a place. One winter a neighbor showed me two maps of the same bus system. One arranged the routes roughly in the shape of the city. The other drew the routes as straight lines and gave every stop the same space. I found transfers faster on the second sheet. I also began to think of the next stop as close enough to walk, even where there was a long stretch with no sidewalk.
  10. Which bus map was more accurate? If I wanted to know whether the green route crossed the red one, I would put my finger on the second sheet. If I needed to know the distance between two streets, I would look for a scale or use a map that showed distances. A thing can be drawn badly for a question it was never meant to answer. It can also be drawn badly for the very job it claims to do.
  11. When the river rose the following spring, the city mailed a map showing the predicted reach of floodwater. I found my building just outside the shaded area. A neighbor a little farther uphill was inside it. She had already brought her books upstairs. I had done nothing. We had both missed a line of print saying that the shading showed what could happen under one set of conditions.
  12. The line was useful as far as it went. My neighbor's basement sat beside a low road, which explained why the shaded shape could reach uphill. My basement stayed dry that year. Neither of those observations makes the border a promise about the next storm. The map helped me see a way the water could travel. It could not tell me where the next storm would start or how long it would last.
  13. That map taught me to be suspicious of my own wish for a yes or no. A clean edge on a page is easier to live with than a changing river. Sometimes the people who make maps need a clear edge so they can decide where to send crews first. A homeowner reading that same edge has a different question. She might want to know whether there is enough uncertainty to move the boxes upstairs anyway.
  14. I now start with the title and look for the smallest words near it. Then I try to say, in a whole sentence, what a line or a patch of shading tells me. That sentence is usually longer than I expect. It may include a date, a purpose, or an assumption about a storm. If I am still wondering about the gate at the end of the road, I need to check something beyond the page.
  15. A good map has to leave things out. If it tried to answer every question, it would be as difficult to read as the place itself. The old road map is still on my wall. When I take it down, I unfold it from the side with the title.

Reading D · Reported feature

A Walk From Exchange Square

By Cole Reyes

  1. At 7:20 on a Thursday, Anika Ross unfolded a city walking map under the clock at Exchange Square. An orange route curved behind the old market and reached a health clinic in about eight minutes. Ross, who uses a cane, checked the little box beside the map title. The time applied to a walk at an average pace. It gave no estimate of how long a person might spend waiting to cross a street.
  2. The route behind the market ended at a set of stairs. Ross knew that before leaving the square. What she wanted to find out was whether the two other routes would be any easier for a neighbor making the same trip. The city was testing a map with different symbols for steps, curb ramps, and places to rest, and Ross was part of the group checking it.
  3. At Finch Street, a curb ramp appeared as a small circle on the test map. The circle was accurate. The ramp was there, but a market delivery van blocked it. Ross reached the next corner and saw that its ramp was open. A teammate who had arrived earlier had found both ramps clear. In the notebook she carried inside her bright yellow vest, Ross wrote down the time of the delivery.
  4. The team considered removing the first ramp from the map. Instead, they kept its symbol and added a note about delivery hours to the next version. A single visit could not show how often the curb was blocked, the group’s organizer said. The market manager confirmed that delivery vehicles usually arrived before eight on weekdays and that arrival times varied.
  5. At the clinic, Ross met a man who planned his trips around benches. A longer route on the draft map included two seats under cover, though it crossed more streets. For him the rests mattered more than the walking time. A clinic employee preferred the market route because she could take the steps and wanted to arrive before her shift. The clinic now pins up a copy with a box explaining the symbols and how the times were estimated.
  6. The walk was one part of the project. A month later, the organizers reviewed notes from different days and sent the transportation department a list of crossings where a mapped ramp was hard to use. The department moved the loading sign at Finch Street. It left the other sites on its inspection list because the reports there differed.
  7. In the city's records I found a copy of the first walking map, printed when the market sold mainly flowers. It folded down to the size of a train ticket. A drawing of the station roof took up most of the cover. Back then the market deliveries took place on the far side of the building. The road on the map has not moved, but the work people do along it has.
  8. As the test continued, the clinic tried a small comparison. On two mornings staff asked 24 visitors to use one of the two maps to choose a route for someone who needed to avoid steps. Half used the old map. Half used the new draft. Nine of the twelve people with the draft picked a route that avoided stairs, compared with five of the twelve using the older map. The staff gave everyone the same starting point.
  9. Nobody in that exercise had to walk the route. Clinic manager Jo Egan said it measured whether people could recognize a suitable route on paper. The group was small, and some visitors already knew the square well. The numbers gave Egan a reason to keep the symbols, but the clinic could not use them to estimate how many people would reach an appointment on time.
  10. One participant had spotted the step-free route on the draft but chose a different path when asked what he would do himself. He used a power chair and said the clinic entrance on the map was sometimes locked. The map team had focused on the street. His comment sent an organizer to try both clinic doors at opening time. A path can take a person to a building without taking them to a usable entrance.
  11. Other comments were harder to act on. A business owner asked the team to draw every shop's front door. He argued that the detail would save newcomers time. An organizer spread a crowded draft on the counter and asked him to trace the route from the square. He had to follow it with a finger to keep it separate from the shop symbols. The final draft marks entrances for the clinic and other public buildings in this area.
  12. A first-time visitor could start with the date and the box explaining the symbols, Egan said. If a detail matters enough to change the route, such as a working lift or an unlocked entrance, she suggests checking with the destination. Ross showed me why the same advice can be less useful to someone standing in the rain with a dying phone. A short note printed next to the symbol can save a call that the traveler may have no way to make.
  13. One transportation employee worried that the notes would go out of date before the city could print the next edition. She proposed an online layer that staff could update. Ross wanted the print edition to show where the information came from and when it was checked. The group used both approaches in a later draft. A printed date helps readers decide how much to rely on a note, while an online update only helps people who can reach it.
  14. The clinic still hears from visitors who have trouble getting from the square to its entrance. Egan now asks which map the visitor used and which part of the trip was difficult. Some problems, like finding a step-free route, can be addressed on paper. Others, like a blocked curb, require work on the street. What looks like the same complaint at the desk can lead to very different changes.
  15. The map on the clinic wall still gives the market route the shortest time. The footnote about pace is now easier to find. Ross keeps a folded copy near her door so she can see which sections will need a closer look the next time she walks over.

Review your draft

Check your essay after drafting
  1. Underline the thesis. Does it give a reason the skill matters? If it mainly states the topic, name what someone can do with the skill and why that outcome matters.
  2. Label each piece of evidence with its reading. Check any paraphrase for who did what and for limits on timing or certainty. Copy quotations exactly and identify the author or title.
  3. Find the reasoning that comes after each detail. If a sentence only repeats the source, explain what that detail lets the reader conclude. Read the reasons together. Can you name what each reading adds?
  4. Read the essay aloud once for complete sentences. Then check spelling, punctuation, and any quotation marks against the originals on this page.

One defensible approach links the proposed storage site in Reading C (par. 6) to the delivery blockage in Reading D (pars. 3–4). The first detail shows why consulting a second kind of map can reveal a competing use of the same space. The second shows why interpreting an accurate symbol still involves thinking about time and checking conditions. Together they can support an argument about preventing costly or unsafe choices. A claim about choosing a route for a particular traveler could instead draw on C’s sketch (par. 5) and the clinic visitors’ different needs in D (par. 5). Glossary

Claim
A position a writer asks a reader to accept and can defend with reasons.
Evidence
A specific detail that supports or tests a claim when the writer explains the connection.
Thesis
The central claim developed across an essay.
Qualification
A condition or limit that narrows what a claim promises.
Paraphrase
A restatement in your own words that preserves the source’s meaning and identifies where it came from.
