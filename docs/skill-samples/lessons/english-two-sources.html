Build one argument from two readings

Make a case for why a skill matters. Choose details from both readings, then show what you can argue with both in view.

Read and decide Study the model Write your essay Glossary

01 / First reading

What makes repair skills worth learning?

Quick start. “A shop keeps spare bicycle tires” gives a fact. What could you argue about why keeping them matters? Say a sentence that goes beyond the fact.

Compare if you’re stuck

One possibility: “Keeping spare tires can get riders back on the road sooner.” That point gives a reason the fact matters.

Read these two original pieces. On paper, pick one detail from each as evidence (a detail that helps support your point). Write a first claim (a point you make and defend) about why repair skills matter. Then ask: what does your second detail add or change?

A / Reflective essay

Know What Still Works

By Ada Jory

1 The sewing machine on my kitchen table had belonged to my aunt. It arrived with a leather case heavy enough to bruise a shin and with a bright sticker that said DO NOT OIL. When the wheel began to catch, I mistook the noise for a verdict. A machine that had survived four decades, I thought, had earned the right to stop.

2 A repair teacher showed me how to turn the wheel with the power off and watch where the resistance began. A bent needle was brushing against the plate; the motor and belt were fine. Once I knew where to look, the noise became a clue instead of a verdict. Replacing the machine would have solved my immediate problem. It would also have hidden how little of the machine had actually failed.

3 I still take electrical faults to a technician, and some things simply wear out. But I no longer read a defect as an instruction to shop. Even when I can’t make a repair myself, I can often describe the fault well enough to ask whether the object is worth fixing. That small shift matters: whoever calls something finished gets to decide what happens to it next.

B / Reported article

Saturday in the Parts Room

By Leon Vale

1 At a neighborhood fix-it workshop, an umbrella dried upside down in a mop bucket. The workshop’s waiting shelf held a handcart, two electric fans, and a toaster. No one was allowed to plug in a damaged appliance before a volunteer checked it.

2 The handcart belonged to a produce vendor. She could show exactly where its wheel rubbed against the frame, but the maker no longer stocked its axle. A volunteer measured the axle and found a matching one on a damaged cart in the parts bin. After the vendor fitted it under supervision, she rolled the cart across the floor with two crates on it. She used the cart at her stall the next morning.

3 The two fan owners went home with their fans still broken: the workshop had no matching switches. One owner had paid bus fare to get there. For the workshop’s coordinator, knowing what to fix was only half the problem; a replacement part had to be within reach. This spring she began collecting parts from equipment the workshop couldn’t save.

Pause here. Commit to a first claim and two paragraph numbers before opening the model. If both details do the same job, see whether another detail adds a consequence, a condition or a limit.

02 / See the choices

Give each reading a job

Open the model after your first attempt

First claim: Repair skills matter because they help people tell what is actually broken. That answers “why,” but it stops once the fault is identified. I want the second reading to tell me whether that judgment can change what happens next.

Why these details? The sticker is vivid, but it tells us little about Jory’s decision. Jory’s choice to call a technician in paragraph 3 would help a different argument about knowing when to seek help. The vendor’s success alone would miss the limit shown by the fans. Choose for the point you’re making.

Revised thesis (the essay’s main claim): Repair skills matter because they help people judge whether replacement is necessary and, when parts and help are available, keep useful things in service.

Read one body paragraph

Diagnosing a fault can make repair possible, even when it takes more than skill to finish the job. In “Know What Still Works,” Ada Jory says “the noise became a clue instead of a verdict” after locating a bent needle. Her change in judgment matters because a single faulty part no longer seems like a reason to replace the whole machine. Leon Vale’s article “Saturday in the Parts Room” shows how identifying a specific fault can pay off. A vendor identifies where her cart’s wheel rubs, but she gets the cart back into service only after the workshop finds a compatible axle and helps her fit it. Together, these examples show why learning to diagnose a fault is valuable: it can open a route to continued use, even if the repair also depends on resources the owner doesn’t have.

How the paragraph works: The opening sentence makes a point. The writer attributes the evidence (names where it came from), quotes Jory’s key wording and paraphrases Vale’s example (restates it in new words). The first underlined sentence explains Jory’s evidence; the second connects both readings to a reason repair skills matter. That connection is synthesis: using sources together to build a point.

Try the removal test. Without Jory you lose the contrast between guessing and diagnosing. Without Vale you lose the concrete case where a diagnosis led to reuse, and the condition that made reuse possible. The readings can support each other while doing different work.

Scale up to an essay. State your main claim up front. Let each body paragraph develop a reason or take the reasoning a step further. For this example, one paragraph could show why judging a defect matters; the next could explain what it takes to turn that judgment into continued use. Use both sources across the essay where they advance the reasoning. End by showing what’s at stake in the choice, such as when to replace something useful.

Test a different pairing

New claim: Repair skills include recognizing when to stop and seek help. Return to Jory and Vale. Which pair most directly helps defend this claim? Say how each detail helps before you check.

Choose a pair of details

Read the comparison after trying

Jory hands electrical work over to a technician. Vale’s vendor does some work herself with a volunteer’s help. The pair shows two different ways to act on a limit. The fan outcomes and the parts collection show material limits; neither shows a person deciding to rely on expertise as directly.

Retry in a sentence: Use either of the other pairs to defend a different reason repair skills matter. Point to the detail that does the most work. If the second detail still feels tacked on, exchange it for another.

03 / Independent writing

Make your own case

Write an essay arguing why keeping accurate field records matters. Develop a central claim and use information from both readings below. Write on paper or in your own private document.

C / Reflective essay

What the Ledger Leaves Out

By Sera Quill

1 In my first week measuring the marsh, I recorded two water levels that differed by almost a handspan. I circled the higher number in red. Only later did I discover that I had made one visit at low tide and the other near the end of the incoming tide. The water had done something ordinary; my notebook had made it look unusual.

2 I began writing down the time, the reference mark I measured from, and any change in the way I took a reading. At first the extra lines felt like an admission that I didn’t trust my own memory. Now I think of them as a way to let a stranger borrow my afternoon. A new observer could put a ruler against the same mark and tell me whether we were seeing the same thing.

3 The notebook has a soaked corner and several pages I wish I could do over. Some dates simply say “access road flooded.” Those blanks can’t tell me how high the water got. But a marked blank does tell me where the evidence stops. An estimated number there would look more useful, right up to the moment someone tried to build a conclusion on it.

D / Reported article

After the Lines Moved

By Niko Hart

1 For six summers a trail crew painted thin white lines across cracks in a stone overlook. Workers photographed each line with a ruler beside it, then saved the date and the location. Visitors rarely noticed the marks; from a distance, they resembled streaks of bird droppings.

2 Last spring a new crew compared its pictures with the oldest ones. The rock at the outer railing had separated farther than the first photographs showed. At a nearby crack, a broken paint stripe turned out to be peeling paint: the distance between two reference marks in the photographs had barely changed. The crew shut the outer railing and asked an engineer to inspect the site. It left the inland trail open.

3 The engineer used the pictures to choose which part of the rock to examine first. The record could show where the surface had changed, she said. It couldn’t show how deep the separation ran or predict when the stone might fall. She asked for a closer survey before the overlook reopened.

Get unstuck after attempting a plan

For each detail you selected, write what it literally says before you write why it matters. Put a mark beside any word that limits what you can infer. Ask what someone could decide with that information and what they would still need to find out.

Compare your reasoning after drafting

After learning that she measured at different points in the tide cycle, Quill realizes that the difference between her recorded water levels was ordinary. Her notes about how she measured give a new observer a way to check her work. Hart’s crew uses a record created by earlier workers to distinguish two changes and take action at one site. That supports a reason to keep records: people can use earlier observations to make better judgments later. Neither piece says accurate records guarantee a correct conclusion. Quill marks a gap, and Hart reports that an inspection is still needed.

If you reached a different defensible claim, check it against the details you picked. If your draft says the records alone proved the overlook was unsafe, distinguish what they showed from what the engineer still had to investigate. If your draft just reports two events, add a sentence that explains the shared reason they make record keeping valuable.

Check before you finish

  • Defend a reason. Underline the part of your thesis that answers “why does it matter?” If you only named the topic, revise that sentence.
  • Give both readings work to do. Draw an arrow from each selected detail to the point it supports. If an arrow needs explaining, put that explanation into the essay.
  • Keep the wording honest. Read each quoted phrase against the original. For each paraphrase, verify who did what and keep any condition, uncertainty or negation that changes the meaning.
  • Read for clarity. Check that each sentence is complete and that pronouns such as “it” and “they” have clear referents. Check spelling, author names, and punctuation around quotations.

Take the method back to your own readings

Decide whether your second reading confirms, extends or limits the first reason you found. Explain why that relationship matters for the claim you want to defend. If the connection only sounds like “they both discuss the topic,” try a different detail or narrow your claim.

Next time, before reopening this page: Recall the removal test. What would disappear from an argument if you took one reading out? Try it on a new pair and explain why your evidence belongs.

Glossary

Claim
A point you make and defend with evidence and reasoning.
Evidence
A quotation, fact or detail from a source that helps support a claim.
Thesis
The main claim an essay develops and supports.
Attribute
Identify who wrote, said or reported a detail so the reader can tell where it came from.
Paraphrase
Restate a specific idea in your own words without changing its meaning. Give its source.
Synthesis
Connect ideas from more than one source to reach or strengthen a point.

All four readings were written for this lesson. The bylines, people and events are fictional.
