Geometry · Grade 3

Around the edge

Find how far it is around a shape, even when some side lengths are missing. Learn when you need to count squares instead.

  • Trace the edge
  • Use matching sides
  • Find missing lengths
  • Count the inside
  • Try a mix
  • Glossary

01 · Follow the line

One trip around

Imagine a robot rolling on the edge of a mat. The perimeter is the distance all the way around that edge. Start at a corner. Follow each side once until you get back to where you started.

Quick start. On a grid, moving from one dot to the next is one step. Start at a dot and move right three steps. Did you pass along three short lines or four?
Check the quick start

Three lines. You can touch four dots, including the start. Count the spaces you travel between dots.

Test the robot’s route

Predict: How many unit steps will the robot travel? Will it count any squares? Use the picture before moving the robot.

At the start. Total: 0 units.

Compare your prediction

Follow the turns: right 4, down 1, left 2, down 2, left 2, up 3. The last stretch matters: it brings the robot back to the start.

4 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 14

The perimeter is 14 units. The robot counts lengths along lines, not squares.

Try it: a route that stops early

A cart goes right 2 units, down 1 unit, then right 4 units. It stops there. How far did it go? Is that a perimeter?

Compare or retry

2 + 1 + 4 = 7. That is 7 units of travel. The cart never returns to its start. If it went 2 units right, 1 down, then only 3 right, what would change?

Check the retry

One unit less: 2 + 1 + 3 = 6. The route still ends away from its start.

02 · Find what repeats

A rectangle has two pairs

Opposite sides are across from each other. In a rectangle, opposite sides match in length: top matches bottom, and left matches right. A blank label does not mean a side has no length.

Seal the nameplate

Given: A rectangle with sides of 11 cm and 3 cm that meet at a corner.

Find: The length of seal needed around the edge, with no gap or overlap.

Work: Copy each length to its opposite side. Add the four lengths. Here the numbers are all in centimeters.

11 + 3 + 11 + 3 = 14 + 14 = 28

The seal needs to be 28 cm long. Each side of an equals sign (=) has the same value. Here both sums equal 28.

A square is a rectangle with four equal sides. You can add the same length four times or multiply that length by 4. The sign × means multiply: 4 × 5 means four groups of five.

Try it: do the edges match?

A square has sides of 5 meters. A different rectangle has sides of 7 meters and 3 meters. Can one piece of cord fit exactly around either shape? Find the perimeter of each before you decide.

Compare or retry

For the square, 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 4 × 5 = 20. For the other rectangle, 7 + 3 + 7 + 3 = 20. Both perimeters are 20 meters, so a 20-meter cord fits either one with no overlap. If you got 10 meters for the rectangle, you counted only two of the four sides.

Retry with a 6-meter square and a rectangle that is 10 meters by 2 meters. Will one cord fit both?

Check the retry

Yes. Each perimeter is 24 meters: four groups of 6 or two groups of 12.

03 · Work back from the total

What part is missing?

When you know the perimeter, you know the whole length around. The side lengths are its parts. Mark the known sides first. Then ask whether the part left over is one side or is shared by equal sides.

One side is missing

Given: A triangle has perimeter 22 cm. Two sides measure 7 cm and 9 cm.

Find: The third side.

Work: Add the known lengths. Subtract that sum from the whole perimeter. All lengths here are in centimeters.

  1. 7 + 9 = 16Two known sides together
  2. 22 − 16 = 6One side left

Check: 7 + 9 + 6 = 22. The 6 cm side makes the given total.

Try it: spot the wrong total

A three-sided sign has sides of 10 cm, 14 cm, and a missing length. Its perimeter is 33 cm. Someone wrote 24 cm on the missing side. What does 24 cm actually measure?

Compare or retry

24 cm is the total for the two known sides: 10 + 14 = 24. It is not what’s left. Subtract: 33 − 24 = 9. Check that 10 + 14 + 9 = 33.

Retry: the sign’s perimeter is 35 cm instead. The two known sides stay the same. What is the missing length now?

Check the retry

35 − 24 = 11, so the missing side is 11 cm.

Two missing sides match

Given: Two matching corner pieces snap together to make a rectangle. The two arms on each piece measure 17 mm in all. The short arm is 6 mm.

Find: The lengths of all four sides.

Work: Find the long arm of one piece. The other piece has the same lengths. All lengths here are in millimeters.

17 − 6 = 11

Check: The sides are 6 mm, 11 mm, 6 mm, and 11 mm. They make a perimeter of 34 mm because 6 + 11 + 6 + 11 = 34.

What if you start with the whole perimeter?

For this same rectangle, start with 34 mm and take out both short sides. The two equal sides share what’s left. The sign ÷ means divide, here to split into equal parts.

  1. 6 + 6 = 12Both short sides
  2. 34 − 12 = 22Both long sides
  3. 22 ÷ 2 = 11One long side

Try it: read a shape maker’s note

For a rectangle, the top and bottom together measure 30 cm. All four sides together measure 38 cm. How long is the left side?

Compare or retry

38 − 30 = 8 and 8 ÷ 2 = 4. The left and right sides measure 8 cm together, so each measures 4 cm. Now try a perimeter of 44 cm with the same top-and-bottom total.

Check the retry

44 − 30 = 14 and 14 ÷ 2 = 7. Each remaining side is 7 cm.

All the sides match

Given: An eight-sided game board has eight equal sides and a perimeter of 48 cm.

Find: The length of one side.

Work: Share the whole perimeter among eight sides. Each gets the same length.

48 ÷ 8 = 6

Check: One side is 6 cm. Eight copies make the whole length because 8 × 6 = 48.

Try it: choose a set of sticks

Build a triangle with three equal sides and a perimeter of 21 cm. You have sets of 3 cm, 7 cm, and 10 cm sticks. Use one set only. What length works? How can you prove it with multiplication?

Compare or retry

Three 7 cm sticks work: 3 × 7 = 21. The 3 cm sticks make only 9 cm, and the 10 cm sticks make 30 cm. If the only sticks available were 10 cm each, what perimeter could your triangle have?

Check the retry

30 cm. Three equal lengths of 10 cm make 30 cm altogether.

04 · Edge or inside?

Same shape, two things to measure

To put tape around an edge, measure its perimeter. To cover the inside, measure its area. Area tells how much space a flat shape covers.

Given: A label measures 6 cm by 4 cm.

Find: The length of tape for its edge and the area to cover with ink.

Edge · tape

Follow the four sides. Add lengths in centimeters.

6 + 4 + 6 + 4 = 20

20 cm of tape

Inside · ink

Count the squares. There are four rows of six squares.

4 × 6 = 24

24 square cm of space

Try it: compare two signs

One sign has 2 rows of 7 unit squares. The other has 3 rows of 6. Each is a rectangle. Which takes more paint to cover, if both get the same coat of paint? Would the same length of tape go around each?

Compare or retry

Compare the insides for paint: 2 × 7 = 14 and 3 × 6 = 18. The second sign covers more square units. Compare the edges for tape: 7 + 2 + 7 + 2 = 18 and 6 + 3 + 6 + 3 = 18. Both take 18 units of tape. An area of 18 square units measures something different from 18 units of length.

Retry: change the second sign to 3 rows of 5 squares. Compare paint and tape again.

Check the retry

The new sign still uses more paint: 15 square units is more than 14. It uses less tape: 5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 16, which is less than 18.

05 · Pick your method

Which tool fits?

Say what you would measure before you do any math. Then try each one on paper or out loud.

  1. Two test mats are each 1 meter by 4 meters. They touch along their 4-meter sides to form one rectangle. How many meters of cord would go around the new shape?
  2. A loop has 45 cm of wire, bent into three equal sides. Could each side be 14 cm? Explain how you can tell.
  3. A design is 5 squares wide and 3 squares tall. Each square gets one stamp. How many stamps do you need?
Check your methods
  1. Add the outside lengths. The joined edge is now inside. 4 + 2 + 4 + 2 = 12, so you need 12 meters of cord. Adding both old perimeters counts the joined edge twice.
  2. Test the length: 3 × 14 = 42. That is 3 cm short of the full loop. Split 45 cm into three equal lengths, so 45 ÷ 3 = 15. Each side is 15 cm.
  3. Count the inside squares: 3 × 5 = 15. You need 15 stamps. Adding side lengths would measure only the outside.

Exit check. Finish both sentences: “I know it’s perimeter when …” and “If a length is missing, I can check it by …” Draw a tiny picture for one of your explanations.

Back at your work: Point to what the question asks you to measure. Mark all the lengths you know. Write a number sentence and name the unit. If you found missing sides, add all sides to check the total.

Next time: Before opening this page, draw a rectangle that is 4 units long and 2 units wide. Trace its perimeter, then sketch the squares inside. Can you say what each count measures?

Glossary

Perimeter
The total length around the edge of a flat shape.
Opposite sides
Sides across from each other in a rectangle. Each pair has equal lengths.
Square
A rectangle with four sides of equal length.
Area
The amount of flat space inside a shape, measured in square units.
Unit of length
A set length, such as a meter, a centimeter (cm), or a millimeter (mm).
Square unit
The space a square covers when each side is one unit long. A 1 cm by 1 cm square covers 1 square centimeter.
=, +, −, ×, ÷
Equals (=) says both sides have the same value. Add (+) joins parts. Subtract (−) can tell how much is left. Multiply (×) can count equal groups. Divide (÷) can split a total into equal groups.
