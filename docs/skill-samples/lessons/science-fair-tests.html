What made it cool more?

Make a prediction about cooling, then figure out whether a test can tell you what caused the change.

A sensor reads 28 °C at first and 24 °C later. How many degrees did the sample cool?

Check your start

It cooled by 4 °C. Count down from 28 to 24, or subtract 24 from 28. The last reading tells how cool it ended up. The difference tells how much it cooled.

Open the glossary for words and units.

1. Give your prediction a reason

A hypothesis is a possible answer to a question that a test can check. To judge one, say what you expect to happen and give a reason based on how the system works.

A model workshop makes two kinds of solid spacers. In a small trial, taking 8 joules (J) of energy out of a 1-gram (g) piece of gray spacer lowered its temperature by 4 °C. Taking 8 J out of a 1 g piece of tan spacer lowered its temperature by 2 °C. These trial values are invented.

Hypothesis. If two larger pieces of equal mass lose the same amount of energy, the gray spacer will cool by more degrees.

I agree with this prediction for a similar temperature range. With the mass and energy loss matched in the trial, gray cooled by more. Tan needs more energy taken away per gram for each degree it cools. That material property is called specific heat. The trial gives a reason to expect a larger drop for gray in the new test. The new readings will show whether the prediction holds.

Try a new prediction

In another trial, an equal-mass cork bead and a glass bead lose the same amount of energy and cool by the same number of degrees, within the sensor’s precision. A teammate predicts that a repeat under the same conditions will give the glass bead a larger drop. Do you agree? Give a reason from the trial.

Compare and retry

The trial gives you a reason to disagree with that prediction. When both beads lost the same energy, their measured drops were alike. The fact that they are made of different materials leaves the size of the drops open. Try again with a new trial result. If the glass bead had cooled by more degrees, how would that change your reason?

2. Follow the energy

Thermal energy transfer is energy moving from a warmer place to a cooler one. A warm tile on a colder surface can lose energy to that surface. How far its temperature drops depends on how much energy the tile loses, its material and its mass, the amount of matter in it.

Think of a whole sample and the energy it needs to lose to cool by one degree. That amount is its heat capacity. A stack of two identical tiles has twice the heat capacity of a single tile. It must lose twice the energy to cool by the same number of degrees. The material’s specific heat stays the same.

Predict before testing. If the same 24 J leaves three tiles together, will they drop by more or fewer degrees than the pair? Why?

Compare all four model settings

One, two, three and four tiles drop by 6 °C, 3 °C, 2 °C and 1.5 °C. Three tiles need to lose 12 J for each degree. The 24 J loss is enough for a 2 °C drop.

The model follows the tiles only. It assumes every tile in a sample reaches the same temperature, with no melting or freezing and no other energy changes. It starts with a known amount of energy lost. A real cold surface can remove energy at different rates, so you would need evidence of how much energy each sample lost.

Check the temperature readings

Two reusable blocks go into a cool chamber. One goes from 31 °C to 26 °C. The other goes from 38 °C to 30 °C. How many more degrees did the second block cool than the first?

Compare your calculation

The first drop is 31 °C − 26 °C = 5 °C. The second drop is 38 °C − 30 °C = 8 °C. The difference is 8 °C − 5 °C = 3 °C. The second block started much warmer, so it can end warmer even after dropping farther.

Retry with two new readings, 33 °C to 27 °C and 30 °C to 29 °C. How much larger is the first drop?

Check the retry

The drops are 6 °C and 1 °C. The first drop is 5 °C larger.

3. Find a second cause

Question. Do two equal-mass handle materials cool by different amounts when they lose the same energy?

In a workshop’s first trial, both handle blanks have the same mass and start at 35 °C. They rest on the same cold bench for one minute. Blank P sits directly on the bench. A thick felt pad sits under blank Q. Each sensor measures the temperature of its blank.

Repair and reason. Give both blanks matching contact with the cold surface. Use a meter set up to track the energy leaving each blank, and stop each run at the same energy loss. That matches the energy loss going into the comparison. Equal time on the same bench alone leaves the amount of energy lost uncertain. In a real setup, check for energy leaving through other paths, such as into the air.

Check the mass link too. Suppose one blank had an extra layer of its own material. Even with equal energy loss, that blank could cool less because more material must change temperature. Weigh the blanks and use equal masses. This repair matches how much material cools, while the energy repair matches how much energy leaves.

Try the same reasoning with a different clue

Two samples of the same material lose the same measured energy. One is a whole strip, and the other is a piece cut from an identical strip. Both are solid and start at the same temperature. A student says the smaller piece must have lost more energy because it cooled farther. How would you reply?

Compare and retry

Both energy losses were measured and match. The piece has less mass, so it needs to lose less energy per degree of cooling. Try this again with the same energy loss from two identical whole strips. Which cause from this check goes away?

4. Audit a new test record

A team wants to test a prediction about two prototype case liners, R and S. They predict R will cool farther than S if equal masses lose equal amounts of energy. Here is their first run. The numbers are invented for practice.

Both liners start at 32 °C and use identical 12 g trays. The same cooling fan runs at the same setting in both trials whenever it is on. A latch stops the fan during a pause. The sensors read the liners themselves.

Liner R

Scale with tray 42 g

  1. 0 min fan starts
  2. 4 min final reading

Final reading 23 °C

Liner S

Scale with tray 57 g

  1. 0 min fan starts
  2. 1 min fan pauses
  3. 3 min fan restarts
  4. 4 min final reading

Final reading 28 °C

  1. Find and compare the temperature drops. What does the record show about this run?
  2. Find two changes you would make to the procedure. For each, use a clue from the cards and explain how the change helps test the prediction.
  3. Do you agree with the team that this run shows R’s material made it cool farther? Explain what more you would need to see.
Compare your audit after trying it

Read the result. R cooled by 32 °C − 23 °C = 9 °C. S cooled by 32 °C − 28 °C = 4 °C. R cooled farther in this run.

Match the sample masses. The tray accounts for 12 g in each reading. R is 42 g − 12 g = 30 g. S is 57 g − 12 g = 45 g. Weigh equal masses of the liners before loading the trays. For the same material and energy loss, a larger mass would cool less. The mass difference alone could help explain the pattern.

Match and check energy loss. R got four minutes of moving air. S got one minute before the pause and one minute after it, for two minutes of moving air. Moving air can take energy away faster than still air. Remove the pause and use matching fan conditions. Check energy loss with a setup that can track the energy leaving each liner, and compare at the same energy loss. Even steady fans at the same setting can cool samples at different rates.

Decide what the evidence supports. The run shows a difference in cooling. It leaves the team’s explanation open. The larger mass and the pause could both help explain S’s smaller drop. If R cools farther in repeated tests with matched mass, measured energy loss and comparable temperature readings, that would support the prediction for those conditions.

Try a repair again

The team now uses 36 g of each liner, the fan runs without a pause, and the sensor records the middle of R and the edge of S. Which part of the test would you check first now? Explain how it could change the reading.

Compare your retry

Check where and when the temperature is read. An edge in moving air can be cooler than the middle. Use comparable sensor positions and let each sample reach an even temperature before taking the final reading, accounting for any energy it loses while you wait. Keep checking that the two samples lose the same energy.

In a new cooling report, one person says “X ended colder,” and another says “X’s material caused a bigger drop.” Which should you tackle first, calculating a change or checking the setup? Explain which move answers each part of the report.

Glossary

Hypothesis
A possible answer to a question that a test can check.
Thermal energy transfer
Energy moving from warmer matter to cooler matter because of a difference in temperature.
Joule (J)
A unit for measuring energy.
Mass (g for grams)
The amount of matter in an object. A gram is a unit of mass.
Degree Celsius (°C)
A unit for a temperature reading or a difference between readings.
Heat capacity
The energy a whole sample needs to gain or lose to change temperature by one degree, while staying in the same state.
Specific heat
The energy needed per unit mass of a material for a temperature change of one degree, while staying in the same state.
