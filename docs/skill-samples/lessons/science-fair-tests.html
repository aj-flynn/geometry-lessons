What made it cool more?

Use thermometer readings to compare cooling. Then design a test that can tell whether the material made a difference.

Which is warmer, 25 °C or 30 °C? How far apart are they?

Check the starting idea

30 °C is warmer. The readings are five degrees Celsius apart. The numbers on a Celsius thermometer get larger as temperature rises.

Open the glossary as you go. The readings and equipment in this lesson are fictional.

Read the change

When a warm pack sits on a cooler tray, energy moves from the pack toward the tray. The pack cools. When the tray is warmer than the pack, energy moves toward the pack and it warms.

A thermometer gives a reading at one moment. To find temperature change, subtract the starting reading from the ending reading. A negative change is a drop.

  • Pack B changed by 31 °C − 36 °C = −5 °C.
  • Pack A dropped 8 degrees. That is a bigger drop than B’s 5 degrees, even though A ended warmer.

Check a warming case

A cool mug goes from 16 °C to 23 °C. What is its temperature change, including the sign?

Compare the warming case

23 °C − 16 °C = 7 °C. A rise gives a positive number. If you used only the ending reading, find the difference between the two readings.

Retry with a cup that goes from 25 °C to 19 °C. Find its change and describe it as a rise or a drop.

Check the retry

19 °C − 25 °C = −6 °C. The cup dropped six degrees.

Predict what could change the drop

Mass measures how much matter a sample contains. Imagine taking the same amount of energy out of a thin bar and a heavier bar of the same material. In the heavier bar that loss is spread across more matter, so each gram loses less energy. Its temperature falls by less.

Test one change in a model

One strip loses 24 joules of energy. A joule (J) measures energy. The strip starts at 30 °C and ends at 24 °C. What if you joined two identical strips and took 24 J from the pair? Predict the new drop.

Compare the two-strip model

The pair drops 3 °C and ends at 27 °C. The total energy loss stays at 24 J, so each identical strip loses 12 J. Half the loss per strip gives half the drop.

The model assumes the strips cool evenly, stay solid and lose energy only where we count it.

Judge a prediction about material

A designer predicts that a wax panel and a clay panel of equal mass will cool by the same number of degrees when they lose equal energy. A separate test found that a gram of clay gave up more energy per degree of cooling than a gram of wax.

I disagree with the designer’s prediction. Each gram gives up the same amount of energy in this comparison. The clay needs to give up more energy for each degree, so the clay’s drop should be smaller. I would check by comparing the two drops in a test.

This property is called specific heat. It describes how much energy one gram of a material gains or loses for a change of one degree. Two materials can have close specific heats. When the values are unknown, a fair test could show similar drops or a difference large enough to measure.

Check a prediction with less information

Two equal-mass model building tiles are made from different mixtures. A teammate predicts that the tile labeled River will cool by more than the tile labeled Grove when each loses the same amount of energy. Do the labels give you a reason to agree? Explain your decision and say what result would support the prediction.

Compare your reason

The labels give no information about how much energy each tile gives up per degree. If River gives up less energy per degree, its drop could be larger. If the tiles have similar specific heats, the drops could be close. Compare the start and end readings for each tile after matched energy losses. A reliably larger drop for River would support the prediction.

Retry by assuming Grove needs less energy per degree. Decide what you would predict now and explain why.

Check the new prediction

Grove should show the larger drop. Each gram loses the same energy, and that energy can cover more degrees of cooling in Grove.

Trace the test before trusting it

A cook tests a cork pad and a rubber pad of the same mass. A cooling dock counts how much energy leaves a pad. The cook wants to know whether the pad material changes the temperature drop for a fixed energy loss.

Suppose one pad drops 9 °C and the other drops 4 °C, but the first lost more energy. Even two pads of the same material could give that pattern if one lost more energy. Set the dock to stop at the same measured energy loss for both. Matching the time alone works only if the same energy leaves each pad per minute and losses elsewhere are small.

Now suppose the dock removes the same energy but one pad has extra layers. That energy loss is shared by more material, which can make its drop smaller. Weigh the pads and use matching masses to remove that competing cause. Keep the same starting temperature, probe placement and reading method. Repeat the trials so that one odd reading does not decide the result.

Repair a test from its setup card

A crew tests two types of display tiles. They want to find out whether tile type affects the size of the temperature drop for a fixed energy loss. Each round tile has a mass of 45 g, even when the tile type changes. The crew runs one cycle per load on a cooling dock. The crew can end a cycle with a timer or a stop button.

Which load cooled more? Can the crew tell from these trials whether the tile type caused the difference? Write two changes to the procedure. For each, use a clue from the setup card and explain how leaving it as it is could change the measured drop.

Compare your test repairs

P drops 3 °C and Q drops 6 °C. These readings show what happened. Two parts of the setup leave the cause open.

  1. P uses 135 g of tiles while Q uses 90 g. With a fixed energy loss, the lighter load can cool by more because the loss is spread across less matter. Use the same number of tiles to match the masses.
  2. The cycle logs give no way to tell whether both loads lost the same energy. Q may have cooled for longer, and losing extra energy can cause a bigger drop even at equal mass. Measure the energy leaving each load and stop each run at the same measured loss.

After the changes, compare repeated temperature drops. A difference bigger than the usual variation would support an effect of tile type for these samples.

Retry with a new log. R and S each have three tiles. Both loads spend 40 seconds in the dock, but the crew switches the dock from low to high for S. S cools more. What can the crew conclude, and what would you check next?

Check the retry

The masses and times now match. S cooled more in this run. The higher setting could remove more energy in 40 seconds, so check how much energy left each load before making a claim about material. Run a new comparison with the same measured energy loss.

Take the method with you

A friend points to an ending thermometer reading and says it proves what caused the cooling. Explain how you would find the drop, then name something you would check in the setup before accepting the claim.

Next time, before you reopen the lesson, draw the path from an experiment setup to a claim. Add one arrow showing where a second cause could affect the result.

Glossary

Temperature change (°C)
Ending temperature minus starting temperature. The unit °C means degrees Celsius.
Energy transfer (J)
Energy moving between objects. A joule (J) is a unit of energy.
Mass (g)
How much matter an object contains. A gram (g) is a unit of mass.
Specific heat
Energy gained or lost by one gram of a material for a one-degree change, with the material staying in the same state.
Fair test
A comparison that changes what you want to study and keeps other conditions that could affect the result alike.
