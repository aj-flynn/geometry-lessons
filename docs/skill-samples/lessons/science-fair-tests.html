What made it cool more?

Follow energy into or out of a sample. Then decide what a temperature change can tell you.

Two drinks are both at 20 °C now. One started warmer. Which cooled more? If you’re unsure, read the two rows below.

Glossary

Read the change

Made-up thermometer readings for two drinks
DrinkBeforeAfter
A26 °C20 °C
B23 °C20 °C

The thermometer gives a reading at each time. Both drinks end at 20 °C. Drink A has farther to fall from its starting reading.

Goal. Find each drink’s signed temperature change, written ΔT (say “delta T”). The sign shows the direction.

  1. Subtract the before reading from the after reading. Use degrees Celsius (°C) for the calculation.
  2. For A, ΔT = (20 − 26) °C = −6 °C.
  3. For B, ΔT = (20 − 23) °C = −3 °C.

Both negative signs show cooling. A dropped 6 °C and B dropped 3 °C. The two readings are measured. The change is calculated.

A new reading

A thermos warms from 16 °C to 21 °C. Write the signed change and explain its sign.

Compare your calculation

ΔT = (21 − 16) °C = +5 °C. If you got −5 °C, check which reading came first in your subtraction. If you got 21 °C, you used the ending reading alone.

Try another pair

A jar cools from 24 °C to 17 °C. Give the signed change.

Check the retry

ΔT = (17 − 24) °C = −7 °C.

Give a prediction a reason

A sample is the portion you test. When a warmer sample touches a cooler surface, energy moves from the sample to the surface. The sample cools. If the sample starts cooler than the surface, the energy moves into the sample and the sample warms. Here we look at small changes with no melting or boiling.

Some materials take more energy per gram to change by one degree than others. That property is called specific heat. A gram is a unit of mass, or how much matter a sample contains. A joule (J) is a unit of energy.

Worked prediction · Two handle inserts

A designer asks if two solid inserts will cool by the same number of degrees when each loses 240 J. Both test pieces have the same mass. Earlier tests of equal-mass pieces found that insert P takes 120 J to warm by 1 °C and insert R takes 60 J. These values are made up for practice.

The designer’s hypothesis, a testable idea, is that the drops will match. I disagree with the prediction that the drops will match. With the same mass, P takes more energy per degree to warm. P also has to lose more energy per degree to cool. So removing the same energy from each should give P the smaller drop. That’s a reason to test the prediction. We still need to measure the two drops.

Make a call · Chilled bottles

Equal masses of two liquids begin at 8 °C. Each gains the same amount of energy from a warm bath. Earlier tests showed that the same mass of liquid L needed more energy to warm by one degree than liquid M. A student predicts that L will have the bigger rise. Do you agree? Explain what happens to the same energy in the two liquids.

Compare your reasoning

I’d predict the smaller rise for L. Each degree of rise uses more energy for L, so the same gain buys fewer degrees. If you chose L for the bigger rise, reread what the earlier tests measured. They compared the energy needed for one degree.

Retry with one fact changed

The earlier tests now show that L and M need the same energy per degree at this mass. Predict the rises and explain.

Check the retry

Expect the same rise for the same energy gain. Two materials can give the same change if they need the same energy per degree at that mass.

Trace what could change the result

Worked repair · A longer run on the same cooler

A test cooler removes energy from a warm pad at a steady 30 joules each minute (30 J/min). A short run takes 2 min and a long run takes 4 min. Imagine the same kind and mass of pad in both runs.

Goal. Decide if reusing the cooler gives the pads equal energy losses.

  1. Short run loses (30 J/min) × (2 min) = 60 J.
  2. Long run loses (30 J/min) × (4 min) = 120 J.
  3. The longer run removes more energy. It can produce a larger drop even with the same material.

For this cooler, set both runs to the same time and check that the rate stays the same. A real device can transfer energy at different rates as contact or temperature changes. A meter that tracks how much energy actually leaves the sample can check the total.

Worked repair · More of the same material

Imagine two identical warm gel pouches that each lose the same share of energy. Compare them with one pouch losing that whole amount. The pair has more mass, so less energy leaves each gram. The pair cools fewer degrees. When you want to compare materials, weigh out the same mass so that an extra amount of material can’t explain the drop.

Check a boundary

One run still removes 30 J every minute. In another run, poor contact lets only 15 J leave each minute. Both last 2 min. Can you treat the energy losses as equal? How would you check?

Compare your check

The second run removes 30 J and the first removes 60 J. The same time allows different totals at different rates. Check the actual energy removed or check the rate during each run as well as timing it.

Retry with a new setting

The second run still removes 15 J/min. Choose a time for it to match the first run’s loss.

Check the retry

(15 J/min) × (4 min) = 60 J. This works while that rate stays steady.

Judge a trial on your own

A shop compares resin and rubber seals. If the same energy leaves each sample, will the two materials cool by different amounts?

Each seal blank has a mass of 25 g, regardless of material. The shop uses the same insulated test chamber on the same setting for each run. The log shows when the chamber is switched on and off. The shop measured temperatures just before and after each run. These records are made up.

Pilot log · seal batches

Resin310:1210:15
Rubber510:3110:37

Both batches began at 30 °C. The resin ended at 29 °C and the rubber ended at 28 °C. The tester claims the material caused the difference. Decide whether the log supports that claim. Write two specific changes to the plan and a separate reason for each. Explain what you’d compare after the new runs.

Compare after you have a plan

The readings show drops of 1 °C and 2 °C. They leave the cause uncertain. Here’s one plan for a second trial:

  • Load three blanks of each material. The pilot used (3 × 25) g = 75 g and (5 × 25) g = 125 g. Changing the mass changes how much energy the batch must lose per degree.
  • Use an automatic three-minute shutoff on both runs. The pilot runs lasted 3 min and 6 min. The second run had more time to remove energy, even at the same setting. Keep the contact with the cooler the same. Check with an energy meter at the cooling surface that the two batches actually lose the same energy.

Compare the changes from the before readings. A difference after matching conditions would support a material effect for these batches under this setup. If you only changed the batch size, check whether the two old run lengths still affect the result.

Retry with a revised log

A coworker uses three blanks in each batch. Both runs last 3 min. A meter reports losses of 120 J for resin and 90 J for rubber. Explain what needs repair now and why the timer alone missed it.

Check the retry

Keep the batch masses matched and use the meter to stop each run at the same energy removed. The new readings show different amounts removed over the same time, so the average rates differed. The temperature changes can reflect that difference.

Explain your choice. When do you subtract thermometer readings, and when do you inspect a procedure? Tell a partner why one method can’t do both jobs.

Glossary

Sample
The portion being tested.
Mass (g)
How much matter is in the sample, measured here in grams.
Energy transfer (J)
Energy moving into or out of the sample, measured in joules.
Rate (J/min)
How many joules transfer each minute.
Temperature (°C)
How warm or cold a sample is, measured here in degrees Celsius.
Temperature change (ΔT)
The after reading minus the before reading.
Specific heat
The energy needed to warm one gram of a material by one degree Celsius.
Hypothesis
A testable idea about what happens or why.
System
The sample or group of objects we choose to track.
