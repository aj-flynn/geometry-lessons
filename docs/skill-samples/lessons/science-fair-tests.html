What made it cool faster?

Make a prediction about cooling. Then design a test that can tell you what caused the change.

Write your practice responses in your own notes or in the space provided. After each attempt, find its check in “Paper comparisons” at the end.

Start with two readings

One mug goes from 25 °C to 19 °C. A second goes from 32 °C to 26 °C. A friend says the second mug cooled less because it ended warmer. What would you tell them?

Compare your reasoning

Both dropped 6 °C. To find the size of a drop, subtract the final reading from the starting reading for each mug. For signed temperature change, written ΔT, subtract in the opposite order.

  • ΔT = final temperature − starting temperature
  • First mug: ΔT = 19 °C − 25 °C = −6 °C
  • Second mug: ΔT = 26 °C − 32 °C = −6 °C

The minus signs show cooling. Each mug fell by 6 degrees Celsius (°C).

Predict from a model

Two stacks in a model cooler

All tiles are identical. The whole of each stack starts at 30 °C and stays at an even temperature as it cools. Each tile needs to lose 20 J to cool by 1 °C.

Stack A1 tile
Stack B3 tiles

In this model, the cooler removes exactly 120 J from each whole stack. J stands for joules, a unit of energy. These are made-up values for the model.

The stacks are warmer than the cooler. Energy moves from the stacks to the cooler. This movement of energy because of a temperature difference is heat transfer. The thermometer records temperature in °C. The model gives energy transferred in J.

Predict first. Lee makes a hypothesis, a testable prediction with a reason. “The stacks will cool by the same number of degrees because the cooler removes the same number of joules from each.” Do you agree or disagree? Use the tile rule to explain your choice.

Review the tile model
Test the prediction

A 1 °C drop takes 20 J from Stack A. Each of the three tiles in Stack B must lose 20 J for the whole stack to drop by 1 °C.

  • Energy lost per 1 °C drop in Stack B = 3 × 20 J = 60 J
  • Drop in A = 120 J ÷ (20 J per °C) = 6 °C
  • Drop in B = 120 J ÷ (60 J per °C) = 2 °C

Disagree with Lee's prediction. Stack B has more of the same material, so changing the whole stack by 1 °C takes more energy. The same energy loss gives B a smaller drop.

Repair the reason. “A cools more because it's colder at the end” only describes what you see. Explain what causes the difference using the energy needed for a 1 °C drop.

More of the same material takes more energy for the same temperature change. Even objects with the same mass (amount of matter) can need different amounts of energy per degree if their materials differ. Adding energy reverses the direction of change in this model. The model keeps the energy needed per degree constant. In a real test that value can vary with temperature, so use the same temperature range when comparing materials. Keep each sample in the same state, such as solid, and measure after its temperature evens out.

Try the rule in reverse

In another model, two blocks have the same mass. Both cool by 3 °C. Block E loses 90 J and block F loses 150 J. Which would need more energy to warm back up by 1 °C? Explain how the model readings support your choice.

Compare the blocks

Block F. Both blocks drop the same number of degrees, and F loses more energy. E loses 30 J for each degree of cooling and F loses 50 J per degree. Warming by one degree takes that same amount of energy moving into each block in this model. The same temperature change can go with different amounts of energy transfer.

Follow the competing cause

A screen over a cooling tray

A club wants to test whether a mesh screen slows the cooling of a tray of water. Both trays start at 36 °C and sit in the same room for five minutes. The tray with the screen sits on a thick cork board. The uncovered tray sits on a cool metal shelf.

In a fair test, keep other conditions the same when they could affect what you measure. Here the screen is the condition the club wants to test. The result is the drop in water temperature.

  1. Different surface under the tray
  2. Different energy loss through the tray's base
  3. Possibly a different drop in water temperature

Imagine removing the screen. Energy can still leave through the bottom of each tray. Cork slows that path compared with contact with cool metal, so the drops could still differ. The club could put both trays on matching cork boards. Then a difference in the trays' bases has less chance of changing the result.

Using the same room and the same timer helps. It does not tell you how many joules each tray lost. Energy can also leave through the top and sides.

Retry with a new setup

A class tests whether a handle wrap keeps soup warmer. Two identical cups hold the same amount of soup and start at the same temperature. One cup has the handle wrap and a lid. The other cup is uncovered and has no wrap. The class compares their temperature drops after four minutes.

Change one step while keeping the class's question. Explain how the change would make the result easier to interpret.

Compare your repair

Put the same kind of lid on both cups, or leave both uncovered. A lid slows energy loss through the top, including loss through evaporation as water leaves as vapor. If both cups have the same lid condition, a difference in how much each cup cooled is easier to connect to the handle wrap. Keep the other matched conditions as well.

Your turn with a procedure diagram

A museum's cooling station

A museum wants to compare how much energy cork and rubber lose when they cool by the same number of degrees. In this simplified setup, the cooling pad is the only place the blocks lose energy. A meter measures how many joules pass from a block into the pad.

Make a case. A visitor predicts that if the blocks cool by the same number of degrees, they must lose the same number of joules. Do you agree or disagree? Give a reason about the materials and name a measurement that would settle the prediction.

Repair the procedure. Propose two specific changes to the plan in the diagram that keep the museum's question the same. For each, explain what could happen in the original plan and how your change helps.

Write your prediction and reason, what you would measure, and both changes and reasons before opening the comparison.

Write your prediction, what you would measure, and both repairs with reasons. Find the museum example in “Paper comparisons” at the end.

Review the procedure
Compare your prediction and repairs

Prediction. Disagree with “must.” Equal masses of different materials can need to lose different amounts of energy to cool by 1 °C. After a verified 4 °C drop from the same starting temperature, compare the joules measured by the pad. We need the readings to say which loses more, or whether they match within what the meter can tell apart.

  1. Read the block itself. Place the probe in the center of B as well as A. The air above B could be colder or warmer than the block, so stopping when the air reaches 26 °C might not give B a 4 °C drop. Wait for an even temperature before using each reading.
  2. Use the same temperature range. Start both blocks at 34 °C and stop each at 30 °C. Energy needed per degree can depend on the temperature range as well as the material. The matching range lets us compare the materials under the same conditions.

“Use the same probe” leaves its placement unclear. Revise that repair to say where the tip goes and what reading ends a run. Keep the masses matched. Repeat to see whether the energy readings consistently differ in this setup.

Take the method with you

  • When you make a prediction, name what should happen and explain how energy would move or how the sample would respond.
  • When you read a test plan, trace the steps from setup to reading to calculated change. For each possible competing cause, give a specific repair and explain why it matters.

Try this next time

Two thermoses start at 41 °C. One is wrapped in a towel and ends at 36 °C. The other is bare and ends at 34 °C. The wrapped thermos stood by a heater. Find both temperature drops. Can you tell from this test how much of the difference came from the towel? Explain.

Compare the recall example

The drops are 5 °C and 7 °C. The warm spot could slow energy loss even without a towel, so these readings cannot separate the two effects. Try again with both thermoses in the same surroundings and only one wrapped.

Glossary

Temperature change (ΔT)
Final temperature minus starting temperature. Negative values show cooling. Positive values show warming.
Joule (J)
A unit used to measure energy, including how much energy moves from one place to another.
Heat transfer
Movement of energy from a warmer object or place to a cooler one because of a temperature difference.
Hypothesis
A testable idea about how something works. Here it gives a prediction and a reason you can test.
Mass
How much matter an object contains. Grams and kilograms are units of mass.
Fair test
A comparison that changes the condition you want to test while matching other conditions that could affect the result.
Temperature probe
A sensor that measures temperature where its tip is placed.
