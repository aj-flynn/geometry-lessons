Follow the traces on a map

Use two clues from a map to explain what may have happened in a place.

A trace is a mark left behind by an event or process. A map key tells you what a map’s marks stand for. All the maps here show made-up places and findings. North is up on each map.

Write your practice responses in your own notes or in the space provided. After each attempt, find its check under “Paper comparisons” at the end.

1. Read the mark, then the pattern

Brook and orchard

Read the map in words

The brook runs across the north edge. The orchard is in the southeast. A long dark patch starts at the south bank of the brook and bends southeast to just beside the orchard. Two hills sit on either side of the long patch near the brook. A separate small dark patch lies in the southwest.

Quick check. Look at the dark mark in the southwest. What does it stand for? Does its size tell you how much material was found?

Check the key

It marks an area where silt was reported. The key gives locations. It gives no weight or thickness for the silt, so the size of the patch cannot tell you how much there was.

Floodwater can carry silt. As water slows over land, some of that silt settles. A claim is an idea we can test with evidence. Here’s one to test. Water from the brook reached the orchard during the storm.

An observation is a detail you can point to. Here, the long silt patch starts at the brook and extends to the orchard.

Reasoning explains why a detail fits the claim. If water spread from the brook to the orchard, it could leave silt along the way as it slowed. The long patch follows that possible route, so it supports the claim.

The map also shows a low opening between the hills toward the orchard. Floodwater could pass through that opening as it spread away from the brook. The opening shows where water could pass; the silt shows where material was left. Neither tells us the exact water depth or direction.

Now picture the long patch shifted south, so it runs from the small southwest patch to the orchard, with a clear gap by the brook. Predict how that change would affect the claim.

Compare your prediction

The dark patch now extends from the southwest patch to the orchard, with a gap by the brook. The map would still report silt by the orchard. It would give weaker support for the brook as the source because the pattern would no longer trace a route out from the brook.

2. Repair a weak explanation

Lookout ridge

Read the map in words

The lookout is near the north edge, and the cabins are near the south edge, a little farther west. Two long dark patches lie between them, one just south of the lookout and one just north of the cabins. A blank gap separates them. A small dark patch lies far to the east of the cabins.

Claim. One fire spread from the lookout toward the cabins.

A response says, “There is dark shading, so one fire definitely crossed the whole ridge.” Point to the marks that give the claim its strongest support. Then rewrite the response using their positions and what a fire can leave behind. How sure can you be?

Compare your repair

“Two patches of charcoal lie along a route from the lookout toward the cabins. A fire could leave burned wood in several places as it spread along that route, so those patches support the claim.”

Swap “dark shading” for what the key says and where the patches are. Then explain how the event could produce that pattern. The map gives no dates for the charcoal. The two patches might come from different fires. A blank area gives no report, so we cannot tell from the map alone whether charcoal is absent there.

Revise one sentence of your response before trying the next map.

3. Try the repair on a new map

Tank and garden

Read the map in words

The tank is in the northwest, the garden in the southeast and the drain in the southwest. Three dark strips with small gaps run diagonally from beside the tank to beside the garden. One separate dark strip extends east from the drain for a short distance. One area of high ground is north of the three strips, and a second is south of them. A lower gap between those areas runs from the tank side toward the garden side.

Claim. An old ditch carried water from the tank to the garden.

Someone points to the mud by the drain. Choose a more useful pattern on the map. Explain why that pattern supports the tank-to-garden claim better than the mud by the drain. Then choose a clue from the shape of the land and explain what it adds.

Compare your retry

Three mud strips run along a diagonal from the tank to the garden. Water traveling in an old ditch could leave mud in stretches along its route. The separate strip by the drain lies off that route and could have come from water at the drain. The high ground leaves a low gap running between the tank and garden. Water tends to follow lower ground, so the gap is a plausible route even without the mud. The gap alone cannot establish that a ditch was dug. To test that idea, look for a narrow channel joining the strips.

4. Write your own case

Willow Field

Read the map in words

A quarry cuts across the middle of the map from north to south. West gate is on the west side and Mill pond is on the southeast side. Two dark patches lie between West gate and the west rim of the quarry. Two more lie between the east rim and Mill pond. There is a separate dark patch on high ground in the southwest. On the west side, the edges of the high ground leave a low strip that curves from West gate toward the quarry. On the east side, they leave a low strip that curves from the quarry toward Mill pond. Both strips meet the quarry rim at about the same north-south position.

A quarry is a pit dug to remove rock. Digging removed the ground in that area. A stream can round and drop stones as it flows.

Claim. Before the quarry was dug, a stream made a bend through Willow Field from West gate to Mill pond.

Use the map to identify two different pieces of evidence for the claim. Explain how each one supports the claim.

Back to the Willow Field map

Check your reasoning

Compare your Willow Field response
  1. Rounded gravel. Two patches lead from West gate to one rim of the quarry. Two more run from the opposite rim toward Mill pond. One stream could have rounded and left gravel along different parts of its bed. Digging could have removed the traces where its route crossed the pit.
  2. Shape of the land. The two strips of low ground curve toward opposite rims of the quarry and line up across it. Water tends to flow through low ground. Before the middle was dug away, the two strips could have been parts of one curved valley.

The southwest gravel patch lies away from that route. The map gives no gravel ages or exact route through the missing ground. Comparing the gravel layers on both sides could help test whether they formed at the same time.

Before moving on, check that your two observations add different information. When you meet an unfamiliar map mark, name what the key says was found. Ask what process could leave that material there and where you would expect to see it. Compare that expected pattern with the map. For a landform clue, ask what the shape would allow or block.

Pond and shelter

Read the map in words

The pond is on the west edge and the shelter is in the east. The long dark patch runs west from just north of the shelter to the center of the map. It stops well east of the pond. Another dark patch sits near the south edge.

Before reviewing the lesson, recall the difference between an observation and an explanation. Then use this map to test the claim that water from the pond reached the shelter. Give one observation and say how strongly it supports the claim.

Compare your recall

Silt was reported by the shelter, but the long patch stops well short of the pond. Water can leave silt as it slows, so the patch fits water being near the shelter. The map gives weak support for the pond as the source, since the pattern does not reach it. The gap could also reflect where people looked.

Glossary

Trace
A mark left behind by a process or event.
Map key
A guide to what the marks, lines or colors on a map represent. Also called a legend.
Silt
Tiny particles of rock and soil, smaller than sand. Water can carry them and leave them behind as it slows.
Claim
An idea we can test by looking for evidence that supports it or makes it less likely.
Observation
A detail you can point to in the map or other data. It becomes evidence when it helps support or test a claim.
Quarry
An open pit dug to take out stone or other material.
Reasoning
An explanation of why an observation fits a claim. It often describes how an event or process could produce the trace.
