Reading sea surface height maps

Read a height at one place, then use two surveys to tell where the water surface changed.

Quick check. A water gauge reads 134 cm. Its usual reading is 140 cm. How far from usual is it, and in which direction?

Compare the gauge reading

It is 6 cm below usual: 134 cm − 140 cm = −6 cm. The minus sign tells you the direction from the usual reading.

A baseline is a reference for comparison. A deviation is the measured value minus that reference. It tells you where the reading is relative to the reference.

Use your own notes for responses. After each attempt, find the matching activity in “Paper comparisons” at the end.

Estimate off Mica Cape

Use the 14 February map to estimate the deviation at the diamond east of Mica Cape in meters. The legend (map key) shows the measured quantity and units.

  1. Find Mica Cape on the coast. Move right to the diamond in the water, where the horizontal line at 35° N meets the vertical line at 12° E. These are its coordinates (latitude and longitude).
  2. The water shade at the diamond lies about halfway between cream and violet. On this map those colors mark 0 and −20 cm, so the deviation is about −10 cm.
  3. There are 100 cm in 1 m. Keep the sign and divide the number of centimeters by 100.
    −10 cm = −0.10 m

The estimate puts the surface about 0.10 m below its own baseline on 14 February. A reading of −0.18 m the day before would mean that the surface rose between readings, even though it was still below its baseline.

2. Test a possible cause

Picture two wide compartments of equal area. They start with the same water height. A pump can move water between them. Assume the walls stay put, there are no leaks, and the pump moves water without changing its volume.

Predict first. The pump runs from Left to Right. Which way does each surface move? What happens to their average height? Then predict what happens if you stop the pump and add the same volume of water to each side.

Compare the pump and refill

The pump transfers water. The left falls and the right rises by the same amount because the compartments have equal areas. Their average stays fixed. This is redistribution, or moving water within a system. Added water raises both surfaces from where they were when the pump stopped. Their average rises too.

Ocean winds and currents can move water between regions. Water can also come in from outside a region. Another way to raise a surface is thermal expansion. Under typical ocean conditions, warming the same mass of seawater makes it take up more space, and cooling makes it take up less. If the area it covers stays fixed, extra volume raises the surface. The compartment model leaves out tides, changes in basin shape and differences in salinity (how much salt is dissolved).

Try another setup. Now the right compartment is twice as wide, with the same front-to-back length. The pump transfers the same volume as before. Which side has the larger height change? Explain what happens to the total volume.

Compare the unequal compartments

The left falls twice as far as the right rises. The same transferred volume is spread across twice the area on the right. The total volume is unchanged. With unequal areas you would have to give the larger area more weight when calculating the average height.

Compare your repaired claim

The water at 8° E, 60° N shifts from a shade between jade and dark pine to a shade near jade on the cream side. The surface fell there, though it stayed above its baseline. At 8° E, 52° N, the surface rose from below its baseline to above it. Write what changed at the specific places instead of saying the whole sound rose.

Recheck your evidence

Compare the survey note
  1. Violet lies on the negative side of the height-deviation scale. It indicates a surface below the baseline. You need another time for a change and a temperature measurement for a temperature claim.
  2. Kestrel Point's tip is on 52° N. At 8° E on that line, the shade is about a quarter of the way from −20 cm to −40 cm, or about −25 cm.
    −25 cm = −0.25 m
  3. The earlier caption gives 4 April, so you need a 5 April survey for the first request. On 4 April the southern water is mainly below its baseline, apart from a green pocket near 4° E. Most of the northern water is above its baseline, though there is a violet patch. Flow from south to north could lower the surface in one part and raise it in the other.
  4. The captions give 4 and 9 April. You need 3 and 8 April maps to answer the exact request. One major change is the broad southern shift toward zero or above. Near 52° N, compare 8° E and 10° E on both dates. Each changes from between violet and dark plum to between cream and jade. A shift in currents could move water south and help raise the surface. Warming could also contribute. Measure the direction and strength of currents, or record temperatures from the surface down on both dates to test the warming explanation. These maps alone leave the cause open.

Glossary

Baseline
The reference value for a comparison. A map can use a different local mean at each location.
Deviation
Measured value minus reference value, in the same units. Zero indicates a match with the reference.
Legend
The map key connecting colors, lines and symbols to quantities or features.
Coordinates
Latitude locates a point north or south of the equator. Longitude locates it east or west of the prime meridian. The map grid lets you follow one coordinate to its crossing with the other.
Redistribution
Movement from one part of a system to another.
Thermal expansion
An increase in the volume of a fixed amount of material as it warms under specified conditions.
