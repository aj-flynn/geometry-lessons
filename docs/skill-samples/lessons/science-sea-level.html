Reading water-height maps

Find a place, read its height difference, and use patterns to test what moved the water.

If the usual water height is 80 cm and today's height is 77 cm, did the water sit above or below usual? Subtract the usual height to check.

Open the glossary for words and units.

Read a snapshot

A baseline is the height used for comparison. In these models it is the long-term average height. Each model lake uses one flat baseline for every place and date. A departure is the height at a place minus its baseline. The height in the warm-up was 3 cm below usual, a departure of −3 cm.

A map for one date is a snapshot. Its legend tells you what the shading measures. On a continuous legend the colors blend, so an in-between shade stands for an in-between value.

Find a spot near Reed Dock

Given. Use the June 8, 2021 map and the spot one grid square north of Reed Dock. The legend uses centimeters (cm).

Goal. Estimate the departure in meters (m).

  1. Find the square for Reed Dock. Move one grid square north, into the water.
  2. Match the spot's pale orange shade to about halfway from −20 to 0 on the legend. The departure is about −10 cm.
  3. Since 100 cm = 1 m, divide the number of centimeters by 100 to get meters. −10 cm = −0.10 m.
  4. The water there was about 0.10 m below its baseline. The negative sign says which side of the baseline the water was on.

Spot a claim the map can't support

“The orange near the dock means the lake was cooling.” Point to the legend title. It names height departure. Testing cooling would take temperature readings on two or more dates.

Check the other end

In the same panel, pick a spot one grid square south of Slate Bluff. Someone says the purple there proves the water is getting higher. Point to a clue that makes that claim too strong. Say what the shade can tell you.

Compare your reasoning

The panel has one date. The shade is on the positive side of the height scale, so the water is above its baseline there. To say it is getting higher, you need an earlier height from the same spot.

Retry. Suppose an earlier map shows deeper purple at the same spot with the same legend. What would that say about the change?

Compare the retry

It was higher on the earlier date. The water fell between snapshots and stayed above the baseline.

Look for a pattern before naming a cause

Two ends on one date

In the 2021 Lantern map, scan several places at each end. Most of the north end has purple shades and most of the south end has orange shades. The orange side reaches farther north on the east shore. One spot near the middle would miss that north–south contrast.

What could lift one end and lower the other? Wind that blows in one direction for hours can push surface water toward one shore. Some of that water comes from the opposite end. This redistribution moves water around inside the lake. If more water flows into the lake than leaves it, the lake gains water. That can lift the surface at both ends.

Predict, then test

Picture a long tray holding water. Which change could put water above the starting line at one end and below it at the other? Predict what each test will do before opening it.

Test a steady push toward the north

Dashed line shows the starting height.

Water piles up at the north end and drops at the south end. In this tray no water spills or enters, so the amount of water stays the same.

Test pouring in more water

Dashed line shows the starting height.

After the surface settles, both ends are higher. More water is stored in the tray.

Close either test to return to the prediction. The drawings exaggerate the slopes. Real lakes have uneven bottoms and can keep sloshing after the wind eases.

Check the proposed cause

A calm afternoon follows a storm. The north end and the south end are both higher than before the storm. Which test fits better? Name one measurement that would help you check.

Compare your reasoning

A gain of water fits better than a simple push from one end to the other. Compare water entering and leaving the lake over the storm. A pair of shore readings alone can't prove how much water was gained.

Retry. On another afternoon the north end is down and the south end is up. Which direction of steady wind would fit?

Compare the retry

Wind blowing toward the south could push water toward that end. Check the wind record before treating the push as the cause.

The same places on two dates

Compare the June 8, 2021 and June 8, 2023 panels using the same legend above. The north end went from strong purple to pale purple. The south end went from strong orange to pale purple. The ends changed in opposite directions and the contrast weakened.

A wind pushing north in 2021 and weaker wind in 2023 could explain why the height difference shrank. Added water alone could lift both ends, but it can't explain why the north end fell. Wind records and water flow records could help separate the effects.

A report says the second Lantern panel was taken on July 8, 2023. Point to the caption before you use the claim. It says June 8. You can compare the dates shown, but you'd need a July panel to check a July claim. When panels use different baselines or legends, find out whether their values can be put on the same scale first.

Check what changed

A camper saw that the north end was purple in both Lantern panels and wrote, “The north end stayed at the same height.” Which map cue would you use to repair that sentence?

Compare your reasoning

Read the strength of the purple on the shared legend. The lighter 2023 shade is closer to zero. The north end fell even though it was still above its baseline.

Retry. A different bay goes from pale orange to deep orange on one shared scale. Which way did its water height move?

It fell farther below its baseline.

1. Correct a map reading

A radio host points to the orange water near Quarry Point on the earlier panel and calls it a sign of rising water. Point to the clue that would make you change the claim. What can the map tell the host instead?

Compare your reasoning

The date tells you it's one snapshot. The legend shows that the orange near Quarry Point means water below its baseline that day. A rise needs a second reading at the same spot. A single negative departure could go with either a rise or a fall since an earlier date.

2. Estimate an offshore height difference

On the earlier panel, find the water two grid squares west of Pump House. Estimate the departure there in meters. Explain how you found the place, matched the shade, and handled the units.

Compare the estimate

The spot is purple between 0 and +20 cm, a little closer to the +20 mark. About +13 cm is a reasonable estimate. +13 cm = +0.13 m. Estimates from +0.10 to +0.16 m fit the shade.

Retry. Suppose the shade at the same spot lands one quarter of the way from 0 to −20 cm on a new map. Give the departure in meters.

Compare the retry

One quarter of 20 cm is 5 cm. This spot is on the negative side, so −5 cm = −0.05 m.

3. Compare whole regions

On the earlier panel, is the western half generally above or below its baseline? Use the grid line through Gull Island to split the lake. A friend uses the water beside the south shore of the island to argue the opposite. Point to the evidence you would use to repair the claim.

Compare your reasoning

Most of the west half has orange shades, so it's generally below its baseline. The small patch of purple immediately south and just west of the island is an exception. Scan the open water along the west shore as well as the patch near the island.

4. Propose a cause for the contrast

A field note dated August 18, 2024 asks why one end was higher than the other. Can either panel support an explanation for that date? Then use the earlier displayed panel to describe a contrast, propose a cause, and name a measurement that could test it.

Compare your reasoning

The captions give August 8 dates, so there is no August 18 panel here. On August 8, 2024, the east is mostly above its baseline and the west mostly below. Steady wind toward the east could push water from the west end. Check the direction and strength of the wind before the snapshot. More stored water alone would be more likely to lift both ends.

5. Explain what changed between panels

Do the dates, baseline and legend allow a comparison? Describe a major change using evidence from more than one part of the lake. Compare a change in water stored with a change in where the water was pushed. Which better fits the evidence? What other data would you ask for?

Compare your reasoning

Both show August 8 with the same baseline and legend, two years apart. Most of the west went from orange to purple and much of the east became a stronger purple. The west changed more. Higher departures on both sides fit a gain in stored water better than a simple shift from one end to the other. Wind could still affect the pattern. To test the gain, compare amounts of water that entered and left, and check whether the wind changed.

Retry. Suppose a third panel with the same legend shows a darker purple west end and a newly orange east end. Name a mechanism that could explain the change from the 2026 panel. What could show you the proposed mechanism is wrong?

Compare the retry

Wind pushing toward the west could raise that end and draw water away from the east. A record showing calm conditions during and before the shift would weaken that idea. With only two snapshots you can't tell when the shift occurred.

Glossary

Average (mean)
The sum of the readings divided by how many readings there are.
Baseline
The reference height. The models use a flat long-term average for each lake.
Departure
Height minus baseline. A positive value is above baseline and a negative value is below it.
Legend
The map key. A continuous legend has blended shades for values between its marks.
Redistribution
Movement from one part of a lake to another.
Snapshot
A map of conditions at one time.
cm and m
Centimeter and meter, units of length. One meter is 100 centimeters.
Synthetic data
Made-up values used to test a method.
