Reading water height maps

Use a map to say where water sits above or below its usual height. Then use two maps to decide what changed and suggest a cause you could test.

A pond gauge reads 107 cm. Its usual reading is 100 cm. How far above or below usual is the water?

Check your start

107 cm − 100 cm = +7 cm. The plus sign says the water is above the usual mark. Try the same subtraction with 94 cm instead of 107 cm.

Tell a shift from a broad change

Strong wind can push surface water along a lake. The downwind end, where the wind is blowing toward, can rise as the other end falls. Water has moved from one part of the lake to another. This is called redistribution.

Water height is a proxy here. It is a clue about what happened to the water, even though it measures only the position of the surface. Predict which end changes in each model test, then open its comparison. Close the test to reset the comparison. Start each test with the same flat surface in a basin with fixed sides.

  • Turn on a strong wind toward the east

    West falls and east rises while the wind pushes the surface east. The same water occupies different parts of the basin. A wind record could help test this explanation.

  • Pour extra water into the basin and let it settle

    Both ends rise once the water spreads out. The amount of water increases. Check rain, stream inflow and outflow.

  • Warm the same amount of water evenly

    For water already at ordinary room temperature, warming makes it take up more space. Both ends can rise. This is thermal expansion. Check water temperatures at several depths.

Real lakes have uneven shores, wind that changes and water layers at different temperatures. In the ocean, currents can also move water across large distances. A few height maps alone give clues about a cause. Weather, temperature or water-flow measurements can test it.

Take the method with you

For a question about one spot, a whole side or a change over time, decide which readings you need. Explain why a single high point can answer only some of those questions. On your next visit, try to recall how you would test whether wind moved water from one side to the other.

Glossary

Reference height
The height used for comparison, such as a long-term usual height.
Departure
Water surface height minus reference height.
Legend
A key that tells what the map’s shades or marks mean.
Centimeter (cm) and meter (m)
Units of length. One meter equals 100 centimeters.
Redistribution
Movement of water from one part of a lake or ocean to another.
Proxy
A measured clue used to learn about something else.
Thermal expansion
An increase in the space a material takes up as it warms.
