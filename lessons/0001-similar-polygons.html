Geometry · Similar polygons

Same shape at a new scale

Match corners and edges, decide which measurement changes, and justify when two shapes are similar.

MatchMeasureTest a claimYour turnGlossary

Quick check. A 5 cm strip becomes 8 cm long. What do you multiply 5 by? What undoes the change?

Check the starting idea

Multiply by 8/5, the target divided by the original. Multiply by 5/8 to undo the change. These fractions are reciprocals, meaning their product is 1.

1. Match the corners first

Two polygons are similar when matching angles have equal measures and all matching side lengths share one multiplier. A shape can be turned or reflected (flipped) and still have the same shape. The corners and sides that match are called corresponding.

Match two metal badges

Given. These pentagonal badges are similar. Equal arc marks identify matching angles. Small boxes mark right angles. The first badge has AB = 7 cm, BC = 3 cm, EA = 6 cm, and m∠D ≈ 143.1°. The target has VW = 9.8 cm, XY = 7 cm, YZ = 4.2 cm, and ZV = 8.4 cm. Here AB means the length of the segment from A to B, and m∠D means the measure of angle D.

Goal. Write the matching order, every angle pair and the side-length proportion. Then find one missing length in each badge and the marked angle in the target.

  1. One arc gives C ↔ X and two arcs give D ↔ Y. Follow the edge from the one-arc corner toward the two-arc corner and continue around both outlines. This gives ABCDE ∼ VWXYZ. The symbol ∼ means “is similar to.”
  2. The matched angles are congruent (equal in measure). Write ∠A ≅ ∠V, ∠B ≅ ∠W, ∠C ≅ ∠X, ∠D ≅ ∠Y and ∠E ≅ ∠Z. So ∠Y measures about 143.1°, because its partner does. Angle measures stay the same when you scale.
  3. Pair both endpoints of each edge, including the edge that closes the walk. The scale factor k is the target length divided by its original partner.
    VW/AB = WX/BC = XY/CD = YZ/DE = ZV/EA = k
    k = (9.8 cm)/(7 cm) = 7/5.
  4. Original → target. Multiply to find WX from BC.
    WX = (7/5)(3 cm) = 4.2 cm.
  5. Target → original. Divide by 7/5, or multiply by its reciprocal 5/7.
    DE = (5/7)(4.2 cm) = 3 cm.

Use the marks and givens as evidence. Zooming or printing changes the physical size of these drawings.

Try a different direction

The original is polygon KLMNP and the target is similar polygon UTQSR, in matching order. RU = 8.8 cm, PK = 5.5 cm, and QS = 12 cm. Write every angle pair and a complete proportion. Find MN and give the factor from target to original.

Check your work and retry

∠K ≅ ∠U, ∠L ≅ ∠T, ∠M ≅ ∠Q, ∠N ≅ ∠S and ∠P ≅ ∠R. With original on top, KL/UT = LM/TQ = MN/QS = NP/SR = PK/RU = 5/8. The last-to-first edge gives the factor, and MN = (5/8)(12 cm) = 7.5 cm.

If you got a larger length, check which figure belongs on top. Retry by writing the five fractions in the opposite direction. What does the new factor mean?

Compare the retry

Reverse every fraction. The factor becomes 8/5, which turns each original length into its target length.

2. Trace the edge or cover the face

Perimeter measures the distance around the boundary. Area measures the region inside it. Both depend on how you scale, but they count different things.

Predict. A rectangular stamp starts 7 cm wide and 5 cm tall. If both lengths double, how many copies of the original face cover the target? How many times as long is the trip around its edge?

Compare your prediction at factor 2

At factor 2 the boundary is twice as long because each of its four sides doubles. The area is four times as large because the face is twice as wide and twice as tall.

For any similar polygons: Side length and perimeter use one factor k. Area uses k². For area, you can split the region into triangles. Each triangle’s base and height scale by k, so each area scales by k². The sum of the areas does too.

The stamp model changes both dimensions together. Its dashed seams mark whole original tiles when they fit as a complete grid. Use the labeled dimensions to reason about fractional factors.

Find trim and ink for a smaller proof

Given. A print shop has a 14 cm by 11 cm sample with a rectangular edge. A similar proof is 8.25 cm tall. Goal. Find the edge trim for both prints, the ratio of proof trim to sample trim, and the proof’s ink area.

  1. Pair height with height. k = (8.25 cm)/(11 cm) = 3/4.
  2. Scale the other dimension. w = (3/4)(14 cm) = 10.5 cm.
  3. Trace the sample’s four sides. Its perimeter is 2(14 cm + 11 cm) = 50 cm.
  4. Each side of the proof is 3/4 as long. Its perimeter is (3/4)(50 cm) = 37.5 cm. The proof-to-sample perimeter ratio is 3/4. Check by tracing its four sides, 2(10.5 cm + 8.25 cm) = 37.5 cm.
  5. The sample’s area is (14 cm)(11 cm) = 154 cm². The proof’s area is (3/4)²(154 cm²) = 86.625 cm². If you report to the nearest hundredth, write approximately 86.63 cm².

Reverse the job. If the proof’s exact area is the given value, find the sample’s area by dividing by (3/4)². That is the same as multiplying by (4/3)², so 86.625 cm² × 16/9 = 154 cm².

Choose a factor before calculating

A display maker reduces a pentagonal design. A 26 mm edge becomes a 19.5 mm edge. The new design covers 207 mm². Find the area of the larger design and explain whether you multiply or divide by the area factor.

Compare your setup

Small/large length = 19.5/26 = 3/4. Small/large area = (3/4)² = 9/16. The known area is the small one. Divide 207 mm² by 9/16 to get 368 mm². Dividing by 3/4 undoes only one dimension.

Retry with perimeter. If the small design has perimeter 90 mm, find the large perimeter.

Check the perimeter retry

Perimeter is a sum of lengths, so divide 90 mm by 3/4 to get 120 mm. The unit remains mm.

3. Test a claim about every pair

Do matching angles settle it?

Compare three rail plates. Each is a parallelogram, a four-sided shape whose opposite sides are parallel. The marked 60° corner forces the same angle measures in all three. Compare short with short and long with long.

A → B gives the same factor on the long and short edges. Both 10.5/7 and 6/4 equal 1.5. Since opposite edges are equal and the angles match, all the similarity conditions hold.

A → C keeps the same long-edge ratio, 10.5/7 = 1.5. The short-edge ratio is now 5/4 = 1.25. A single change in the short edge breaks similarity. So two parallelograms with a 60° corner are sometimes similar. A and B show a match, and A and C give a counterexample, a case that breaks the general claim.

Always · prove it for any allowed pair
Two triangles with angles 40°, 55° and 85° always match. Any pair shares two angle measures, so the triangle angle-angle similarity test applies.
Sometimes · give a match and a counterexample
The rails show both outcomes with the same kind of polygon and the same angle condition.
Never · find a condition that cannot match
A convex pentagon has every interior angle under 180° (use corners without straight angles). A concave pentagon has an inward corner with interior angle above 180°. That angle has no partner in the convex shape.

Build your own evidence

Two triangles each have exactly one 40° angle. Are they always, sometimes or never similar? Write complete angle sets for any examples you need, and check that each set can form a triangle.

Check your claim and retry

Sometimes. For a match use the set 40°, 65°, 75° for both. For a counterexample compare that set with 40°, 50°, 90°. Each set has three positive angles totaling 180°. Each contains exactly one 40° angle. The second pair has different angle sets.

Retry by adding the condition that both triangles also have a 70° angle. Which kind of evidence do you need now?

Compare the retry

Always. Every allowed pair has both 40° and 70°, so angle-angle proves similarity. The remaining angle is 70° in both.

Your turn · Make a press seal

The proof and press seals are similar. A single arc marks one pair of corners, and a double arc marks another pair. On the proof, PQ = 5 cm, SP = √40 cm, and the enclosed area is 24 cm². On the press seal, LN = 7.5 cm and ∠K measures approximately 102.5°. A fabricator offers a third seal whose matching side lengths are all doubled from the proof, but its double-arc corner measures 110°.

On paper, write the matching order and all angle pairs. Give the side proportion with press lengths on top. Find the length of ML, the measure of ∠R, and the press area. Which seal could use the same outline template after resizing, the press seal, the offered seal, or both? Use the givens to justify your decision.

Compare after you have a complete argument

PQRS ∼ LNKM. ∠P ≅ ∠L, ∠Q ≅ ∠N, ∠R ≅ ∠K and ∠S ≅ ∠M. LN/PQ = NK/QR = KM/RS = ML/SP = 7.5/5 = 3/2. Therefore ML = (3/2)√40 cm, which is about 9.49 cm. The angle at R measures about 102.5° because it corresponds to K. The press area is (3/2)²(24 cm²) = 54 cm².

The press seal uses the same resized template. The offered seal fails the angle condition because its matching angle differs from the proof’s. Matching length ratios alone do not guarantee similarity here.

Recall tomorrow. Sketch any similar pair. Name the original and the target. Explain how to get the new perimeter and area from one known side pair, then explain how you would reverse the two changes.

Glossary

Polygon and vertex
A polygon is a closed shape bounded by line segments. A vertex is a corner. The plural is vertices.
Corresponding and congruent
Corresponding parts are matched partners. Congruent angles have equal measures, shown with ≅.
Similar (∼)
Matching angles are congruent and matching side lengths all have the same target-to-original ratio.
Ratio, proportion and scale factor
A ratio compares quantities by division. A proportion equates ratios. The scale factor is the ratio of a target length to the corresponding original length.
Reciprocal
The number that multiplies with a given nonzero number to give 1.
Perimeter and area
Perimeter measures the outline in length units such as centimeters (cm). Area measures the face in square units such as square centimeters (cm²) or square millimeters (mm²).
Interior angle, convex and concave
An interior angle is measured inside the shape at a vertex. A convex polygon has no inward corner. A concave polygon has at least one inward corner.
Counterexample
An allowed case that disproves a claim about every allowed case.
Approximation (≈)
A value close to the exact value, often obtained by rounding.
