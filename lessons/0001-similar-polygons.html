Similar polygons

When one shape is a resized copy of another, what changes and what stays the same? Match the parts, choose a direction, and explain what the measurements tell you.

Match and scaleMatch parts · Find lengths

Match the parts

Start with an ordered statement below. When no matching list is supplied, use the angle marks to build one.

Start here if ratios feel unfamiliar

A ratio compares quantities by division. A short ribbon is 8 cm long and a long ribbon is 12 cm. What multiplier takes the short ribbon to the long one?

Choose one:

  • 12 − 8 = 4
  • 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5
  • 8 ÷ 12 ≈ 0.67
Compare your reasoning

Yes. 12 divided by 8 is 1.5: one whole short ribbon plus half of it. A multiplier tells how many times as long, while subtraction tells how much longer.

See the ratio bridge

12 ÷ 8 = 1.5 compares the long ribbon with the short one. Multiplying checks it: 8 × 1.5 = 12. A factor above 1 enlarges; a factor between 0 and 1 reduces.

A polygon is a closed flat shape made of straight sides. A vertex is a corner; vertices means more than one corner.

Similar means the same shape: matching angles have equal measures and all matching lengths share one multiplier. A corresponding part is the part that matches.

A similarity statement is an ordered matching list. In this new example, JKLMN ∼ TUVWX means the first letters match, then the second letters, and so on. The symbol ∼ (also written ~) means “is similar to”; ↔ means “matches.”

J ↔ T · K ↔ U · L ↔ V
M ↔ W · N ↔ X

These drawings have the same size to isolate the matching step. A similar copy could also be resized. The stated correspondence, not which side looks “closest,” is your evidence.

The symbol ∠K means “the angle at vertex K.” For example, ∠K and ∠U have equal measures. Side KL matches UV because both endpoints match. Rotating (turning) or reflecting (making a mirror image of) a figure does not change its similarity. Angles with equal measures are congruent. Write ∠K ≅ ∠U. The symbol ≅ means “is congruent to.” A scale factor changes lengths, while each angle keeps its measure.

Record the full match

Start at J and follow the list once around both boundaries. Write each angle pair as ∠J ≅ ∠T, ∠K ≅ ∠U, ∠L ≅ ∠V, ∠M ≅ ∠W, and ∠N ≅ ∠X.

For the sides, start with JK and TU. Step forward by one letter to KL and UV, then continue. After MN and WX, return to the starting corners to include NJ and XT. Count five side pairs for five vertices.

Try a new correspondence

Two hexagons are similar, BDFHJL ~ NPRTVX. Which side matches JH? Then record the matching angle for ∠J and the side pair that closes the two outlines. Compare after you choose.

Choose one:

  • VT
  • RV
  • VX
Compare your reasoning

You matched both endpoints in this new hexagon: J maps to V and H maps to T. VT and TV name the same side; reversing the letters does not change the segment.

Hint for the match

Line up the two names in rows. Start at the first letter in each row, and for the closing edge look at the last letter too.

Check your angle and closing edge

J and V are in the fifth slot, so ∠J ≅ ∠V. The last-to-first edges are LB and XN. In the direction from the first hexagon to the second, their ratio is XN / LB, in the same order as TV / HJ.

If the figures use angle marks

Read the angle marks

A vertex is a corner. The small curved lines inside a corner are angle arcs. Within a diagram, angles with the same arc count are marked as equal in measure. Two arcs identify a group; they do not mean two degrees. Different arc counts do not, by themselves, say that angles are unequal.

The two figures in each pair below are given to be similar. The marks identify matching corners. Equal angle marks alone would not prove that general polygons are similar; their corresponding side lengths must also share one scale factor. Use the givens, not the apparent size or position.

Match corners before sides

Predict: which corner matches K, even after the figure turns?

Compare your match and follow the endpoints

K matches R because both have one arc. Continuing by the marks gives L with W, M with T, and N with S. The symbol ↔ means “corresponds to,” so we can record K ↔ R, L ↔ W, M ↔ T, and N ↔ S.

Now match a side by both endpoints: side KL joins the one-arc and two-arc corners. Its corresponding side is RW, joining those same marked groups in the other figure. Turning the figure does not change the match.

Match a reflected pair

This is a different pair of similar figures. Which side in the first figure corresponds to HZ in the reflected figure? Side names list their two endpoints; HZ and ZH name the same side.

Hint: notice the endpoints

Find the arc count at H and at Z. In the first figure, find the corner in each of those two groups. The side must join both corners.

Choose one and explain both endpoint marks:

  • UV
  • WX
  • UX
After your attempt: compare and retry

H matches X because each has two arcs. Z matches W because each has four. HZ therefore corresponds to XW, also named WX. UV joins the three-arc and one-arc groups; UX matches only one required group. If you chose either, cover this explanation, track both endpoints, and try again.

Keep one direction

Write original → target above your work. Then use matching sides in that direction:

The scale factor, written k, is the positive multiplier for a length. It has no unit because the matching length units cancel in the division.

k = target length ÷ original length
target length = k × original length

A ratio compares quantities by division. A proportion says two ratios are equal. Read the pentagon pairs from their ordered names. Put a side from the target in every numerator (the top of a fraction) and its original partner in the denominator (the bottom).

TU / JK = UV / KL = VW / LM = WX / MN = XT / NJ = k

If the direction reverses, use the reciprocal, meaning one divided by the factor: 1 / k. Use the same units before dividing. A larger target gives k greater than 1. A smaller target gives k between 0 and 1. If a question names the two quantities in a ratio, read “first to second” as first ÷ second, even when it reverses your scaling direction.

Start with an easy anchor. Doubling a length uses a factor of 2. Returning to the original uses a factor of one half. Try a 6 cm ribbon: doubling gives 12 cm; halving 12 returns to 6. The two factors undo each other because 2 × one half = 1.

Resize a clipped panel

A maker cuts a smaller panel from a scaled copy of a five-sided template. The original bottom edge is 26 cm, and the copy’s matching edge is 16.9 cm. Both drawings use the same unit scale. Before opening the steps, predict whether the copy’s boundary is longer or shorter. The perimeter is the total length of that boundary.

Use the complete side pair

For original → copy, divide 16.9 by 26 to get 0.65. The right edge of the copy is 0.65 × 14 = 9.1 cm. Check that 9.1 ÷ 14 gives the same factor as 16.9 ÷ 26.

Find both perimeters

Add each original side once: 14 + 13 + 14 + 26 + 19 = 86 cm. Each side of the copy gets multiplied by 0.65. You could scale all five sides and add, or multiply the whole sum once: 0.65 × 86 = 55.9 cm.

Read the order of the requested ratio

“Copy to original” asks for 55.9 ÷ 86 = 0.65. “Original to copy” asks for 86 ÷ 55.9 = 20/13. Compare the sizes first. The original’s boundary is longer, so original ÷ copy is greater than 1.

Work back to the original

Two similar flat game tokens have a matching pair of sides measuring 25 mm on the original and 35 mm on the copy. A different side of the copy is 21.7 mm. How long is that side on the original?

Compare your reasoning

The forward factor is 35 ÷ 25 = 1.4. To find the original length, divide 21.7 by 1.4 to get 15.5 mm. Multiplying 15.5 mm by 1.4 returns the given copy length.

Hint about going back

If x stands for the missing original length, then 1.4 × x = 21.7 mm. What undoes multiplication?

Pause here. Can you name the matching parts and explain which way your multiplier goes? That is enough for this visit if you are working on side lengths.

Area and shapeResize · Compare shapes

Explore the change

Imagine enlarging a rectangular art print. The original is 13 cm by 9 cm. Its perimeter is 44 cm and its area (space inside) is 117 cm². The notation cm² means square centimeters. Resize both dimensions together. Try 2, then 0.8. Compare the width, boundary, and interior with the original at both settings. Explain which comparison changes fastest and why.

Predict before you move

When both dimensions change by the same factor, does the area use that factor once or twice?

Choose one:

  • Use it once
  • Use it twice
Compare your reasoning

Good prediction. Area combines width and height, so look for the scale factor twice. Watch both dimensions as the print changes.

Drag, or use the arrow keys. Below 1 shrinks the print; above 1 enlarges it.

Reveal the doubled picture

Why the square? Area uses two lengths. The target area is (k × width) × (k × height), so it contains two factors of k. Perimeter adds lengths, and each length gets only one factor.

In the fixed starting example, at k = 1.6, the perimeter factor is 1.6 and the area factor is 2.56. Ratios must use the same original-to-target direction. The same rules apply to other similar polygons. Imagine splitting a polygon into triangles with no overlap. Each triangle’s base and its perpendicular height both scale by k, so each piece’s area scales by k². Add the pieces to get the whole area.

From a multiplier to a measurement

For a different hexagonal stencil, the original perimeter is 42 cm, the original area is 70 cm², and every length grows by 1.2. Predict whether both measurements grow before reading on.

See the two calculations

Perimeter: 42 × 1.2 = 50.4 cm. Area: 70 × 1.2² = 100.8 cm². The perimeter adds lengths, while area combines two dimensions. You can use the original area directly: you do not need to find a base and height.

Find the area of a smaller stencil

A similar seven-sided stencil is made from an original that covers 200 mm². A matching edge measures 34 mm on the original and 15.3 mm on the copy. Find the area covered by the copy. Before calculating, decide what kind of measurement the edge pair gives you.

Compare your reasoning

The length factor is 15.3 ÷ 34 = 0.45. The area factor is 0.45 × 0.45 = 0.2025. The copy covers 200 × 0.2025 = 40.5 mm². The factor has no unit. The area is in square millimeters and is smaller than 200 mm².

Hint about what is given

You know the area of the whole original, so you can scale that area directly. You do not need to reconstruct the stencil.

Test the shape

Right angles alone do not settle whether two rectangles are similar. Pair the longer sides, then pair the shorter sides. The two target-over-original length ratios must agree.

For the art-print example, compare 20.8 / 13 with 14.4 / 9. Each is 1.6, so one uniform scale works for both dimensions. You can also compare long-side / short-side within each rectangle. A turn does not change that aspect ratio: the longer length divided by the shorter length.

If the side factors differ, one uniform scale cannot produce the target. The difference tells you that at least one dimension changed by a different factor. For general polygons, check the corresponding angle condition as well as all corresponding side ratios.

Same scale, or just a stretch?

Both targets below get wider. Compare the width and height factors to see which keeps the original shape. All three drawings use the same unit scale.

The stretched rectangle still has four right angles. Its proportions changed because only one dimension grew. This is why matching angles alone is not enough for rectangles.

A useful checking routine

Write the correspondence. Put both ratios in the same direction and units. Compare exact fractions before rounding. Then check that every required condition is supported by the givens, not merely the appearance of a drawing.

Try it here: diagnose a stretch

One rectangular fabric patch is 25 cm by 14 cm. Another is 40 cm by 25.2 cm. Which observation settles whether one uniform scale can make the second patch?

Choose one:

  • The perimeters differ
  • The angles are right
  • The side factors differ
Compare your reasoning

Yes. Width uses 40/25 while height uses 25.2/14. The unequal factors indicate a stretch rather than one uniform scale.

Hint about what must match

A uniform scale needs the same factor in both dimensions. Check the two ratios before making your conclusion.

Reason and practiceTest claims · Choose a method

Reason about a claim

For an “always, sometimes, or never” claim, decide what evidence would support your choice before choosing a label.

  • Always: give a reason that works for every allowed pair. A few successful drawings cannot prove “always.” Try to break your claim with a counterexample: one allowed case that makes it false.
  • Sometimes: build an allowed example where the claim holds and an allowed counterexample where it fails.
  • Never: find a requirement that makes the claim impossible for every allowed pair. One failing example is not enough.

A reason for every case

Consider this new claim: “A triangle’s perimeter is greater than its longest side.” Decide whether testing many drawings or explaining the boundary gives stronger evidence.

Compare the reasoning

The perimeter includes the longest side plus two positive lengths. It must be greater than the longest side. This establishes always because the reason covers every allowed triangle, including ones you have not drawn.

Test the evidence

A learner claims, “Making a similar copy changes the perimeter.” Copies of the same size are allowed. Which tests would establish sometimes?

Choose one:

  • Factors 1.2, 1.4, and 1.6
  • Factors 1 and 1.3
  • Factor 1 alone
Compare your reasoning

Those cases support sometimes for this new claim. Factor 1 keeps the perimeter; factor 1.3 changes it. One unchanged case disproves always, and one changed case disproves never.

Hint for building evidence

Think about which multiplier leaves a length unchanged, then choose a different positive multiplier. Both are allowed in a similarity.

Could a counterexample be disallowed?

A learner proposes a scale factor of 0, which would collapse a polygon to a point. Before reading on, decide whether this is an allowed similar polygon.

Check the condition

It is not allowed here: similarity uses a positive scale factor and produces a polygon with positive side lengths. A counterexample must satisfy the conditions of the claim. An impossible or excluded case cannot settle it.

Rule out every case

Never needs a reason that rules out every allowed case. A contradiction is a conflict between facts that cannot both be true.

Worked claim: “A polygon and its uniform enlargement can have equal perimeters.” Here every side has positive length, and enlargement means multiplying every length by the same factor greater than 1.

Predict: Could choosing a different polygon make the claim work? Explain before opening the reasoning.

Compare with a reason covering every case

The claim is never true under these givens. Perimeter is the sum of the side lengths. Every positive side becomes longer, so their sum must become larger. Equal perimeters would require that same sum to stay unchanged: a contradiction. This reason covers every allowed polygon and every enlargement factor greater than 1; it does not depend on one drawing.

New check: choose enough evidence

Two similar polygons enclose positive areas. The copy has a smaller area than the original. Claim: “The copy can have a larger perimeter.” Which evidence is enough to show that this claim is never true?

Use copy ÷ original for every ratio. Recall: the length factor multiplies perimeter, and its square multiplies area.

  1. For one copy made with half-sized lengths, both area and perimeter decrease; that example disagrees with the claim.
  2. A larger perimeter would require a factor greater than 1; squaring that factor would make the area larger too.
  3. For many different smaller-area copies, the measured perimeters decrease; all those drawings disagree with the claim.

After choosing, compare the evidence

B is sufficient. Suppose the copy did have a larger perimeter. Its length factor would be greater than 1. The area factor is that number squared, so it would also exceed 1. The copy would have a larger area, contradicting the given smaller area. This rules out every allowed pair.

A tests one case; C tests many cases. Neither covers all cases. If you chose either, retry by asking: “Which option uses a relationship that applies to every similar pair?” Then explain the contradiction in your own words.

Choose a claim label

Same border, same area?

Two rectangular cards have equal perimeters. Are their areas always, sometimes, or never equal? Explain what would be enough evidence before choosing. The cards are allowed to have the same dimensions.

Hint about allowed pairs

Compare pairs with the same sum of length and width. For each pair, work out what multiplying the dimensions tells you.

Choose one: always, sometimes, or never.

Compare your evidence

Two 17 cm by 11 cm cards have equal perimeters and equal areas. A 17 cm by 11 cm card and a 20 cm by 8 cm card also have equal perimeters (56 cm), but their areas are 187 cm² and 160 cm². The first pair rules out never. The second rules out always. If your choice differed, identify which pair you missed and try to build your own.

Practice and explain

You have checked each idea as you learned it. Now mix them: choose the method before calculating. Use a hint and retry if needed, then finish with the exit check.

Read a perimeter ratio in the requested order

A park map shows similar six-sided garden beds. A 4 m edge on the small bed matches a 7 m edge on the large bed. The small bed’s perimeter is 32 m. Find the ratio of small perimeter to large perimeter. Enter a decimal rounded to three places.

Compare your reasoning

From small to large the length factor is 7 ÷ 4 = 1.75. The large perimeter is 32 × 1.75 = 56 m. In the requested order, 32 ÷ 56 = 4/7 ≈ 0.571. The ratio is below 1 and has no unit.

Hint about the ratio order

Read “small to large” as small divided by large. Should the answer be above or below 1?

An icon returns

An outline icon was enlarged with a length factor of 1.25. What length multiplier takes the enlarged icon back to its original size?

Compare your reasoning

The return length multiplier is 1 ÷ 1.25 = 0.8. Check: 1.25 × 0.8 = 1, so the two changes undo each other.

Hint about undoing a change

What number multiplied by 1.25 would give one?

A badge corner

Two seven-sided badges are similar. A corner of the original measures 118° (degrees), and the target length factor is 1.8. Which statement about the matching corner is justified?

Choose one:

  • Multiply the angle by 1.8
  • Multiply the angle by 1.8²
  • Keep the angle equal
Compare your reasoning

The target angle stays 118°. Similarity keeps corresponding angles equal, even when lengths grow. 1.8 describes lengths; it is not an angle multiplier.

A cut-paper pattern

A hexagonal paper pattern has area 88 mm². A similar copy uses a length factor of 0.6. What is the copy’s area? First name the relationship you need.

Compare your reasoning

The area multiplier is 0.6 × 0.6 = 0.36. The copy area is 88 × 0.36 = 31.68 mm². The factor has no unit; the resulting area uses square millimeters.

Hint about units

Your final answer is a measurement in mm², not a unitless factor.

A border for a new shape

A five-sided emblem has side lengths 2.4, 3.1, 4.6, 2.7, and 3.2 cm. In a similar copy, the side corresponding to 3.2 cm is 5.44 cm. How much cord goes exactly once around the copy? Ignore knots and extra overlap.

Compare your reasoning

The original perimeter is 2.4 + 3.1 + 4.6 + 2.7 + 3.2 = 16 cm. The length factor is 5.44 ÷ 3.2 = 1.7. The copy needs 16 × 1.7 = 27.2 cm of cord. Scaling every side and then adding gives the same result.

Hint about the quantity

Perimeter is the sum of all side lengths. The known pair gives target/original = 5.44/3.2. Use that same factor on the sum, or scale all five sides and then add.

Exit check: use the whole idea

A fresh similar floor-plan outline has a perimeter multiplier of 1.9. Without looking back, find its area multiplier and explain why the two multipliers differ.

Compare your reasoning

The perimeter factor is also the length factor. The area multiplier is 1.9 × 1.9 = 3.61. Check your explanation: perimeter adds lengths, while area combines two length dimensions. Your explanation is a reflection, not an automatically graded response.

Keep nearby: glossary and sources

Your glossary

Similar polygons
Polygons with the same shape: corresponding angles have equal measures, and every corresponding side length has the same scale factor. Size and orientation may differ.
Corresponding parts
Vertices, angles, and sides that match under the similarity. Their order matters.
Angle arcs
Curved marks inside an angle. The same arc pattern marks equal angle measures; its arc count identifies a group rather than a number of degrees. Different patterns alone do not assert unequal angles.
Congruent angles
Angles with equal measures. Enlarging a similar figure does not enlarge its angles.
Symbols ∼, ≅, ∠, ↔, and °
∼ means similar to. ≅ means congruent to. ∠ names an angle. ↔ marks corresponding parts. ° means degrees, a unit of angle measure. A superscript ² means squared, or multiplied by itself.
Scale factor, k
The positive multiplier that takes each original length to its matching target length.
Perimeter and area
Perimeter measures the boundary in length units. Area measures the interior in square units.
More words in this lesson
Polygon and vertex
A polygon is a closed flat shape made of straight sides. A vertex is a corner where two sides meet; vertices is the plural.
Proportion
A statement that two ratios are equal. A ratio compares two quantities by division.
Reciprocal
One divided by a nonzero number. It reverses a multiplication scale factor.
Aspect ratio
A comparison of a rectangle's longer side to its shorter side. It describes the rectangle's proportions.
Contradiction
A conflict between facts that cannot both be true. To rule out a claim, show that accepting it would conflict with the givens or an established relationship.
Counterexample
An allowed example that shows a claim is not true in every case.
Linear and square units
Linear measurements describe lengths, such as centimeters. Square units measure area, such as square centimeters, written cm².
