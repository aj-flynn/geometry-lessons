Similar polygons

Match the parts. Keep the direction. Then decide whether you are scaling a length or an area.

Work through this in short stops. Every example here is new; use the method to return to your own work independently.

Know the words

Similar polygons
Polygons with the same shape: corresponding angles have equal measures, and every corresponding side length has the same scale factor. Size and orientation may differ.
Corresponding parts
Vertices, angles, and sides that match under the similarity. Their order matters.
Congruent angles
Angles with equal measures. Enlarging a similar figure does not enlarge its angles.
Scale factor, k
The positive multiplier that takes each original length to its matching target length.
Perimeter and area
Perimeter measures the boundary in length units. Area measures the interior in square units.

Definition reference: Math Open Reference: similar polygons.

Match before measuring

A similarity statement is an ordered matching list. In this new example, JKLMN ∼ TUVWX means the first letters match, then the second letters, and so on.

J ↔ T · K ↔ U · L ↔ V
M ↔ W · N ↔ X

These drawings have the same size to isolate the matching step. A similar copy could also be resized. The stated correspondence, not which side looks “closest,” is your evidence.

For example, ∠K and ∠U have equal measures. Side KL matches UV because both endpoints match. Rotating or reflecting a figure does not change its similarity.

How do I match a side?

Find the partner of each endpoint, then join those partners. Keep the same matching when you write every ratio.

Keep one direction

Write original → target above your work. Then use matching sides in that direction:

k = target length ÷ original length
target length = k × original length

A proportion says two ratios are equal. For the pentagons, target-over-original stays consistent:

TU / JK = UV / KL = VW / LM = WX / MN = XT / NJ = k

If the direction reverses, use the reciprocal, 1 / k. Keep units consistent before comparing. A target that is larger should give k greater than 1; a reduction gives a positive k less than 1.

A fresh missing-side example

Two similar cardboard labels have a corresponding pair of sides measuring 17 mm on the original and 27.2 mm on the target. Another original side is 23 mm. What method finds its matching target side?

Hint: identify the complete pair

Use the pair with both measurements known to find the multiplier. Do not compare unrelated sides.

Hint: keep target over original

The multiplier is 27.2 ÷ 17. Apply that same multiplier to the other original side.

Walk through this new example

27.2 ÷ 17 = 1.6, so the target side is 23 × 1.6 = 36.8 mm. A proportion for the same reasoning is target side / 23 = 27.2 / 17. Check the result by dividing the matching target side by its original side.

Why the square? Area uses two lengths. The target area is (k × width) × (k × height), so it contains two factors of k. Perimeter adds lengths, and each length gets only one factor.

Here, at k = 1.6, the perimeter factor is 1.6 and the area factor is 2.56. Ratios must use the same original-to-target direction. For similar polygons of any shape, the same length/perimeter/area rules apply.

Test the shape

Right angles alone do not settle whether two rectangles are similar. Pair the longer sides, then pair the shorter sides. The two target-over-original length ratios must agree.

For the art-print example, compare 20.8 / 13 with 14.4 / 9. Each is 1.6, so one uniform scale works for both dimensions. You can also compare long-side / short-side within each rectangle. A turn does not change that aspect ratio.

If one ratio works and the other does not, investigate a stretch rather than a uniform enlargement. For general polygons, check the corresponding angle condition as well as all corresponding side ratios.

A useful checking routine

Write the correspondence. Put both ratios in the same direction and units. Compare exact fractions or cross-products before rounding. Then check that every required condition is supported by the givens, not merely the appearance of a drawing.

Reason about a claim

For an “always, sometimes, or never” claim, decide what evidence would support your choice before choosing a label.

  • Always: give a reason that works for every allowed pair. A few successful drawings cannot prove “always.” Try to break your claim with a counterexample.
  • Sometimes: build an allowed example where the claim holds and an allowed counterexample where it fails.
  • Never: find a requirement that makes the claim impossible for every allowed pair. One failing example is not enough.

Try the logic on a different subject: “A positive number equals its cube.” The number 1 fits; the number 2 does not. Those two cases support “sometimes.” Now apply that style of reasoning to a geometric claim, using its definitions and conditions. Make the final classification yourself.

A hint for the match

Line up the first letters of each name, then the second letters. A side needs two partners, not just one.

Choose the right factor

A new mosaic is made at a linear scale factor of 1.7. By what factor does its area change? Enter the factor, not a square-unit measurement.

A hint about dimensions

Imagine scaling both the base and height. Count how many factors enter their product.

Take the method back

On your own problem: name the matching parts, mark the comparison direction, choose length or area, and justify the relationship before calculating. Do the calculation yourself.

Later, without looking: explain why a perimeter scales once while an area scales twice. Ask in the private chat if a step is unclear; say where your reasoning got stuck.
