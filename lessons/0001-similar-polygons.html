Similar polygons

Match the corners, follow the edges and choose what to scale.

Imagine checking a print proof that arrives turned sideways. You need to tell whether it kept its shape and predict how much material it uses. Two polygons are similar when all matching angles are equal and all matching side lengths share one multiplier in a consistent order.

Warm up with two strip lengths

A 14 cm strip is shortened to 9 cm. Write the new-to-old ratio and say which length goes first.

Check the warm-up

The new length is the numerator, so the ratio is 9 cm ÷ 14 cm = 9/14. The units cancel. Reversing the order gives 14/9, the multiplier that takes you from 9 cm back to 14 cm.

01 · Match

Find the edge by its endpoints

A pair of chamfered mats

The mats are similar. All sides are measured in centimeters. Matching arc counts mark matching angles. One mat has been turned and flipped. The two views are fitted separately. Use their printed lengths.

Goal. Write the matching order, then find the scale factor, the multiplier that takes an original length to its matching enlarged length. Use it to determine p, q and v.

  1. Start with the marks. The two marked corners are neighbors. The single arcs match T with M and the double arcs match U with N. Walk from T to U and from M to N, then keep following each boundary. This pairs V with W, R with K and S with L.

    Corresponding parts are the parts that line up when the shapes match. The similarity statement RSTUV ∼ KLMNW records those pairs in the same position.

  2. Write the angle pairs. The symbol ≅ means “is congruent to.” The five angle statements are ∠R ≅ ∠K, ∠S ≅ ∠L, ∠T ≅ ∠M, ∠U ≅ ∠N and ∠V ≅ ∠W. The marked angle at M is therefore v° = 135°. Turning and scaling preserve the angle between the same two edge directions.

  3. Write the side pairs. Match both endpoints. In the proportion, an equality of ratios, the enlarged lengths are on top. The last ratio includes the edge that closes each mat.

    KLRS= LMST= MNTU= NWUV= WKVR

    Here letter pairs in ratios stand for lengths. The scale factor is target length divided by its matching original length. Use WK and VR, the known pair.

    k = 11.2 cm ÷ 7 cm

    k = 1.6

  4. Go forward or undo the scale. With p and q measured in centimeters, their equations are p/8 = 1.6 and 6.4/q = 1.6. Multiply to go forward. Divide by the multiplier to undo it.

    p = 8 × 1.6 = 12.8

    1.6q = 6.4

    q = 6.4 ÷ 1.6 = 4

    Check the direction. The enlarged UV match is 12.8 cm, longer than 8 cm. The original LM match is 4 cm, shorter than 6.4 cm.

02 · Measure

Trace the border. Count the tiles.

Two tiled cutouts

Each tile is 1 cm by 1 cm. The dark outline traces the entire boundary, including the turn into the notch.

The perimeter is the length around the boundary. The area is the amount of surface inside it. Measure the perimeter in centimeters and the area in square centimeters.

Predict first. From the original to the enlargement, write four values: the scale factor, one corresponding-side ratio, the perimeter ratio and the area ratio. Identify which are equal. Use the tiles and the outline to explain the difference.

Test your prediction against the cutouts

Scale factor and side ratio. The long top edge gives k = 6 cm ÷ 4 cm = 3/2. Each side uses that same factor.

Perimeter ratio. The edge lengths of the original in centimeters are 4, 2, 2, 2, 2, 4. They add to 16 cm. On the copy they are 6, 3, 3, 3, 3, 6 and add to 24 cm. The perimeter ratio is 24/16 = 3/2. Every term in the sum was multiplied by 3/2, so the sum was too.

Area ratio. Remove the missing corner from each whole grid. The original covers 4 × 4 − 2 × 2 = 12 tiles and the copy covers 6 × 6 − 3 × 3 = 27 tiles. The area ratio is 27/12 = 9/4. The rows get longer and there are more rows. Both dimensions use 3/2, so the area factor is (3/2)2 = 9/4.

If you gave all four ratios as 3/2, find a block of 2 × 2 tiles in the original that scales to a block of 3 × 3 tiles. Use that block to revise your area explanation.

For any similar polygon pair. Trace and add the sides to see why the perimeter uses k once. To see why area uses k2, split the original polygon into triangles and use the matching cuts in the copy. Every base and corresponding height scales by k. Each triangle area, and therefore their sum, scales by k2.

Use one known area. Suppose you make a third cutout with a 5 cm top edge. You know the original top edge is 4 cm and the original area is 12 cm². Find the area of the third cutout without counting its tiles.

k = 5 cm ÷ 4 cm = 5/4

Athird = (5/4)2(12 cm²)

Athird = (25/16)(12 cm²) = 18.75 cm²

Work back from the border

Restoring a print frame

The original and the smaller proof frame are similar rectangles. A trim strip exactly 75 cm long went once around the proof frame, with no gap or overlap. For the original frame, a 98 cm strip is available.

Goal. Decide if the 98 cm strip will cover the original frame, then find the original short edge for the cutting guide.

The strip follows the boundary, so 75 cm is the proof’s perimeter. To go from proof to original, put the original long edge on top. That multiplier applies to every edge and therefore to their sum.

k = 34 cm ÷ 25.5 cm = 4/3

Poriginal = (4/3)(75 cm) = 100 cm

The 98 cm strip is 2 cm short. The two long edges use 68 cm, leaving 32 cm shared equally by the two short edges.

Original short edge = (100 cm − 2 × 34 cm) ÷ 2 = 16 cm

Verify using the smaller frame

The proof’s short edge is (75 cm − 2 × 25.5 cm) ÷ 2 = 12 cm. Scaling that edge back to the original gives (4/3)(12 cm) = 16 cm.

03 · Defend

Decide what the givens guarantee

Three rectangular labels

Each corner measures 90°. Lengths are in the same unit.

Claim to test. Are two rectangular labels always, sometimes or never similar?

Make a pair that works. For A → B, the short sides give 10/5 = 2 and the long sides give 16/8 = 2. Every angle is 90° and opposite edges repeat, so these checks cover the whole boundary.

Make a counterexample. A → C has the same right angles, but 10/5 = 2 and 14/8 = 7/4. A common multiplier would keep short edges shorter than long edges, so switching the pairings cannot make the factors equal. A and C are two allowed rectangles that fail the claim. The two cases together justify sometimes.

Always
Show why every allowed pair must match. For example, triangles with angles 25°, 75° and 80° match by AA similarity, which says that two matched angles suffice for triangles.
Sometimes
Give an allowed pair that works and an allowed pair that fails.
Never
Show an incompatibility that applies to every allowed pair. A polygon with an inward corner has an interior angle above 180°. A convex polygon has no angle above 180°, so it cannot match that angle.

G · Class claims
  1. Always. Both have angles alternating 70°, 110°, 70°, 110°. Start at a 70° corner and match the longer adjacent edges. If the short sides are s and t, then the long sides are 2s and 2t. The short-side and long-side ratios both equal t/s, and opposite sides repeat.
  2. Sometimes. Take two rhombi with acute angles 60° and sides 5 and 7 units, respectively. The angle lists match and every side ratio is 7/5. For a pair that fails, keep the first and replace the second with a 75° rhombus of side 7 units. Its angles are 75° and 105°, so matching side ratios alone cannot establish similarity.
  3. Never. Multiplying six equal lengths by one common factor still gives six equal lengths. The other hexagon has unequal sides, so the side condition fails even if its angles match. Such a hexagon is possible: draw lengths 2, 3, 2, 2, 3, 2, turning 60° in the same direction at each corner. The opposite edges cancel in pairs, closing the figure.

If you gave one example for “always,” replace the specific sizes with arbitrary lengths and show what the class guarantees. If you gave one failed pair for “never,” show why the conflict persists for every pair.

Glossary

Similar polygons
Polygons that match exactly after a uniform resizing and any turns, flips or slides needed. All corresponding angles are congruent and all corresponding side lengths share one scale factor.
Corresponding
Matching parts of two figures. For a side, check both endpoints. For an angle, check the vertex and its adjacent sides.
Similarity statement (∼)
Lists corresponding vertices in matching positions. Following the letters traces a boundary and returning to the first letter closes it.
Congruent angles (≅)
Angles with equal degree measures. In ∠R ≅ ∠K, ∠ names the angle and ≅ describes the match.
Proportion
An equality of two ratios. A chain of equal ratios uses the same order throughout.
Scale factor
The positive multiplier from original to target. Divide a target side length by its matching original side length, using the same units.
Perimeter
The sum of the boundary lengths. Use linear units such as cm.
Area
The measure of a region. A square centimeter (cm²) is the area of a 1 cm by 1 cm square.
Counterexample
One allowed case that shows a proposed general claim can fail.
Convex polygon
A polygon whose line segment between any two points in it stays inside or on the boundary.
