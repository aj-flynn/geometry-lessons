Before you start

A 14 cm strip is shortened to 9 cm. Write the new-to-old ratio and say which length goes first.

Check the warm-up

The new length is the numerator, so the ratio is 9 cm ÷ 14 cm = 9/14. The units cancel. Reversing the order gives 14/9, the multiplier that takes you from 9 cm back to 14 cm.

Words to know

What “similar” means

Imagine checking a print proof that arrives turned sideways. You need to tell whether it kept its shape and predict how much material it uses. Two polygons are similar when all matching angles are equal and all matching side lengths share one multiplier in a consistent order.

Problem type 1

Match turned polygons, then find a missing length

Use this when one polygon is turned or flipped and you need a matching angle, the scale factor or a missing side. Find each edge by its endpoints, never by its position on the page.

The mats are similar. All sides are measured in centimeters. Matching arc counts mark matching angles. One mat has been turned and flipped. Goal: write the matching order, then find the scale factor, the multiplier that takes an original length to its matching enlarged length. Use it to determine p, q and v.

  1. Start with the marks

    Match the marked corners

    T with M, U with N

    The two marked corners are neighbors. The single arcs match T with M and the double arcs match U with N.

  2. Follow the boundary

    Keep walking both boundaries

    RSTUV ∼ KLMNW

    Walk from T to U and from M to N, then keep following each boundary. This pairs V with W, R with K and S with L. The similarity statement records those pairs in the same position.

  3. Angle pairs

    Read off the missing angle

    ∠T ≅ ∠M, so v° = 135°

    The five angle statements are ∠R ≅ ∠K, ∠S ≅ ∠L, ∠T ≅ ∠M, ∠U ≅ ∠N and ∠V ≅ ∠W. Turning and scaling preserve the angle between the same two edge directions.

  4. Scale factor

    Use the known pair

    k = 11.2 cm ÷ 7 cm = 1.6

    WK and VR are both known. The scale factor is the target length divided by its matching original length, so the enlarged length goes on top.

  5. Go forward

    Multiply to find p

    p = 8 × 1.6 = 12.8

    UV matches NW, so p/8 = 1.6. Multiply to go forward. The enlarged match, 12.8 cm, is longer than 8 cm, as it should be.

  6. Undo the scale

    Divide to find q

    1.6q = 6.4, so q = 6.4 ÷ 1.6 = 4

    ST matches LM, so 6.4/q = 1.6. Divide by the multiplier to undo it. The original match, 4 cm, is shorter than 6.4 cm.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Match the marked corners first.

    T with M, U with N

    Why?

    Matching arc counts mark congruent angles, and those are corresponding corners. The copy can be turned or flipped, so the marks are the evidence, not the position on the page.

  2. From the matched corners, walk both boundaries in the same order and write the similarity statement.

    RSTUV ∼ KLMNW

    Why?

    Corresponding parts are the parts that line up when the shapes match. The statement records each pair in the same position, so every later pair can be read straight from it.

  3. Write the angle pairs and read off any missing angle.

    ∠R ≅ ∠K, ∠S ≅ ∠L, ∠T ≅ ∠M, ∠U ≅ ∠N, ∠V ≅ ∠W

    Why?

    The symbol ≅ means “is congruent to.” Similar polygons have every pair of corresponding angles congruent, so the marked angle at M is v° = 135°.

  4. Write the side proportion with the enlarged lengths on top, including the edge that closes each mat. Then find k from the known pair.

    KLRS= LMST= MNTU= NWUV= WKVR

    k = 11.2 cm ÷ 7 cm = 1.6

    Why?

    Match both endpoints of every edge. In the proportion, an equality of ratios, letter pairs stand for lengths. The scale factor is target length divided by its matching original length, so every ratio in the chain equals k. Use WK and VR, the known pair.

  5. Multiply by k to go forward. Divide by k to undo it. Then check the direction.

    p = 8 × 1.6 = 12.8, q = 6.4 ÷ 1.6 = 4

    Why?

    With p and q in centimeters, the equations are p/8 = 1.6 and 6.4/q = 1.6. The enlarged UV match should be longer than 8 cm, and the original LM match shorter than 6.4 cm.

If you matched edges by position

Suppose you paired UV with the longest upright edge of the enlarged mat. Instead, circle U and V in the statement, find N and W in the same positions, then replace the denominator or numerator that used the wrong edge. The edge can change direction on the page.

Words to know

Trace the border. Count the tiles.

The perimeter is the length around the boundary. The area is the amount of surface inside it. Measure the perimeter in centimeters and the area in square centimeters.

Perimeter
The length around the boundary: add every edge. Measured in a length unit such as cm.In the diagram: the red outline, including both edges of the notch.
Area
The amount of surface inside the boundary. Count the unit tiles that cover it.In the diagram: the blue tiles inside the outline.
Square centimeter (cm²)
The area of a 1 cm by 1 cm square. Area is measured in square units.In the diagram: the yellow tile at the top right.

Predict first

Two tiled cutouts

Predict first. From the original to the enlargement, write four values: the scale factor, one corresponding-side ratio, the perimeter ratio and the area ratio. Identify which are equal. Use the tiles and the outline to explain the difference.

Test your prediction against the cutouts

Scale factor and side ratio. The long top edge gives k = 6 cm ÷ 4 cm = 3/2. Each side uses that same factor.

Perimeter ratio. The edge lengths of the original in centimeters are 4, 2, 2, 2, 2, 4. They add to 16 cm. On the copy they are 6, 3, 3, 3, 3, 6 and add to 24 cm. The perimeter ratio is 24/16 = 3/2. Every term in the sum was multiplied by 3/2, so the sum was too.

Area ratio. Remove the missing corner from each whole grid. The original covers 4 × 4 − 2 × 2 = 12 tiles and the copy covers 6 × 6 − 3 × 3 = 27 tiles. The area ratio is 27/12 = 9/4. The rows get longer and there are more rows. Both dimensions use 3/2, so the area factor is (3/2)2 = 9/4.

If you gave all four ratios as 3/2, find a block of 2 × 2 tiles in the original that scales to a block of 3 × 3 tiles. Use that block to revise your area explanation.

Scaling by k Every length, and so the perimeter, is multiplied by k. The area is multiplied by k2.

For any similar polygon pair. Trace and add the sides to see why the perimeter uses k once. To see why area uses k2, split the original polygon into triangles and use the matching cuts in the copy. Every base and corresponding height scales by k. Each triangle area, and therefore their sum, scales by k2.

Problem type 2

Scale a perimeter: work back from the border

Use this when you know one figure's perimeter, or a strip that goes around it, and need the other figure's perimeter or a missing edge.

Restoring a print frame. The original and the smaller proof frame are similar rectangles. The original long edge is 34 cm and the proof long edge is 25.5 cm. A trim strip exactly 75 cm long went once around the proof frame, with no gap or overlap. For the original frame, a 98 cm strip is available. Goal: decide if the 98 cm strip will cover the original frame, then find the original short edge for the cutting guide.

  1. Read the givens

    Name what the strip measures

    Pproof = 75 cm

    The strip follows the boundary once, so 75 cm is the proof’s perimeter.

  2. Scale factor

    Put the original on top

    k = 34 cm ÷ 25.5 cm = 4/3

    To go from proof to original, put the original long edge on top.

  3. Scale the perimeter

    Multiply the perimeter by k

    Poriginal = (4/3)(75 cm) = 100 cm

    That multiplier applies to every edge and therefore to their sum.

  4. Answer the question

    Compare with the strip

    98 cm < 100 cm

    The 98 cm strip is 2 cm short.

  5. Find the edge

    Work out the short edge

    (100 cm − 2 × 34 cm) ÷ 2 = 16 cm

    The two long edges use 68 cm, leaving 32 cm shared equally by the two short edges.

  6. Verify

    Check with the smaller frame

    (75 cm − 2 × 25.5 cm) ÷ 2 = 12 cm

    Scaling the proof’s short edge back to the original gives (4/3)(12 cm) = 16 cm, the same answer.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Decide what the given number measures.

    75 cm = proof perimeter

    Why?

    The strip went once around the proof frame with no gap or overlap, so its length is the perimeter, the length around the boundary.

  2. Write the scale factor with the figure you want on top.

    k = 34 cm ÷ 25.5 cm = 4/3

    Why?

    A scale factor is target length ÷ matching original length. Here you are going from the proof to the original, so the original long edge is the numerator.

  3. Multiply the known perimeter by k.

    Poriginal = (4/3)(75 cm) = 100 cm

    Why?

    Every edge is multiplied by the same k, and a perimeter is a sum of edges. Every term in the sum was multiplied by k, so the sum was too. Perimeter uses k once.

  4. Compare with what the question asks.

    98 cm < 100 cm: 2 cm short

    Why?

    To cover the frame, the strip must be at least as long as the original perimeter.

  5. Find a missing edge from the perimeter, then verify it with the other frame.

    (100 cm − 2 × 34 cm) ÷ 2 = 16 cm

    Why?

    Opposite edges of a rectangle are equal, so the two long edges use 68 cm and the two short edges share the remaining 32 cm.

    Verify using the smaller frame. The proof’s short edge is (75 cm − 2 × 25.5 cm) ÷ 2 = 12 cm. Scaling that edge back to the original gives (4/3)(12 cm) = 16 cm.

Problem type 3

Scale an area: use the factor twice

Use this when you know one figure's area and a pair of matching lengths, and need the other figure's area without counting.

Use one known area. Suppose you make a third cutout with a 5 cm top edge. You know the original top edge is 4 cm and the original area is 12 cm². Find the area of the third cutout without counting its tiles.

  1. Pair the lengths

    Find k from the top edges

    k = 5 cm ÷ 4 cm = 5/4

    The third cutout is the target, so its top edge goes on top.

  2. Why twice

    A block grows in both directions

    (3/2)2 = 9/4 = 27/12

    In the 4 cm and 6 cm cutouts, a 2 × 2 block of tiles scales to a 3 × 3 block. The rows get longer and there are more rows. Both dimensions use 3/2, so the area uses it twice.

  3. Square it

    Square the scale factor

    k2 = (5/4)2 = 25/16

    Both dimensions of the third cutout scale by 5/4, so its area scales by 5/4 twice.

  4. Scale the area

    Multiply the known area

    Athird = (25/16)(12 cm²) = 18.75 cm²

    Start from the known area, 12 cm², and multiply by k2.

  5. Check

    Check the direction and units

    18.75 cm² > 12 cm²

    The third cutout is larger, so its area must be larger too. Multiplying by 5/4 once would scale a length, not an area.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Find k from a pair of matching lengths, with the target on top.

    k = 5 cm ÷ 4 cm = 5/4

    Why?

    Area questions still start from the side scale factor: target length divided by matching original length, in the same units.

  2. Square the scale factor.

    k2 = (5/4)2 = 25/16

    Why?

    Split the original polygon into triangles and use the matching cuts in the copy. Every base and corresponding height scales by k. Each triangle area, and therefore their sum, scales by k2.

  3. Multiply the known area by k2.

    Athird = (25/16)(12 cm²) = 18.75 cm²

    Why?

    The area ratio is k2, so the new area is k2 times the known area. Areas are in square units such as cm².

  4. Check the direction.

    k > 1, so the area grows

    Why?

    An enlargement has a larger area and a reduction a smaller one. If you applied a side factor once, include what happens to a height as well as a base.

Problem type 4

Decide what the givens guarantee

Use this when a claim is about a whole class of figures: are they always, sometimes or never similar?

Claim to test. Are two rectangular labels always, sometimes or never similar? Three labels to work with: each corner measures 90°, and lengths are in the same unit.

  1. Read the class

    What every rectangle guarantees

    every angle = 90°

    All rectangles have four 90° corners, so the angles always match. The side lengths can vary, so the sides decide.

  2. Make a pair that works

    Test A and B

    10/5 = 2 and 16/8 = 2

    The short sides and the long sides give the same factor. Every angle is 90° and opposite edges repeat, so these checks cover the whole boundary.

  3. Make a counterexample

    Test A and C

    10/5 = 2 but 14/8 = 7/4

    A → C has the same right angles, but the two factors differ.

  4. Rule out a re-pairing

    Short with short, long with long

    no common multiplier

    A common multiplier would keep short edges shorter than long edges, so switching the pairings cannot make the factors equal.

  5. Verdict

    One works, one fails

    sometimes

    A and C are two allowed rectangles that fail the claim. A and B pass. The two cases together justify sometimes.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Write what the class guarantees and what can vary.

    rectangles: angles fixed at 90°, sides vary

    Why?

    For a claim about a whole class, check what the definition fixes and what can vary. Whatever is fixed always matches, so the decision rests on what can vary.

  2. Try to build an allowed pair that works.

    A to B: 10/5 = 16/8 = 2

    Why?

    One similar pair shows the answer cannot be never.

  3. Try to build an allowed pair that fails.

    A to C: 10/5 = 2, but 14/8 = 7/4

    Why?

    A counterexample is one allowed case that shows a proposed general claim can fail. One is enough to rule out always.

  4. Choose the verdict the evidence supports.

    one works, one fails: sometimes

    Why?

    A verdict has to hold for every allowed pair. The working pair rules out never and the failing pair rules out always, so sometimes is the only verdict left.

What each verdict needs

Always
Show why every allowed pair must match. For example, triangles with angles 25°, 75° and 80° match by AA similarity, which says that two matched angles suffice for triangles.
Sometimes
Give an allowed pair that works and an allowed pair that fails.
Never
Show an incompatibility that applies to every allowed pair. A polygon with an inward corner has an interior angle above 180°. A convex polygon has no angle above 180°, so it cannot match that angle.

Try it · G

Three claims about classes

Classify each as always, sometimes or never. Justify each choice with the type of evidence it calls for. Use the properties stated here and sketch a pair if that helps.

  1. Two parallelograms, each with adjacent sides in the ratio 2:1 and an acute angle of 70°.
  2. Two rhombi. A rhombus is a parallelogram with all four sides equal.
  3. A regular hexagon and a hexagon with all angles equal but unequal side lengths. A regular polygon has equal sides and equal angles.
Compare the three justifications
  1. Always. Both have angles alternating 70°, 110°, 70°, 110°. Start at a 70° corner and match the longer adjacent edges. If the short sides are s and t, then the long sides are 2s and 2t. The short-side and long-side ratios both equal t/s, and opposite sides repeat.
  2. Sometimes. Take two rhombi with acute angles 60° and sides 5 and 7 units, respectively. The angle lists match and every side ratio is 7/5. For a pair that fails, keep the first and replace the second with a 75° rhombus of side 7 units. Its angles are 75° and 105°, so matching side ratios alone cannot establish similarity.
  3. Never. Multiplying six equal lengths by one common factor still gives six equal lengths. The other hexagon has unequal sides, so the side condition fails even if its angles match. Such a hexagon is possible: draw lengths 2, 3, 2, 2, 3, 2, turning 60° in the same direction at each corner. The opposite edges cancel in pairs, closing the figure.

If you gave one example for “always,” replace the specific sizes with arbitrary lengths and show what the class guarantees. If you gave one failed pair for “never,” show why the conflict persists for every pair.

Glossary

Similar polygons
Polygons that match exactly after a uniform resizing and any turns, flips or slides needed. All corresponding angles are congruent and all corresponding side lengths share one scale factor.
Corresponding
Matching parts of two figures. For a side, check both endpoints. For an angle, check the vertex and its adjacent sides.
Similarity statement (∼)
Lists corresponding vertices in matching positions. Following the letters traces a boundary and returning to the first letter closes it.
Congruent angles (≅)
Angles with equal degree measures. In ∠R ≅ ∠K, ∠ names the angle and ≅ describes the match.
Proportion
An equality of two ratios. A chain of equal ratios uses the same order throughout.
Scale factor
The positive multiplier from original to target. Divide a target side length by its matching original side length, using the same units.
Perimeter
The sum of the boundary lengths. Use linear units such as cm.
Area
The measure of a region. A square centimeter (cm²) is the area of a 1 cm by 1 cm square.
Counterexample
One allowed case that shows a proposed general claim can fail.
Convex polygon
A polygon whose line segment between any two points in it stays inside or on the boundary.
