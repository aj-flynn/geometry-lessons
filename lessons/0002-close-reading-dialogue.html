A clock repairer's workshop

“This clock is a stubborn mule.”

A clock isn't an animal. What shared quality is the speaker pointing to?

Start there. First work out the meaning. Then ask what the choice of words reveals.

01Meaning first

What does the speaker mean?

Identify who is talking, who is listening, and what is happening. Read the whole sentence and the lines around it before deciding what a phrase means.

Original scene: two repairers are trying to restart a clock.

APPRENTICE: “This clock is a stubborn mule. I have adjusted the gears, but it still will not run.”

REPAIRER: “Then let us check one part at a time.”

Literal paraphrase

Restate the intended meaning in plain language, without the figurative image or added judgments.

Here: The clock still does not work despite the apprentice's adjustments.

Don't mechanically replace each word. Calling the clock an actual mule would miss the meaning.

Interpretation

Explain what the wording might suggest about an attitude, a character, or an idea.

Here: Calling the clock “stubborn” suggests that the apprentice feels frustrated with the repair.

This is an inference. The wording supports it; it does not prove everything about the speaker.

Try three reading lenses

Keep the same line. Change the question you ask about it.

Another original line: “Your plan is a ladder; it gives us a way past each difficulty.”

Plain meaning: The plan provides steps for overcoming difficulties. Interpretation: The speaker seems hopeful about the plan because the ladder image emphasizes progress. It does not establish that the plan will succeed.

New original line: a stagehand says, “The fan swallowed my instructions; nobody heard a word.” Which paraphrase adds no inference?

Compare after trying

The first option preserves the intended meaning: the fan was too noisy for the instructions to be heard. Anger is a possible inference, but it adds an emotion and the unsupported claim that the explanation was important. A fan literally eating words misreads the figurative image.

02Notice the choice

Name the device. Explain its effect.

Figurative language helps a speaker express something beyond a word-for-word literal meaning. For definitions, use the Poetry Foundation glossary. Naming the device is only the start: ask which quality it emphasizes and why that matters here.

Metaphor

Describes one thing as another, creating an implied comparison.

Original line: “Your plan is a ladder.”

The plan offers a way forward, as a ladder offers a way up. The comparison suggests progress.

Simile

Explicitly compares unlike things, often using like or as.

Original line: “The loose spring trembled like a leaf.”

The comparison emphasizes small, quick movements. The spring is not literally a leaf.

Personification

Gives a nonhuman thing a human action, feeling, or quality.

Original line: “The clock scolded us with each tick.”

A clock cannot scold. The human action makes the ticking seem reproachful or demanding.

Hyperbole

Uses deliberate exaggeration for emphasis, not as a factual measurement.

Original line: “I waited a thousand years for that one chime.”

The impossible duration emphasizes how long the wait felt, perhaps showing impatience.

A useful caution: “I like clocks” contains like but makes no comparison. “As the clock rang, we left” uses as to mean when; it is not a simile. Devices can overlap, so explain the actual wording instead of treating labels as a guessing game.

Hint 1 · What should I notice?

Find words that don't make sense as a literal description. What is being compared, given human behavior, or exaggerated?

Hint 2 · Which definition fits?

Ask: Is one thing described as another? Is a comparison made with like or as? Is something nonhuman behaving like a person? Is a quantity deliberately overstated?

Hint 3 · How do I explain the effect?

Try: “The word/image ___ emphasizes ___. In this situation, it makes the speaker sound ___ because ___.” Keep the claim no larger than the evidence.

“The empty chair sulked in the corner.” Which explanation best fits this original line?

Compare after trying

Personification fits: a chair cannot feel resentful or sulk. The word creates a gloomy impression of the empty chair. There is no explicit like/as comparison or exaggerated number.

Quick recall · Three more original lines

Decide before expanding each explanation.

“The spare key was a silver fish in my palm.”

Metaphor: the key is described as a fish. The image might emphasize its small, shiny shape; context would help narrow the effect.

“The curtain drifted like a jellyfish.”

Simile: “like” connects the curtain's movement to the movement of a jellyfish.

“This toolbox weighs a million tons!”

Hyperbole: the enormous weight is a deliberate overstatement that emphasizes how heavy it feels.

03Make the link

A trait needs more than a label.

A trait is a tendency in how someone behaves, such as patience or caution. A feeling is a temporary emotion. One frustrated sentence can show a feeling without proving that the speaker is always impatient.

Original scene: a repair has failed.

APPRENTICE: “I bent the small wheel. I will tell the customer and make a new one.”

REPAIRER: “We will need to explain the delay.”

APPRENTICE: “Then I will explain it myself.”

  1. Claim: name a defensible trait or attitude. “The apprentice acts responsibly in this moment.”
  2. Evidence: point to precise words or an action. The apprentice says, “I will tell the customer and make a new one.”
  3. Reasoning: explain the connection. Admitting the mistake and offering to repair the damage show a willingness to take responsibility.
  4. Limit: stay open to other readings. This moment supports responsibility; it cannot prove that the apprentice is always responsible.

Consider alternatives. Perhaps the apprentice also fears consequences. That possibility doesn't erase the evidence of responsible behavior. It means the motive is less certain than the action. Dialogue may contain boasting, mistakes, jokes, or deception; compare words with actions and other moments.

Build your own explanation, one step at a time

First point to a choice the character makes. Next choose a trait that fits that choice. Then finish: “This suggests ___ because ___.” If the “because” only repeats the trait, explain the behavior more precisely.

Weak: “Responsible because they are responsible.” Stronger: “Responsible because they accept the cost of correcting their own mistake.”

New original line: a mapmaker says, “This route looks shorter, but the label is blurred. I will compare two records before marking it final.” Which claim has the strongest support?

Compare after trying

The third claim connects checking two records with caution about uncertain information. The mapmaker has not refused to travel. Blurred ink does not establish who caused it or whether anyone intended to deceive. Other traits, such as being methodical, could fit if supported by precise reasoning.

04Slow it down

When the wording feels unfamiliar

Shakespeare wrote in Early Modern English. Familiar-looking words can carry older meanings, and word order can feel unusual. Use this routine without turning your reading into a word-by-word guessing exercise. Folger explains these reading challenges.

  1. Set the scene. Who speaks to whom? What does the speaker want? What just happened? Separate spoken dialogue from stage directions.
  2. Follow the sentence. Read through a line break if the sentence continues. Use punctuation and grammar to find the thought; read aloud more than once.
  3. Find the sentence's core. Who or what does what? Locate the subject and main verb, then reconnect phrases that interrupt them. Keep negatives, conditions, and pronoun references.
  4. Resolve one obstacle at a time. Check the edition's gloss or a reliable historical dictionary. Test the definition in context; a modern meaning may not fit.
  5. Paraphrase, then interpret. Restate the meaning simply. Return to the original to notice images, tone, and possible double meanings. Mark uncertainty instead of filling gaps with invented facts.

Small word toolkit

Reference: Shakespeare’s Words on pronouns, verb forms, and other language features.

thou / thee
Both can mean singular “you”: thou as subject; thee as object.
thy / thine
Possessive forms: “your” / “yours”; thine can also appear before a noun, especially before a vowel sound.
art / dost / hath
Older verb forms: “are” with thou, “do” with thou, and “has.”
wherefore
Usually “why,” not “where.” Check what question the speaker is asking.
ere / hence
Ere means “before.” Hence can mean “from here” or “therefore,” depending on context.
'tis / o'er
Compressed forms of “it is” / “over.” Expand them while working out the sentence.

These are starting points, not a replacement for the text's notes. Pronoun choices can also carry social meaning; use the scene to judge that.

Invented old-fashioned sentence: “This small wheel dost thou guard, though it be cracked.”

Hint 1 · Find the subject and verb

“Thou” is the subject. “Dost … guard” is the verb phrase. What is being guarded?

Hint 2 · Restore familiar order

Put the subject and verb before the object. Keep the final “though” clause: it expresses a contrast.

Compare a plain paraphrase of this invented sentence

“You guard this small wheel even though it is cracked.” That preserves who does what and the contrast. Why the person guards it is still uncertain. It might be valuable, useful, or sentimental—but the sentence does not tell us.

In “Wherefore dost thou keep the broken key?”, what should a paraphrase preserve?

Compare after trying

The question asks why the key is kept. “Wherefore” asks for a reason; fear would be an invented reason. This is an original practice sentence, not a line from a play.

Put it together

A fresh scene. Your reading.

Original scene: two gardeners are tending a greenhouse.

GARDENER: “The leaves rustle like paper. Shall we soak every pot?”

ASSISTANT: “Some pots are still damp. I will check the soil in each one before adding water.”

Hint · Keep only the meaning

Focus on the sound. Avoid adding a judgment about the gardener or a conclusion about the plants' health.

Compare your paraphrase

One possible paraphrase: “The leaves make a soft, dry, rustling sound.” It preserves the sound comparison without claiming that the plants are dead. Your wording can differ if it keeps the meaning.

Hint · Name the connection

What two things are compared? Which word makes that comparison explicit? Which sense does the image appeal to?

Compare your explanation

It is a simile: “like” compares the sound of leaves to paper. The comparison emphasizes a dry rustling sound. Naming the device alone leaves out its effect.

Hint · Look at the decision

The assistant chooses to check individual pots rather than treat them all alike. What tendency does that choice suggest? Explain how the choice supports your word.

Compare your reasoning

One supported reading: the assistant acts carefully. “I will check the soil in each one” shows attention to the pots' different conditions before deciding how much water to add. “Observant” or “methodical” could also fit if explained with evidence. The dialogue does not establish that the assistant is always right.

Check your three responses
  • My paraphrase preserves the meaning without adding a motive or outcome.
  • I name a device and explain the effect of specific words.
  • I support a trait with a detail and explain the connection.
  • I distinguish a reasonable inference from something the scene cannot establish.

If one part is missing, revise just that part. Different interpretations can work when the words and reasoning support them.

A close-reading glossary

Open the printable reading reference for the routine, key terms, and a self-check.

Dialogue
Spoken exchanges between characters. Ask who is speaking and who can hear them.
Context
The surrounding words and situation that help determine meaning.
Literal meaning
The ordinary, nonfigurative sense of the words. Literally, a mule is an animal. A plain paraphrase of a figurative statement instead explains its intended meaning.
Paraphrase
A restatement of the intended meaning in your own words, without adding new claims.
Interpretation / inference
An explanation or conclusion drawn from evidence and reasoning. It can be plausible without being certain.
Evidence
A specific textual detail—words, actions, or stage directions—that supports a claim.
Reasoning
The explanation of how the evidence supports the claim.
Characterization
How a text presents a character through description, speech, actions, and others' responses.
Tone
The attitude conveyed by wording and delivery, such as playful, anxious, or dismissive.
Imagery
Language that evokes sensory experience. It can be literal or figurative.
Metaphor / simile
A metaphor describes one thing as another; a simile explicitly compares, often with like or as.
Personification / hyperbole
Personification gives human qualities to something nonhuman; hyperbole exaggerates for emphasis.

Take the routine with you.

Tomorrow, before reopening this lesson, recall the routine and invent one new figurative sentence. A few days later, try the fresh-scene check again without opening its guidance. Can you explain the difference between a paraphrase and an interpretation? Can you name one way to test a character claim?

  1. Read the surrounding dialogue and identify the speaker.
  2. Work out the plain meaning before drawing conclusions.
  3. Notice a language choice and explain its effect.
  4. Support your claim with a precise detail and a “because.”
  5. Check for a different possible reading and keep uncertainty visible.

Return to your own reading and apply the routine independently. If you're stuck, ask a follow-up in the private chat about the step that is difficult.
