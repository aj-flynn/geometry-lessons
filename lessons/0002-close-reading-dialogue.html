Start with one sentence

“The red tag Mara shall not remove till noon.” Who does what, and when does the restriction end? Say it in everyday English before you check.

Check the word order

Mara must leave the red tag in place until noon. Move the object, “the red tag,” after “remove” to hear the usual order. Keep “not” and “till noon”: noon ends the restriction; the line doesn’t tell Mara to remove the tag then.

01 / Worked reading

From a line to a reading

Original scene / A

At the floodgate

A mill worker and an apprentice stand by a closed gate. A bell signals when the water crew is ready.

Nella: The gate thou shalt not lift till the bell ring twice. The river is a fist against it, and thy arm is no shield against that blow.

Ivo: I meant to steady the gate with my arm. I heard one stroke; I’ll wait for the next.

Nella: Stand back with me. The ground here is slick.

The marked sentence begins with the river and turns to the apprentice.

A device is a deliberate way of using language for an effect. First ask whether the words could make sense literally, at face value, in the scene. If the writer makes a nonliteral comparison or stretches ordinary meaning, look for figurative language.

  1. Find and name it. Copy enough of the quotation (the exact words) to support the point you’ll make. “The river is a fist” shows the direct comparison, but leaves out why Nella is warning Ivo personally. To explain that, keep the “no shield” phrase too.
  2. Translate it. To paraphrase is to keep the meaning while changing the words. Nella tells Ivo, “Don’t lift the gate until the bell rings twice. The river is pushing hard against the gate, and your arm can’t protect you from that force.” In the original, thou means “you,” thy means “your,” and shalt helps give the command its force. Read “the gate” as the object of “lift,” and keep “twice.” The warning doesn’t predict the gate will break.
  3. Explain the effect. A fist suggests a blow. The “no shield” clause shows that Nella thinks Ivo could be hurt trying to help. Is she just giving orders? Ivo says he meant to steady the gate with his arm, then agrees to wait. That reply shows the warning changes his plan. The speaker is Nella, the character saying the words; the playwright uses her choice of image to show her concern.

Repair a thin response. “Nella is protective because she says the river is a fist” gives a trait (a way someone tends to act) and an image, but skips the link. Add what she does with that danger: “She says ‘Stand back with me.’ By asking him to move to safety with her, she shows concern for him in this moment.” The image helps us understand the warning; the instruction gives separate evidence for the trait.

Reread the floodgate scene
Look up a device or an older form

Simile: a nonliteral comparison using words such as like or as. “The latch snapped like a firecracker” compares sounds. In “I like the latch,” like just means “enjoy.”

Personification: giving something nonhuman a human action or quality. “The crooked floorboards complained” makes creaks sound like protests. An image can be both personification and a metaphor; explain the human quality if that is what matters.

Hyperbole: exaggeration meant to be heard as exaggeration. “I knocked a thousand times” can stress a long wait; the same number in a count of manufactured parts may be exact.

Older English often uses thou as the subject (“you”), thee as the object (“you”), and thy before a noun (“your”). Thou dost becomes “you do”; she doth becomes “she does.” Find the subject and main action across the whole sentence. Keep limits such as never, unless, and till when you smooth the wording.

02 / Guided practice

Test two readings

Original scene / B

Closing the studio

Sam and Ren are leaving a shared ceramics studio.

Sam: I’ve turned the key. Let’s go.

Ren: Your coat’s still on the hook. I’ll hold the door while you fetch it. Then let me tug the handle; the latch slipped yesterday.

Sam: It clicked this time.

Ren: It clicked yesterday, too.

An inference is a conclusion you draw from clues. Ren may be considerate or careful here. Both can fit. Before you compare, copy a short quotation for each reading. Explain why one detail does a better job than the other of showing how Ren decides whether to leave.

Reread the studio scene

Inspect one claim at a time

Compare after trying

“I’ll hold the door while you fetch it” supports considerate: Ren offers help without being asked. It explains why Ren waits for Sam. For the decision to check the latch, “It clicked yesterday, too” matters more: Ren knows that a click didn’t guarantee the latch held. Pair that reason with “let me tug the handle” to support careful in this scene. If you used Ren’s location as evidence, replace it with the reason Ren wants another test.

Use what you learned from the comparison to revise your explanation. Then try the next scene.

Quick retry

A courier arrives to carry a crate upstairs.

Bea: If thou wouldst take it, test the handle first. The bottom is a trapdoor.

Cal: I’ll carry it from underneath. Put the small box on top; I can make one trip.

Pick one nonliteral phrase and name its device. Put Bea’s full turn into everyday English, then explain the effect of her image. Infer one trait for Cal from the way Cal responds; quote the words that do the most work.

Compare the retry

“The bottom is a trapdoor” is a metaphor. “If you want to take it, test the handle first. The bottom could give way.” Keep both the condition and the order of actions. A trapdoor opens downward, so the image makes the risk of a sudden spill vivid.

Cal’s “I’ll carry it from underneath” suggests resourcefulness: Cal changes how to lift the crate so the weak bottom is supported. “I can make one trip” can support a different claim, such as valuing efficiency. Match your quotation to the trait you chose.

Keep a condition when the order changes

“Had I saved the receipt, I could prove the purchase” means “If I had saved the receipt, I could prove the purchase.” Starting with had can replace if; it does not turn an imagined condition into something that happened. In older dialogue, hadst thou uses the same pattern with “you,” and thou wouldst means “you would.”

Thine can mean “your” before a vowel sound, as in “thine own coat”; on its own, as in “the coat is thine,” it means “yours.” Use the whole sentence to choose the meaning.

03 / Independent reading

One last proof

Original scene / C

The print shop

Merrin and Sal are printing flood-route signs for the town. Sal brings over a revised design. They have time for one test sheet, called a proof, before the full run.

  1. Merrin: The border is a fence about the arrow. Thine eye knows where to find the gate, but a stranger will only see the fence.
  2. Sal: I like the border on the market signs. These aren’t market signs.
  3. Merrin: Hadst thou seen the first proof, thou wouldst know why I kept the border. My little arrow shook like a loose tooth.
  4. Sal: Then cut the border and make the arrow wider. The empty space will do some work for us.
  5. Merrin: I’ve set the thing a hundred times. I can set it once more, if the street name stays under it.
  6. Sal: I’ll take the proof across the street to see if I can read it. The front window makes everything look nearer.
  7. Merrin: Wait. I used last year’s street name. I’ll change it before we waste the ink.
  8. Sal: Use the back of this spoiled sheet for the test. Save the clean paper for the run.

Work on paper or in a private document. Finish both tasks before comparing.

Read the language

  1. Find four examples of figurative language. For each, give the exact quotation and line number, name the device, translate the words into natural English, and explain what the wording adds.
  2. For one image that develops across several phrases, quote enough to show how it changes. Explain what it suggests about one speaker’s attitude toward the other’s work. Test that reading against the other speaker’s reply.
  3. Translate Merrin’s full turn in line 3 in the appropriate entry.

Read the speakers

Infer two distinct traits for Merrin and two for Sal. For each trait, give a short, exact quotation and explain how the wording or action supports that trait in this scene.

Notes

After you’ve tried both

Compare the language entries
1 · “The border is a fence about the arrow. Thine eye knows where to find the gate, but a stranger will only see the fence.”
Metaphor developed across two sentences. “The border makes the arrow hard to find. You know where to look, but someone who hasn’t seen this design before will notice only the border.” The fence is an obstacle; the gate is a way past it. The second sentence shifts the focus from the border itself to what Sal can see that a stranger can’t. Merrin credits Sal’s eye but questions whether the design works for new readers. Sal’s “These aren’t market signs” accepts a difference in purpose, and the suggestion to widen the arrow (4) shows that Sal takes the concern seriously. Neither reply tells us that Sal feels insulted.
3 · “My little arrow shook like a loose tooth.”
Simile. “If you had seen the first test print, you’d know why I kept the border. My little arrow looked wobbly, like a loose tooth.” The comparison makes the arrow’s earlier weakness vivid and helps explain why the border seemed useful at first. “Hadst thou seen” sets an unreal past condition; it doesn’t say Sal saw that proof.
4 · “The empty space will do some work for us.”
Personification. Blank space can help the arrow stand out. Treating it as a helper makes removing ink sound like an active design choice.
5 · “I’ve set the thing a hundred times.”
Likely hyperbole. Merrin says the design has already been reset many times. With no tally in the scene, the round number reads like an expression of fatigue, especially beside “once more.” It makes the decision to try again feel more effortful.

Check your selection: “I like the border” gives a preference, so the word “like” doesn’t make it a simile. For a comparison, name the two things being compared. For any entry, keep the plain meaning and the effect in separate sentences if you find yourself repeating the same point.

Compare the character entries
Merrin · persistent
“I can set it once more, if the street name stays under it” (5). Even after repeated attempts, Merrin agrees to reset the sign, while keeping the name under the arrow. “If” limits the agreement.
Merrin · accountable
“I used last year’s street name. I’ll change it before we waste the ink” (7). Merrin names their own mistake and offers to correct it before printing. The first-person wording matters more for this trait than the fact that they are working at a press.
Sal · attentive to readers
“I’ll take the proof across the street to see if I can read it” (6). Sal tests it where the intended readers will see it, rather than trusting the view from the window.
Sal · resourceful
“Use the back of this spoiled sheet for the test” (8). Sal finds a use for otherwise wasted paper and saves the clean supply.

Different labels can work. If you called Sal creative based on line 4, explain how treating empty space as useful changes the approach to the sign. If you used the same reason twice for one speaker, look for a second action that shows a different tendency.

Take the method back to your reading

  1. Read the whole exchange first. Mark who speaks, who is addressed, and what happens just before and after.
  2. For each figure, find the smallest exact quotation that still contains the cue. Translate it, keeping who acts, when, how often, and under what conditions. Then explain what the original wording adds.
  3. For each trait, underline the words that carry your claim. If those words could fit any character, narrow the claim or find more telling evidence.

Recall it tomorrow

Before rereading the lesson, try this new exchange.

Dee: Don’t ring till I signal. This room is a drum.

Yan: I’ll wrap the clapper first; the little ones just fell asleep.

Name the device, translate Dee’s lines, and explain what the image adds. Then choose an exact quotation and infer one trait for Yan.

Compare after recalling

“This room is a drum” is a metaphor for how strongly sound carries here. Dee asks Yan not to ring before the signal; the drum image makes the noise feel hard to contain. Yan says “I’ll wrap the clapper first,” suggesting thoughtfulness about the sleepers: Yan acts to muffle the sound instead of merely waiting.

Glossary

Device
A choice about how to use language to create an effect.
Literal
Using a word or expression in its direct, ordinary sense.
Figurative language
Language used beyond its literal meaning, such as through comparison or exaggeration.
Metaphor
A comparison that describes one thing in terms of another without using like or as to set up the comparison.
Quotation
Exact words copied from a source. Keep the speaker and enough context to show what the words refer to.
Paraphrase
A restatement in your own words that preserves the meaning, including its limits.
Speaker
The voice saying the words. In dialogue, a character is usually the speaker. A narrator tells the story; the playwright wrote the script.
Trait
A tendency in how someone behaves or thinks. A short exchange can suggest a trait without proving it holds everywhere.
Simile
A nonliteral comparison signaled by wording such as like or as.
Personification
Describing something nonhuman as though it had a human action or quality.
Hyperbole
Deliberate exaggeration for emphasis or effect.
Inference
A conclusion drawn from clues that the text doesn’t state directly.
