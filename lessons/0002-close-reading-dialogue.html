What a line lets you infer

Put older dialogue into today’s English. Then use the writer’s exact words to explain what an image does or what a character’s choices reveal.

Before you start

In “Did you lock the cabinet?” is the speaker asking about a fact or stating one? Hold on to that difference when you rewrite a line.

01 Language

Keep the meaning. Explain the image.

Start with who speaks and who hears. A speaker says the line, a character is a person in the story, a narrator tells the story, and the author writes it. A character’s judgment belongs to that character unless the text gives you reason to attribute it elsewhere.

In older dialogue, thou is you as in you listen, thee is you as in I hear you or with you, and thy means your. Thou art means you are. Dost thou trust? puts the helping verb first, just as Do you trust? does today. Rewrite in two passes. First work out the words and their order. Then say the thought naturally, keeping any question, condition, uncertainty or negative.

Figurative language adds meaning through moves such as comparison or exaggeration. When you spot like or as, check for a comparison. “The labels curled like dry leaves” is a simile. “I like those labels” uses like to mean enjoy. If a thing acts like a person, as in “The hinges protested,” look for personification. If a speaker stretches the facts for emphasis, as in “That queue took a century,” look for hyperbole. Devices can overlap. Explain what the particular wording does.

Try the same image with two different clues

Imagine a different line just before “This ledger is a sieve.” In each version, predict what is slipping through and what the book fails to keep.

Several deliveries are missing from the record.

The missing information is delivery entries. The image makes the book seem incomplete. You would want to check the count before relying on it.

Strangers can read the details in the record.

Here information escapes to people outside the counting room. The same holes suggest weak privacy. A book could have every entry and still let details leak.

Check your reading

[A mapmaker looks at a rough draft.]

Mapmaker   If thou dost use that sketch, wilt thou lose thy way? The ink has swallowed the footpaths.

Wilt thou means will you. First rewrite both sentences aloud and identify the device in the second sentence. Then pick the explanation that keeps the meaning and explains the image.

Which explanation fits both sentences?

Compare the three readings

The middle reading keeps the question and the condition. “The ink has swallowed the footpaths” makes a metaphor by treating ink like something that can swallow. In context the paths on the page are obscured. The first reading turns a question into a certainty. The last reading moves the effect from the page to the real paths.

Retry with one changed clue

Suppose the mapmaker asks “Wilt thou lose thy way even if thou dost leave that sketch here?” Say the new question in modern English first.

Compare the retry

“Will you get lost even if you leave that sketch here?” The speaker now asks whether leaving the sketch behind would still result in getting lost. Keep even if and keep the question.

02 Character

Build a claim the words can carry

A trait is a tendency in how a character behaves. A character can be annoyed for a minute and show a tendency to share responsibility through what they choose to do. An inference is a conclusion you draw from clues. Say what the scene supports, then stop where the evidence stops.

  1. Claim. The binder shows accountability in this exchange. That means accepting responsibility for something they did.
  2. Evidence. Quote “Put my initials on the repair slip.” The split gives context, while the instruction records who will take responsibility.
  3. Reason. By asking for their own initials, the binder makes their role in the repair visible. “The cover split” alone would show damage without showing who accepts responsibility.
  4. Test an alternative. The instruction could sound bossy because it is an order. A single short order during a repair gives limited evidence of a controlling habit. The phrase “my initials” gives more direct evidence for accountability here.

For a second trait, look for a different choice and quote the detail that supports it. If both explanations make the same point, combine them and find another clue. Brackets in a script can hold stage directions, which describe actions or setting outside the spoken dialogue.

Check your claim

[A team is about to hang a mural.]

Installer   I brought a spare hook. Show me how you’d hang it. We can test both methods on this board.

Find the shortest continuous quotation that shows the installer is open to another person’s idea. Say how the words support the trait before choosing.

Which quotation gives the most direct evidence?

Compare the evidence

The invitation “Show me how you’d hang it.” is enough to support the claim. The spare hook supports being prepared. The whole speech includes both clues, which makes it less precise for the particular claim about openness.

Retry with a different claim

Find the shortest useful quote for “The installer wants a fair comparison.” Give a reason.

Compare the retry

“We can test both methods on this board.” The installer proposes comparing both approaches using the same surface. That makes the conditions more alike.

03 Transfer

Read a new scene

This original script mixes a few older turns of phrase into a scene about community radio. The team is checking an audio clip before a broadcast. Read the whole scene, then draft your responses on paper. Choose the lines and traits yourself before opening either comparison.

[Kemi and Dax listen to a clip. The last words are hard to hear.]

Kemi   The hiss comes in like sand under a door. Canst thou hear the last word?

Dax   Nay. That switch refuses to listen. I have pressed it a thousand times since breakfast!

[Dax tries the switch again. Kemi checks the test sheet.]

Kemi   Twice, according to the sheet. Mark the spot where the hiss starts. We can replay just that part.

Dax   My shortcut was a dead end. I’ll show the next shift what I tried so they can skip it.

Canst thou means can you. Nay means no.

Explain the language

Choose two different figures of speech. For each, copy just enough to capture the device, name it, rewrite the line in modern English and explain which detail in the figure changes what you picture or understand. For at least one response, explain how the words shape the exchange between the coworkers.

Explain the people

For each character, write two distinct traits you can defend. For each trait, include an exact quotation and a sentence that connects a particular word or choice to the claim. If you have two possible labels, test which one the words support more directly.

Compare the language after writing
  • “The hiss comes in like sand under a door.” Simile. “A hissing noise gets into the recording. Can you hear the last word?” The sand image makes the intrusion feel hard to keep out. Kemi’s question invites Dax to test how much it matters.
  • “That switch refuses to listen.” Personification. “That switch won’t respond.” The verb refuses makes a technical problem feel like someone is choosing to ignore Dax. That helps explain the frustration in the reply.
  • “I have pressed it a thousand times since breakfast!” Hyperbole. “I’ve pressed it over and over since breakfast!” The count on the sheet gives a reason to read the number as exaggeration. The gap between the numbers makes Dax’s effort sound prolonged and frustrating.
  • “My shortcut was a dead end.” Metaphor. “My quicker method didn’t get us anywhere.” A dead end stops progress along a route. Dax uses the image to explain why the next shift should skip the method.

For a retry, take a figure you skipped. Write the plain meaning first, then add a separate sentence about what the original image contributes.

Compare the character claims after writing
  • Kemi, precise. “Twice, according to the sheet.” Kemi checks the exact count and names the source.
  • Kemi, methodical. “Mark the spot where the hiss starts.” Kemi breaks the problem into a particular segment the team can replay.
  • Dax, persistent. “Dax tries the switch again.” This direction records an action after earlier attempts. The inflated count would be weaker evidence of the number of tries.
  • Dax, helpful to teammates. “I’ll show the next shift what I tried so they can skip it.” The phrase “so they can skip it” gives a reason for sharing the failed method.

Other claims can fit. For instance, frustrated explains Dax’s outburst in the moment. To support a broader trait, look for a repeated choice or name the limit of the evidence. For a retry, find a different phrase for one claim. If it loses the reason that supports your claim, keep the stronger quotation.

Take the method to another text

When the wording is unfamiliar, first find the speaker, the action and any question or condition. When the words are clear but their effect is unclear, find the image or action that changes what you infer. Explain why you chose that starting point for one line in the radio scene.

On your own draft, put your exact quotation next to the original line and check the speaker and wording. Check whether the plain meaning keeps the question, condition or uncertainty. Underline the word your analysis depends on and ask whether you have explained what it contributes. For two traits, point to two different choices. Use a narrower claim if the scene shows only one moment.

Next time, before reopening this lesson, write the three steps from memory: what you observe, what the words mean and what the words let you infer. Try them on a line you have never read.

Glossary

Observation
Something you can point to directly in the wording or directions.
Quotation
Words copied exactly from the text.
Plain meaning
What the speaker says, expressed in familiar words.
Condition
A circumstance on which a question, prediction or claim depends, often introduced by if.
Figurative language
Language that uses images, exaggeration or other moves beyond the ordinary literal meaning.
Device
A technique the writer uses to create an effect.
Metaphor and simile
A metaphor treats one thing as another. A simile marks the comparison with a word such as like or as.
Personification and hyperbole
Personification gives a thing a human action or quality. Hyperbole exaggerates for emphasis.
Interpretation
An explanation of what a detail suggests or does in its context.
Inference and trait
An inference is a conclusion drawn from clues. A trait is a tendency in how a character behaves.
Stage direction
A note in the script about an action, setting or way of speaking. Bracketed directions here are separate from dialogue.
Older forms in this lesson
Thou and thee are forms of you. Thy means your. Art, dost, wilt and canst go with thou. Nay means no.
