Three reading moves

Read dialogue
closely.

Find a line worth quoting. Put it in plain English. Explain what the words show about the speaker.

Dialogue is an exchange of spoken words between characters. Read for meaning, notice how the wording works, and use details to support a claim. Try one section at a time. Every practice passage here is an original invention.

New to close reading? Try a small first check.

An observation reports what the text states. An inference uses a detail to reach a conclusion.

Original line. “The tailor folded the cloth twice before cutting.” Is “The tailor folds twice” or “The tailor wants to avoid a mistake” directly stated? Decide before opening the comparison.

Compare and choose your starting point

Folding twice is stated. Wanting to avoid a mistake is a possible inference about a motive. Other motives could fit. If this distinction feels new, begin with “Find the meaning.” If it feels clear, try its practice check first.

Which step feels unclear? Think about meaning, language choice, and evidence. Keep that question in mind, or ask it in the private chat.

Meaning first

What does the speaker mean?

Context means the surrounding words and situation. Identify who is talking, who is listening, and what is happening. Read the whole sentence and the lines around it before deciding what a phrase means.

Original scene. Two repairers are trying to restart a clock.

APPRENTICE: “This clock is a stubborn mule. I have adjusted the gears, but it still will not run.”

REPAIRER: “Then let us check one part at a time.”

Plain paraphrase

A paraphrase puts the intended meaning in your own words. Keep the speaker’s meaning and any uncertainty, limits, or conditions.

Here. The apprentice tried to get the clock going by adjusting its gears, but it still does not work.

Don't mechanically replace each word. Calling the clock an actual mule would miss the meaning.

Interpretation

An interpretation explains what the wording might suggest about an attitude, a character, or an idea.

Here. Calling the clock “stubborn” suggests that the apprentice feels frustrated with the repair.

This is an inference, a conclusion drawn from evidence. The wording supports that reading. The speaker’s exact feelings are still uncertain.

Keep the words. Change the question.

“Your plan is a ladder. It gives us a way past each difficulty.”

A metaphor describes one thing as another to make a comparison. Here the plan is described as a ladder. Choose a question, say your answer, then compare.

Reading-lens comparison for paper or without JavaScript

Original line. “Your plan is a ladder. It gives us a way past each difficulty.” First find the image, restate its meaning, and infer an attitude.

Compare the three readings

Words: “Your plan is a ladder” is a metaphor. Plain meaning: the plan provides steps for overcoming difficulties. Interpretation: the speaker seems hopeful because the ladder image emphasizes progress. The plan's eventual success is still uncertain.

New original line: a stagehand says, “The fan swallowed my instructions. Nobody heard a word.” Which paraphrase adds no inference?

Compare after trying

The first option preserves the intended meaning. The fan was too noisy for the instructions to be heard. Anger is a possible inference, but it adds an emotion and the unsupported claim that the explanation was important. A fan literally eating words misreads the figurative image.

Optional · When a sentence uses older wording

Read through a line break when the sentence continues. Find the subject, the person or thing the sentence is about, and the main verb, its central action or state. Put the sentence in familiar order. If a word is still unclear, check your edition’s gloss, a short word note, and test that meaning in the sentence.

Invented old-fashioned example. “This torn map shall I keep, though the rain has blurred it.” Say who will do what and preserve the contrast. Then try the same move on “Should the mist lift, the road shall we see.” What condition must stay?

Compare both sentences

“I’ll keep this torn map even though the rain has blurred it.” The second sentence means “If the fog clears, we’ll see the road.” The sentence makes the result depend on a condition. It leaves open whether the fog will clear.

Notice the choice

Find a line worth quoting.

Figurative language uses words beyond their ordinary, literal meaning. Scan a few lines for a comparison, a surprising action, or a deliberate exaggeration. A quotation copies the speaker’s exact words. Copy enough to show what the comparison refers to.

Metaphor

Describes one thing as another to make a comparison. “Your plan is a ladder.” A ladder gives a way up, so the plan sounds like a way forward.

Simile

Compares unlike things explicitly, often using like or as. “The loose spring trembled like a leaf.” The leaf helps us picture small, quick movements.

Personification

Gives a nonhuman thing a human action, feeling, or quality. “The clock scolded us with each tick.” Scolding makes the ticking seem like a repeated criticism.

Hyperbole

Exaggerates deliberately for emphasis. “I waited a thousand years for that chime.” The impossible duration makes the wait feel very long.

Test what the words are doing. In “I like clocks,” like means “enjoy.” In “As the clock rang, we left,” as means “when.” A large number can also be an actual count. Some images fit more than one device. Explain the wording that makes your choice fit.

Original scene. Museum workers are moving a stone sculpture.

NOOR: “The crate has six handles.”

CAL: “I like the padded grips.”

NOOR: “This staircase is a bottleneck. We can move only one crate at a time.”

Which line gives you something to explain beyond its literal wording? Find the shortest complete quotation that shows the image and what it describes. Then say what the image adds.

Compare a worked reading
In this rehearsal scene, which quotation and explanation show what the wording adds?

LUIS: “The rehearsal starts at six. I like the side entrance.”

MAE: “The hallway is a drum. The actors will hear every footstep.”

Compare after trying

Mae describes the hallway as a drum. The shared quality is how loudly a small impact can sound. “The actors will hear every footstep” connects that sound to a possible disruption. There is no evidence that the actors keep time to the footsteps. Luis expresses a preference.

Quick retry. A field reporter says, “The wind barged into the recording. You can hardly hear my interview.” Choose a quotation. Explain what the action suggests beyond “the wind is audible.”

Compare the effect

“The wind barged into the recording” uses personification. To barge in is to enter abruptly, like an uninvited person interrupting a conversation. The wind sounds intrusive enough to make the interview hard to hear.

Make the link

A trait needs more than a label.

A trait is a tendency in how someone behaves, such as patience or caution. A feeling is a temporary emotion. One frustrated sentence can show a feeling. A trait claim grows stronger when more than one moment supports it. Evidence is a specific detail that helps you test a claim.

Original scene. A repair has failed.

APPRENTICE: “I bent the small wheel. I will tell the customer and make a new one.”

REPAIRER: “We will need to explain the delay.”

APPRENTICE: “Then I will explain it myself. Let’s set the old wheel beside the new one and compare each tooth before we put the new one in.”

  1. Claim: name a defensible trait or attitude. “The apprentice acts responsibly in this moment.”
  2. Evidence: point to precise words or an action. The apprentice says, “I will tell the customer and make a new one.”
  3. Reasoning: explain the connection. Admitting the mistake and offering to repair the damage show a willingness to take responsibility.
  4. Limit: stay open to other readings. This moment supports responsibility. It takes more evidence to show that the apprentice is always responsible.

To support a second trait, look for a different choice. “Compare each tooth before we put the new one in” supports methodical, meaning careful about following a step-by-step process. Checking each tooth before fitting the wheel shows that process. Offering to tell the customer supports responsibility for a mistake. Each claim has its own reason.

When you read an exchange, track what each speaker does with the other’s words. The repairer raises the delay. The apprentice offers to explain it and proposes a check. The reply shows how they divide up a problem. If you want to say how two speakers work together, point to a line from each.

Test a claim against new context

Original scene. A ceramics club is packing an exhibition.

PACKER: “Leave your bowls here. I will wrap them after I finish mine.”

PARTNER: “That will take your whole afternoon.”

PACKER: “Then I will stay until they are safe.”

Predict: which words best support “The packer is willing to help”? Explain why those words are stronger evidence than the partner's comment. Could helping and wanting praise both be possible?

Compare the evidence

“I will stay until they are safe” commits the packer’s time to protecting someone else's bowls. The partner's comment describes the cost, but it is the packer who chooses to accept it. This supports helpful behavior in this moment without proving a purely generous motive.

Now change just the next stage direction. A stage direction describes action or delivery rather than words spoken aloud. Predict how each version would change your confidence about the packer's motive.

Explain the change: which claim stays supported in both versions? Which claim needs more caution?

Compare both contexts after predicting

Quiet version: the packer stays after everyone leaves and carefully wraps the bowls. Helping happens without immediate public praise, strengthening a generous reading. Private motives remain uncertain.

Audience version: when visitors enter, the packer calls out, “See how much I do for this club!” Wanting recognition now has direct support. The packer can want praise and still help. The action and the motive are different questions.

In both: accepting the work supports helpful behavior. A claim about an entirely selfless motive needs more caution. Keep the original evidence when you revise a claim in light of new context.

Consider alternatives. The apprentice might also fear consequences. A claim about responsible behavior can still fit the choice to fix the mistake. The private motive is less certain. Compare what characters promise with what they do.

Build your own explanation, one step at a time

First point to a choice the character makes. Next choose a trait that fits that choice. Try completing “This suggests ___ because ___.” If the “because” only repeats the trait, explain the behavior more precisely.

Weak: “Responsible because they are responsible.” Stronger: “Responsible because they accept the cost of correcting their own mistake.”

New original line: a mapmaker says, “This route looks shorter, but the label is blurred. I will compare two records before marking it final.” Which claim has the strongest support?

Compare after trying

The third claim connects a specific decision—checking before finalizing—with caution about uncertain information. A shorter route makes speed a plausible interest, but checking is the action the mapmaker chooses. Comparing records does not establish distrust of their makers. The stated concern is a blurred label. Other traits, such as being methodical, could fit with precise reasoning.

Quick retry. After a display case sticks, a museum worker says, “I’ll test every latch in order. Give me the step stool and I’ll show you how the release works.” Pick one line or phrase for being methodical and another for being willing to teach. Explain how the two quotations do different work.

Compare the two links

“I’ll test every latch in order” shows a planned sequence of checks. “I’ll show you how the release works” is an offer to demonstrate the process to someone else. The first supports a claim about method. The second supports a claim about how the worker treats a learner.

Put it together

A fresh scene. Your reading.

Try the scene aloud, on paper, or in the optional boxes. Notes stay on this page and disappear when you reload. Open the comparisons after making your own attempt.

Original scene. Two volunteers prepare a radio broadcast.

OPERATOR: “The silence is a locked door. No signal is getting through.”

HOST: “The green light is on. We must be ready.”

OPERATOR: “The light was on during yesterday’s failure too. I unplugged a cable before lunch. I’ll tell the crew I might have caused the delay.”

HOST: “Then I’ll record a test before we go live. Let’s ask the new volunteer to run the next test with me so she can learn the controls.”

Hint · Find the shared quality

After you find an image, ask which part of it matters in this scene. Then check the next sentence.

Compare a reading

“The silence is a locked door” uses a metaphor. The operator means the equipment is failing to send a signal. A locked door blocks entry. The image makes the failed connection sound like a barrier to getting the broadcast started. The host’s reply treats the green light as a sign that everything is ready. The two speakers are paying attention to different clues.

Hint · Gather choices before naming traits

For each person, mark two things they notice, do, or say they’ll do. Explain what each detail shows. If both answers give the same reason, look for a different kind of choice.

Compare your reasoning

Operator. “The light was on during yesterday’s failure too” suggests attentiveness because the operator remembers when the same signal gave misleading information. “I’ll tell the crew I might have caused the delay” suggests accountability because the operator volunteers to disclose a possible mistake. The cause of the delay is still uncertain.

Host. “Then I’ll record a test before we go live” suggests a willingness to reconsider because the host changes course after hearing new evidence. “Let’s ask the new volunteer to run the next test with me so she can learn the controls” suggests a willingness to teach because the host offers the newcomer a chance to try with support. The scene ends with a proposal, so check what the host actually does before drawing a stronger conclusion.

Other traits can fit when you explain a precise link. Read each claim beside its quotation. If the two links for a speaker sound the same, try a different quotation or trait.

Your next move

Original scene. Two volunteers set up a book swap.

LEA: “We have enough boxes for today.”

IVO: “Only if the extra books arrive tomorrow.”

LEA: “I’ll bring a spare box in case it comes early.”

A reader writes “Ivo says the extra books arrive tomorrow.”

Hint · Test the sentence against the whole reply

What does Ivo assume would have to happen for Lea’s statement to hold? What is still unknown?

Compare your choice after trying

Start by checking whether the response preserves Ivo’s meaning. “Only if” makes having enough boxes depend on when the extra books arrive. A closer reading is “Ivo thinks there will be enough boxes today only if the extra books arrive tomorrow.” Lea’s offer to bring a spare makes sense because an early delivery is still possible.

A close-reading glossary

Open the printable reading reference for the routine, key terms, and a self-check.

Observation
Something the text directly states or shows, separated from conclusions about its meaning.
Stage direction
A description of action or delivery, not spoken dialogue.
Trait / feeling
A tendency in behavior / a temporary emotion. One moment does not prove a permanent trait.
Subject / main verb
Who or what a sentence is about / its central action or state.
Gloss
A short explanation of a word in an edition’s notes.
Dialogue
Spoken exchanges between characters. Ask who is speaking and who can hear them.
Context
The surrounding words and situation that help determine meaning.
Literal meaning
The ordinary, nonfigurative sense of the words. Literally, a mule is an animal. A plain paraphrase of a figurative statement instead explains its intended meaning.
Paraphrase
A restatement of the intended meaning in your own words, without adding new claims.
Interpretation / inference
An explanation or conclusion drawn from evidence and reasoning. It can be plausible without being certain.
Evidence
A specific textual detail—words, actions, or stage directions—that supports a claim.
Reasoning
The explanation of how the evidence supports the claim.
Quotation
The exact words copied from the text, with enough context to preserve their meaning.
Tone
The attitude conveyed by wording and delivery, such as playful, anxious, or dismissive.
Imagery
Language that evokes sensory experience. It can be literal or figurative.
Metaphor / simile
A metaphor describes one thing as another. A simile explicitly compares, often with like or as.
Personification / hyperbole
Personification gives human qualities to something nonhuman. Hyperbole exaggerates for emphasis.

I will give it time to grow.” Paraphrase it, explain the image, and infer an attitude with a limit before consulting your notes. Can you explain the difference between a paraphrase and an interpretation? Can you name one way to test a character claim?

  1. Read the surrounding dialogue and identify the speaker. Mark candidate lines and choose the smallest complete quotation that supports your point.
  2. Work out the plain meaning before drawing conclusions.
  3. Notice a language choice and explain its effect.
  4. Support your claim with a precise detail and a “because.”
  5. For a second claim about the same character, look for a different choice or reaction. Check that each quotation supports the specific claim beside it.
