Read particle recipes

Count atoms by separating the recipe for one particle from the number of particles.

Learn one method at a time. Stop after a check if you need a break; return to your own work after the exit explanation. Open the glossary or use the quick reference.

Start with one particle

Start by separating a recipe from the number of copies made from it. In this lesson, an atom is one unit of an element, and a particle is the particular unit we are counting. Symbols let us track different atom types without drawing every atom. An element is a substance whose atoms share one atomic identity. A chemical symbol begins with a capital letter and may include a lowercase letter: Si is one symbol, not S followed by i.

Our fictional recipe Q3R2 means three Q tokens and two R tokens inside one group. Q and R are invented labels, not real elements. Small lower numbers are subscripts; a missing subscript means one. This model teaches counting, not whether such a substance exists.

A large number in front is a coefficient: here it counts complete copies and multiplies every count inside the group. Changing copies preserves the recipe; changing a subscript changes the recipe. Before using real chemistry notation, decide what one counted unit represents.

Check the unit first

Before moving on, explain what one complete group means. If that feels unclear, draw a box around one recipe and count only inside that box.

A museum model keeps the same total number of tokens but redistributes them: each group now contains four Q and one R. What changed?

Compare reasoning after an attempt

Yes. The total can stay fixed while composition changes. Compare the counts within one group.

Predict → add a group → explain

Before playing: when one complete group is added, will both kinds of atom increase? Will the recipe inside a group change?

Reveal the model stages after predicting
  1. One Q₃R₂ group contains 3 Q and 2 R: 5 atoms altogether.
  2. Two identical groups contain 6 Q and 4 R: 10 atoms altogether.
  3. Three identical groups contain 9 Q and 6 R: 15 atoms altogether.

Make a prediction, then reveal the stages or use Next step.

Explain why the ratio of Q to R stays the same even as the total grows.

Count first, then compare types

Look at two separate features: how many atoms are connected in one particle, and whether their symbols match. A sketch A—A contains two matching symbols; A—B contains two different symbols. Those observations answer different questions. Neither sketch alone tells you what a real element normally does.

For an unfamiliar element, find its elemental form in a reliable reference for the stated conditions. Then count the connected atoms and compare their symbols. A single symbol in a periodic table identifies an element; it is not a complete particle drawing.

Scientific prefixes are useful clues to counts. For example, “tri-” signals three in many terms. Use the prefix and your course reference to choose the word for a particle’s atom count independently. We will not list elemental pairs here.

Some substances consist of separate molecules (bounded groups of bonded atoms). Others form extended crystals: ordered arrangements that repeat through a solid. An ion is an atom or group with a net electric charge. For an ionic crystal, the written recipe records a repeating ratio rather than a separate molecule. Ask what the notation is describing before counting.

Your turn: a different recipe

A fictional display contains four identical groups, each with two J tokens and five L tokens. Count just the L tokens across the display.

Hint 1: Notice

Find the number of complete groups.

Hint 2: Recall

Each group contributes the same five L tokens.

Hint 3: Choose a strategy

Write “number of groups × L tokens per group,” then calculate independently.

Compare reasoning after an attempt

Yes: four groups each contribute five L tokens. Explain why J is not included.

A new diagram shows a connected pair of matching atom symbols. What can the drawing establish?

Compare reasoning after an attempt

Yes. Matching symbols indicate the same atom type, and the pair contains two atoms. The drawing does not establish its usual natural form.

From a drawing to a recipe

Use the same method with real symbols. This original schematic shows one chloroethane particle. C labels carbon, H labels hydrogen, and Cl labels chlorine. The lowercase l stays with C: Cl is a different symbol from C.

Make a three-row inventory on paper before choosing. Then write one compact recipe and explain how you handled the two-letter symbol and the absent subscript.

Which compact recipe matches this one drawn particle?

Compare reasoning after an attempt

Yes. The single drawn particle has two C labels, five H labels, and one Cl label. One chlorine needs no printed subscript. The leading coefficient would count complete copies, not one atom type.

Now reverse the task: without looking at the picture, use your chosen recipe to rebuild the three-row inventory. If you made three identical copies, which number would change in the notation, and which counts inside a copy would stay fixed?

Compare your method after trying both directions

Reading back should recover two carbon atoms, five hydrogen atoms, and one chlorine atom per particle. Three copies change the coefficient, while the subscripts stay fixed. This checks that your symbol reading and your choice of counted unit agree.

Exit check: explain a new case

A model has two kinds of atom. One display doubles the number of complete particles; another changes the recipe inside every particle. Which action can change the substance represented? Explain how you would check without doing any assigned problem.

Check your method after making an attempt

Track two separate records: the atom recipe within one particle and the number of copies. Changing only copies preserves the recipe; changing the recipe can represent a different substance.

If this is difficult, return to the model, make one change, and describe what stays the same. Then try the exit check again without looking.

Glossary: tools for thinking

Element / molecule / ion / crystal
An element has one atomic identity. A molecule is a bounded group of bonded atoms. An ion has net charge. A crystal is an ordered repeating solid arrangement.
Atom / symbol
An atom is a unit of an element. A symbol is a compact label used to represent a type.
Particle / group
Particle is a general word for a counted unit; state whether you mean an atom, a molecule, or an ion. A group in our token model contains the recipe once.
Subscript / coefficient
A subscript is a small lower number attached to a symbol. A coefficient is a multiplier before the complete recipe.
Ratio
A comparison of quantities, such as three Q tokens for every two R tokens.
Prefix
A word part placed at the beginning that can provide a clue to meaning.

Use your course reference to connect these prerequisite ideas to the scientific terms you are learning. Choose the final wording in your own work.
