Read a formula as a particle recipe

Count atoms by separating the recipe for one particle from the number of particles.

Use these original models to learn a method, then try your own work independently. Open the glossary or use the quick reference.

Start with one particle

Start by separating a recipe from the number of copies made from it. In this lesson, an atom is one unit of an element, and a particle is the particular unit we are counting. Symbols let us track different atom types without drawing every atom.

Our fictional recipe Q3R2 means three Q tokens and two R tokens inside one group. Q and R are invented labels, not real elements. Small lower numbers are subscripts; a missing subscript means one. This model teaches counting, not whether such a substance exists.

A large number in front is a coefficient: it counts complete copies and multiplies every count inside the group. Changing copies preserves the recipe; changing a subscript changes the recipe. Before using real chemistry notation, decide what one counted unit represents.

Predict → add a group → explain

Before playing: when one complete group is added, will both kinds of atom increase? Will the recipe inside a group change?

Reveal the model stages after predicting
  1. One Q₃R₂ group contains 3 Q and 2 R: 5 atoms altogether.
  2. Two identical groups contain 6 Q and 4 R: 10 atoms altogether.
  3. Three identical groups contain 9 Q and 6 R: 15 atoms altogether.

Make a prediction, then reveal the stages or use Next step.

Explain why the ratio of Q to R stays the same even as the total grows.

Count first, then compare types

Look at two separate features: how many atoms are connected in one particle, and whether their symbols match. A sketch A—A contains two matching symbols; A—B contains two different symbols. Those observations answer different questions. Neither sketch alone tells you what a real element normally does.

For an unfamiliar element, find its elemental form in a reliable reference for the stated conditions. Then count the connected atoms and compare their symbols. A single symbol in a periodic table identifies an element; it is not a complete particle drawing.

Scientific prefixes are useful clues to counts. For example, “tri-” signals three in many terms. Use the prefix and your course reference to choose the word for a particle’s atom count independently. We will not list elemental pairs here.

Some substances consist of separate molecules (bounded groups of bonded atoms). Others form extended crystals. For an ionic crystal, the written recipe records a repeating ratio rather than a separate molecule. Ask what the notation is describing before counting.

Exit check: explain a new case

A model has two kinds of atom. One display doubles the number of complete particles; another changes the recipe inside every particle. Which action can change the substance represented? Explain how you would check without doing any assigned problem.

Check your method after making an attempt

Track two separate records: the atom recipe within one particle and the number of copies. Changing only copies preserves the recipe; changing the recipe can represent a different substance.

If this is difficult, return to the model, make one change, and describe what stays the same. Then try the exit check again without looking.

Glossary: tools for thinking

Atom / symbol
An atom is a unit of an element. A symbol is a compact label used to represent a type.
Particle / group
Particle is a general word for a counted unit; state whether you mean an atom, a molecule, or an ion. A group in our token model contains the recipe once.
Subscript / coefficient
A subscript is a small lower number attached to a symbol. A coefficient is a multiplier before the complete recipe.
Ratio
A comparison of quantities, such as three Q tokens for every two R tokens.
Prefix
A word part placed at the beginning that can provide a clue to meaning.

Use your course reference to connect these prerequisite ideas to the scientific terms you are learning. Choose the final wording in your own work.
