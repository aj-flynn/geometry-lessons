Words to know

Keep two electron ledgers

Imagine two atoms joined by one line. Predict how many electrons this adds to the whole drawing and how many it puts around each atom.

Check the entry skill

The whole drawing contains two electrons in that pair. Both atoms have those same two electrons around them.

A Lewis electron dot structure records where the outer electrons are counted. A skeleton shows which atoms link. A line is one shared pair. Two dots beside one atom are a lone pair, a pair assigned to that atom alone. The starting stock is the number of valence electrons, the outer electrons counted in this model.

Skeleton
Which atoms link, before any pairs are counted.In the diagram: H joins C, and C joins N. H and N do not touch.
Shared pair (a line)
One pair of electrons shared by the two atoms the line joins.In the diagram: the red lines: one between H and C, three between C and N.
Bond multiplicity
The number of lines on one connection: single, double or triple.In the diagram: H–C is single (1 line); C–N is triple (3 lines).
Lone pair
Two dots beside one atom, assigned to that atom alone.In the diagram: the blue pair beside N.
Local ledger
The electrons around one atom, compared with the octet check. A line counts for both atoms it joins.In the diagram: the shaded circle: C sees 2 + 6 = 8. N sees 6 + 2 = 8; H sees 2.
Global ledger
Every electron in the drawing, each line counted once. It must equal the total of valence electrons from the formula.In the diagram: the panel: 8 in lines + 2 in dots = 10, the same as H 1 + C 4 + N 5.

Check two ledgers Read subscripts as atom counts; multiply each count by its valence entry. For an ion, add one electron per unit of negative charge or remove one per unit of positive charge. The neutral examples below need neither adjustment. Check the total once for the whole particle and check each atom separately. For H look for two around it; for the other atoms here use the octet check, looking for eight. These targets are useful for this set; odd-electron particles and some other species fall outside the shortcut.

Problem type 1

Build a dot structure from a formula

Use this when you are given a formula and asked for a complete electron dot structure.

Example: neutral chloramine, NH2Cl. Goal: choose its skeleton and account for every electron.

Look up the atom’s column. For the main group atoms here, use group 1 directly or subtract 10 from groups 13–17. Source: NIST’s periodic table (group columns and atomic electron configurations).

Neutral atoms: group lookup
AtomGroupValence e⁻
H11
N155
Cl177

  1. Count the budget

    Add up the valence electrons

    5 + 2 × 1 + 7 = 14

    N is in group 15, so each neutral N contributes 5. The subscript gives two H at 1 each, and Cl brings 7. That is 14 valence electrons to place.

  2. Choose the skeleton

    Put N between the three end atoms

    H, H and Cl around N · 14 left

    Two H atoms can each make only one link here. Cl can fill its shell with one link and three lone pairs. So the three of them sit at the ends, and N goes in the middle.

  3. Draw the links

    One line along each link

    3 lines × 2 = 6 · 14 − 6 = 8 left

    Start with one line along each link. A line is one shared pair, so three lines use 6 electrons.

  4. Place lone pairs

    Give Cl three lone pairs

    3 pairs × 2 = 6 · 8 − 6 = 2 left

    Cl needs three lone pairs to go with its one link. They use 6 more electrons, leaving 2.

  5. Spend the rest

    The last 2 become a lone pair on N

    1 pair × 2 = 2 · 2 − 2 = 0 left

    The 2 electrons left become a lone pair on N. Every valence electron is now in the drawing.

  6. Check both ledgers

    Count the whole drawing, then each atom

    6 in lines + 8 in dots = 14

    Global: 6 in lines + 8 in dots = 14 electrons. Local: N has 6 shared + 2 lone = 8; Cl has 2 shared + 6 lone = 8; each H has 2 shared.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Add up the valence electrons from the formula.

    5 + 2 × 1 + 7 = 14

    Why?

    Each neutral atom brings the valence electrons its group gives it, and a subscript says how many of that atom there are. An ion would also gain one electron per negative charge or lose one per positive charge; chloramine is neutral.

  2. Choose the skeleton: put the atoms that make only one link at the ends.

    H, H and Cl around N

    Why?

    H can make only one link here, and Cl can fill its shell with one link and three lone pairs. An atom with only one link cannot sit between two others, so N goes in the middle.

  3. Draw one line for each link and take its electrons off the budget.

    3 lines: 14 − 6 = 8 left

    Why?

    A line is one shared pair. Every connection in the skeleton needs at least one, so the lines are paid for first.

  4. Spend what is left as lone pairs, two electrons at a time.

    Cl: 8 − 6 = 2 · N: 2 − 2 = 0

    Why?

    This model places electrons in pairs. Cl completes its shell with three lone pairs, and the last pair stays on N, so nothing is left over.

  5. Check both ledgers: the total once, then each atom.

    6 + 8 = 14 · N: 6 + 2 = 8

    Why?

    A shared pair counts once in the whole drawing but appears around both atoms it joins. A drawing can fill every shell with the wrong total, or match the total and leave an atom short, so check both.

Problem type 2

Move a pair without spending another pair

Use this when every electron is placed but an atom still sees fewer than eight.

Example: ketene, C2H2O. Both H atoms are on the same end C, as shown in NIST’s ketene record. The dot positions are a model.

Neutral atoms. Read the group number directly for H. For groups 14 to 16, subtract 10. The group columns are in NIST’s periodic table.

Valence electron lookup
AtomHCNO
Group1141516
Valence e⁻1456

  1. Count the budget

    Ketene has 16 valence electrons

    2 × 4 + 2 × 1 + 6 = 16

    Both H atoms are on the same end C. Read each subscript, multiply by the valence entry, and add.

  2. Spend every electron

    The middle C sees only 4

    8 in lines + 8 in dots = 16

    Draw four single lines, using 8 electrons. Put six of the remaining electrons next to O and the last pair next to the H-bearing C. Every electron is spent, but the middle C sees only 4.

  3. Move a pair

    Move one O pair into the C–O link

    middle C: 4 → 6 · total still 16

    Move one O dot pair into the C–O link. O still counts that pair locally, and the middle C now sees 6. No electron was added.

  4. Move another pair

    Move the end C pair into the C–C link

    middle C: 6 → 8 · total still 16

    Move the end C dot pair into the C–C link. The middle C now sees 8. The end C still counts the shared pair.

  5. Check both ledgers

    Every atom sees its target

    12 in lines + 4 in dots = 16

    Each C and O sees 8 electrons, and each H sees 2. Ketene has two single and two double links. The dot positions are a model.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Build the skeleton and spend every electron as in Problem type 1.

    middle C sees 4

    Why?

    Placing every electron first shows whether any atom is short. A short atom after all 16 are spent cannot be fixed by adding electrons, because the total is fixed by the formula.

  2. Move a lone pair from a neighbor into the link it shares with the short atom.

    O pair → C–O line · middle C: 4 → 6

    Why?

    The pair stays between the same two atoms, so the global ledger does not change. The neighbor still counts the pair around itself, and the short atom gains two.

  3. Repeat with another neighbor until the short atom sees eight.

    end C pair → C–C line · 6 → 8

    Why?

    Each move adds one line to one connection and keeps the same neighbors. Only the line count on that connection, its multiplicity, goes up.

  4. Check both ledgers again, then count multiplicity per connection.

    12 + 4 = 16 · 2 single, 2 double

    Why?

    A move can fix one atom and leave another short, so recheck every atom. Multiplicity belongs to each connection, so count lines link by link.

Same method, three pairs on one link: hydrogen cyanide

Start with H–C–N and put the last 6 of the 10 electrons on N. C sees only 4. Move two N pairs into the C–N link so both atoms see 8. Check the total: 8 in lines + 2 in dots = 10.

Bond multiplicity counts lines on one connection. Ketene has two single and two double links. HCN has one single and one triple link. Moving a pair keeps the same neighbors. These drawings omit exact angles.

Optional: when two complete drawings compete

Calculate formal charge, a charge label for the dot model. Start with an atom’s neutral valence count; subtract its lone electrons and one electron per bond line. Favor small charge magnitudes among valid alternatives for these molecules. Other species may need more than one drawing. In ketene the H-bearing C has 4 − 0 − 4 = 0; O has 6 − 4 − 2 = 0.

Each C sees six from its three single links, so move one lone pair from each O onto its own C–O link. That gives three single and two double connections: 14 electrons in lines + 8 in dots = 22. Glossary

Valence electrons
Outer electrons available in the simple main-group bonding model. For a neutral atom, use the group lookup shown in the activity.
Skeleton
The starting pattern of which atoms are connected. Use one line per link at first, then decide whether any links require more shared pairs.
Lewis electron dot structure
A drawing that shows bonded atoms, shared electron pairs and unshared valence electrons. A line counts as a shared pair; two dots next to one symbol count as a lone pair.
Bond multiplicity
The number of shared pairs along one connection. Identify the line count of the connection and match the vocabulary to an original drawing. Multiplicity does not say how evenly those pairs are shared.
Lone pair
Two electrons assigned to one atom in the Lewis drawing. They belong in that atom’s local count and in the molecule’s total count.
Octet check
Eight electrons around an atom, counting shared electrons as neighbors of both atoms. H instead has room for a duet. This is a check for the examples here, with exceptions in other species.
Formal charge
A bookkeeping label found by assigning one electron from each shared pair to each partner and both lone electrons to their own atom. Sum these labels to get the overall charge. These are distinct from partial charges.
