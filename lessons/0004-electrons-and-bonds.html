Follow the electrons

Build and audit an electron drawing: keep the total budget and each atom’s shell in balance.

Start with the electron budget. Then choose a model, sketch the connections, and audit the drawing. Open the glossary or use the quick reference.

An electron budget

Electrons carry negative charge. The outer electrons used in bonding are valence electrons. A Lewis drawing is an electron-accounting model: dots track electrons and lines can stand for pairs. Two dots are one pair. A pair used between atoms is counted once in the whole drawing’s budget.

Build a budget before drawing: add the valence electrons supplied by all atoms, add electrons for negative charge, and subtract electrons for positive charge. Record how many electrons have been placed and how many remain. A line costs two electrons in this accounting, but the names for different bond patterns are a separate vocabulary step for you to make using your course reference.

A lone pair is a pair that is not shared between atoms. Many introductory atom models use an eight-electron outer-shell goal (an octet); a hydrogen atom uses two. These are limited guidelines, not universal rules. When checking a local shell, a shared pair contributes around both atoms; when checking the overall budget, count it only once.

A flat Lewis drawing records electron accounting and connections. It does not by itself establish the actual shape in space. Start small: if two dots mean one pair, how many electrons do three pairs use? Think “three groups of two”; if uncertain, sketch three pairs before continuing.

Check two kinds of counting

Two atoms share a pair. A learner counts that pair twice in the total budget because both atoms use it in their shell check. Which audit repairs the mistake?

Compare reasoning after an attempt

Yes. One shared pair is still only two electrons in the entire structure. Both neighboring atoms include that pair when checking their own shells.

Choose a model before drawing

Look for evidence of a small group held together by shared electrons before building its Lewis drawing. To describe an extended solid of charged particles, track the attraction of opposite net charges across the solid. Losing a negative electron makes a particle more positive. Gaining one makes it more negative. The total charge stays the same when an electron moves between the two.

Choose a starting skeleton

A skeleton is a proposed map of which atoms connect. Start with the formula’s inventory and any known charge or connection information. These guidelines apply to small introductory molecules, not every possible chemical structure.

  1. Place hydrogen at an end: in this model it can share one pair, not connect two other atoms. An end atom is called terminal.
  2. In an introductory molecule with one non-hydrogen atom and several hydrogens, start with that atom at the center. For common simple molecules with several possible centers, the less electron-attracting non-hydrogen atom is often a useful first candidate, not a guarantee.
  3. With several non-hydrogen atoms, propose a connected backbone (the main chain or framework), then attach terminal atoms. Use any known connection information; a formula can allow more than one arrangement.
  4. Test the proposed connections against the electron budget and every local shell. Reject a proposal that cannot pass those checks. If several remain, use further structural evidence rather than guessing from the formula or a tidy picture.

Fresh decision: a neutral model has the atom inventory H2Se. Se is the symbol for selenium. Under the ordinary one-pair limit for H, compare the two proposed connection maps before drawing any electron dots.

Build a new structure

Our worked case is ethene, C2H4: two carbon atoms joined together, each attached to two hydrogens. It has two carbon centers. This connection pattern is supplied; a formula alone does not always determine connections.

Find valence counts using a periodic table. For the main-group columns 1, 2, and 13–18, the usual neutral-atom counts are 1, 2, and 3–8, respectively; helium is an exception with 2. This shortcut does not cover transition metals. Carbon is in group 14 and hydrogen in group 1. So this neutral example has 2 × 4 + 4 × 1 = 12 electrons. The atomic number counts protons, not the valence-electron supply.

Predict before stepping: a trial drawing has 8 electrons around the left carbon, 6 around the right carbon, and 12 in its whole budget. If a lone pair on the left becomes shared between the carbons, what happens to each carbon’s count and to the whole budget? Make three separate predictions.

Read the construction stages
  1. Start with the six atoms and a twelve-electron budget.
  2. Five connections use ten electrons. Two electrons remain.
  3. A trial lone pair uses the remainder, but the right carbon is two electrons short of its local octet.
  4. Move the trial pair between the carbons. The same twelve electrons now complete both carbon octets and all hydrogen shells.

The left carbon already counted the pair before it moved, so its local count stays at eight. The right carbon gains local credit and goes from six to eight. The whole drawing still has twelve electrons.

Predict, then step through the construction. All four stages are also explained in text.

For a new simple structure, first complete the terminal atoms’ shells, then place any remaining electrons around a central atom. If the budget is used but an octet is short, consider moving a neighboring lone pair into a shared position. Never add electrons beyond the budget. More complex cases can require formal-charge comparisons (how electrons are assigned to atoms), resonance (several valid electron placements), or exceptions to the octet; ask for a new example if your check reaches one.

A new trial drawing uses exactly its budget, but one ordinary carbon has only six electrons around it. What should you inspect next?

Compare reasoning after an attempt

Yes. Inspect whether an existing neighboring pair can be shared while preserving all required shell counts and the total. A move needs a fresh local audit.

Place lone pairs and check for extra sharing

Two short drawings use the same audit. Try each one before opening its comparison. In these small neutral examples, hydrogen counts two electrons and the other atoms count eight around themselves.

Three neighbors, room for a lone pair

In dimethylamine, nitrogen (N) connects to two carbon atoms and one H. Each carbon also connects to three H atoms. N supplies five valence electrons, each C supplies four, and each H supplies one. Draw the connections and decide where the remaining electrons belong.

Hint · count around the three interior atoms

Count every connection. Draw one line for each, then count how many electrons each carbon and the nitrogen can include around itself.

Compare the dimethylamine drawing

Whole drawing: 5 + 2 × 4 + 7 × 1 gives 20 electrons. The nine lines use 18 electrons, leaving one pair. At each atom: each H already counts two electrons and each C counts eight. N has three shared pairs, so putting the remaining lone pair on N brings its count to eight. If you drew a second line next to N, recount the shell of the neighboring atom that would also count that pair.

One short chain, more than one shared pair

Draw chloroacetylene from the skeleton H—C—C—Cl. The lines here mark neighbors only. Cl supplies seven valence electrons, each C supplies four, and H supplies one. Find the budget and show all dots and lines. Which atoms share more than one pair?

Hint · start at both ends

Give each neighboring pair of atoms one shared pair to start. Complete Cl’s local shell with lone pairs. Then check how many electrons are left for the two carbons and where those electrons can count around both.

Compare the chloroacetylene drawing

Whole drawing: 1 + 2 × 4 + 7 gives 16 electrons. The three starting lines and the three lone pairs on Cl use 12. Use the last four electrons as two more shared pairs between the carbons. At each atom: each C now counts four shared pairs, Cl counts one shared pair and three lone pairs, and H counts one shared pair.

If you kept the last four electrons as one lone pair on each carbon, each carbon would still count only six electrons. Close the comparison and try again. Keep the same budget and make every local count work.

Construct, then audit a new molecule

Sketch the neutral molecule with formula C2H4O in a sample whose oxygen has exactly one neighboring atom. Use ordinary neutral bond counts here: each carbon shares four pairs in all, each oxygen shares two, and each hydrogen shares one. The counts describe shared pairs, so two pairs can connect the same two neighbors. Propose the connections first, then show all shared and lone pairs.

Hint 1 · place the ends

Start by marking the atoms that can have only one neighbor. Which atoms can connect the rest of the molecule into one piece?

Hint 2 · compare neighbors with pairs

Oxygen has one neighbor but needs to share two pairs. If you choose that neighbor, how many pairs can it still use to connect to the rest?

Hint 3 · check both budgets

Use groups 14, 1, and 16 to count the supplied electrons for C, H, and O. Track each pair once in the overall budget, then count the pairs around each atom.

Compare the complete drawing after an attempt

The two carbons must connect. Only they can be interior atoms in this case. A hydrogen cannot be oxygen’s neighbor because it can share only one pair. Connect oxygen to either carbon with two shared pairs. The carbons have eight pair positions in all. Oxygen uses two of those positions and the four hydrogens use four. That leaves two positions, one at each carbon, for a single shared pair between the carbons. The carbon bonded to oxygen has room for one H. The other carbon connects to three H atoms. This structure is called ethanal.

Whole drawing: 2 × 4 + 4 × 1 + 6 gives 18 electrons. The seven lines and two lone pairs account for 14 + 4 electrons. At each atom: both carbons count four shared pairs, oxygen counts two shared pairs and two lone pairs, and each hydrogen counts one shared pair.

If your oxygen has two neighbors, revisit the sample evidence. If both carbons look identical, recount the pairs spent connecting one carbon to oxygen. Close the comparison, revise, and audit each atom again. Removing the oxygen-neighbor clue would leave more than one possible connection pattern for this formula.

Exit check: explain a new case

A fictional Lewis drawing has a 12-electron budget but displays two bond lines and five lone pairs. Describe how to audit it and what kind of correction is needed. Do not infer a real molecule or shape.

Check your method after making an attempt

Count each line and each lone pair as two electrons, once in the total. The drawing uses more than its budget; remove or rearrange electrons only after checking the intended connections and shell goals. A tidy drawing is not proof of a valid budget.

If this is difficult, return to the model, make one change, and describe what stays the same. Then try the exit check again without looking.

Glossary: tools for thinking

Electron / valence
An electron carries negative charge. Valence electrons are outer electrons used in bonding.
Budget / pair
A budget is the total available. A pair contains two items; count two electrons for each represented pair.
Nucleus / net charge
The nucleus is the positive central region of an atom. Net charge is the balance after combining positive and negative contributions.
Lone pair / octet
A lone pair is a pair not shared between atoms. An octet is an eight-electron outer-shell goal in some simple models.
Skeleton / terminal / backbone / main-group column
A skeleton is a proposed connection pattern. Terminal means at an end. A backbone is the main connected chain or framework. Main-group columns are 1, 2, and 13–18 on the periodic table.
Lewis drawing / formal charge / resonance
A Lewis drawing tracks valence electrons and connections. Formal charge is an electron-assignment check. Resonance uses several valid electron placements for one connection pattern.
Model / group
A model shows selected features. A group on the periodic table is a numbered column.
