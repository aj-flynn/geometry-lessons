Account for electrons in a drawing

Build electron dot structures and count each kind of bond. Keep your sketches and notes beside you as you work.

Keep two electron ledgers

Imagine two atoms joined by one line. Predict how many electrons this adds to the whole drawing and how many it puts around each atom.

Check the entry skill

The whole drawing contains two electrons in that pair. Both atoms have those same two electrons around them.

A Lewis electron dot structure records where the outer electrons are counted. A skeleton shows which atoms link. A line is one shared pair. Two dots beside one atom are a lone pair, a pair assigned to that atom alone. The starting stock is the number of valence electrons, the outer electrons counted in this model.

Read subscripts as atom counts; multiply each count by its valence entry. For an ion, add one electron per unit of negative charge or remove one per unit of positive charge. The neutral examples below need neither adjustment. Check the total once for the whole particle and check each atom separately. For H look for two around it; for the other atoms here use the octet check, looking for eight. These targets are useful for this set; odd-electron particles and some other species fall outside the shortcut.

  1. N is in group 15, so each neutral N contributes 5. Total: 5 + 2 × 1 + 7 = 14 valence electrons.
  2. Two H atoms can each make only one link here. Cl can fill its shell with one link and three lone pairs. Put N between the three terminal atoms. Start with one line along each of those links: three pairs use 6 electrons.
  3. Give Cl three lone pairs, using 6 more electrons. The 2 left become a lone pair on N.
  4. Global: 6 in lines + 8 in dots = 14 electrons. Local: N has 6 shared + 2 lone = 8; Cl has 2 shared + 6 lone = 8; each H has 2 shared.

Ketene

C2H2O. Both H atoms are on the same end C, as shown in NIST’s ketene record. The dot positions are a model.

  1. Budget: 2 × 4 + 2 × 1 + 6 = 16 valence electrons.
  2. Draw four single lines, using 8 electrons. Put six of the remaining electrons next to O and the last pair next to the H-bearing C. The middle C sees 4 electrons.
  3. Move one O dot pair into the C–O link. O still counts that pair locally and the middle C now sees 6.
  4. Move the end C dot pair into the C–C link. The middle C now sees 8. The end C still counts the shared pair.
  5. Check the total: 12 electrons in lines + 4 in dots = 16. Each C and O sees 8 electrons, and each H sees 2.

Hydrogen cyanide

Start with H–C–N and put the last 6 of the 10 electrons on N. C sees only 4. Move two N pairs into the C–N link so both atoms see 8. Check the total: 8 in lines + 2 in dots = 10.

Bond multiplicity counts lines on one connection. Ketene has two single and two double links. HCN has one single and one triple link. Moving a pair keeps the same neighbors. These drawings omit exact angles.

Optional: when two complete drawings compete

Calculate formal charge, a charge label for the dot model. Start with an atom’s neutral valence count; subtract its lone electrons and one electron per bond line. Favor small charge magnitudes among valid alternatives for these molecules. Other species may need more than one drawing. In ketene the H-bearing C has 4 − 0 − 4 = 0; O has 6 − 4 − 2 = 0.

Compare after your attempt

Hydrazine: 2 × 5 + 4 × 1 = 14 electrons. Five singles use 10; two lone pairs use 4. Each N has 6 shared and 2 lone electrons nearby. Each H has 2 shared.

Formaldehyde: 4 + 2 × 1 + 6 = 12 electrons. Two singles and one double use 8; the two O lone pairs use 4. The C has 8 shared, the O has 4 shared and 4 lone, and each H has 2 shared.

Propyne: 3 × 4 + 4 × 1 = 16 electrons. Five singles and one triple use 16; no lone pairs remain. Each C touches four lines; each H touches one.

Check that the atom labels and every lone pair match your own accounting.

Revise: In hydrazine, the two local N counts both include the N–N line. If you count it twice in the whole drawing, you get 12 electrons in lines plus 4 in dots. Cross out the extra 2 in the whole count: 10 in lines plus 4 in dots gives 14. Each N still sees the shared pair. Mark each line once as you check your own total.

Compare after your attempt

2 × 4 + 2 × 1 + 2 × 6 = 22 electrons. Five initial lines use 10. Three lone pairs per O would use the other 12. Each C sees six from its three single links, so move one lone pair from each O onto its own C–O link. That gives three single and two double connections: 14 electrons in lines + 8 in dots = 22. Each C and each O sees eight, and each H sees two.

Full carbon shells pass one check. The atom inventory also fixes a budget, and each line and dot in the drawing must fit it. Check the local shells of O and H as well.

One Si–Si line and three Si–Cl lines from each Si give seven single bonds. Both Si atoms have four neighbors and no lone pairs. Each Cl has three lone pairs and one line. Global: 14 electrons in lines + 36 in dots = 50. Every Si and Cl sees eight.

Glossary

Valence electrons
Outer electrons available in the simple main-group bonding model. For a neutral atom, use the group lookup shown in the activity.
Skeleton
The starting pattern of which atoms are connected. Use one line per link at first, then decide whether any links require more shared pairs.
Lewis electron dot structure
A drawing that shows bonded atoms, shared electron pairs and unshared valence electrons. A line counts as a shared pair; two dots next to one symbol count as a lone pair.
Bond multiplicity
The number of shared pairs along one connection. Identify the line count of the connection and match the vocabulary to an original drawing. Multiplicity does not say how evenly those pairs are shared.
Lone pair
Two electrons assigned to one atom in the Lewis drawing. They belong in that atom’s local count and in the molecule’s total count.
Octet check
Eight electrons around an atom, counting shared electrons as neighbors of both atoms. H instead has room for a duet. This is a check for the examples here, with exceptions in other species.
Formal charge
A bookkeeping label found by assigning one electron from each shared pair to each partner and both lone electrons to their own atom. Sum these labels to get the overall charge. These are distinct from partial charges.
