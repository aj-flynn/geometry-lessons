Put bond arrows in space

Use a bond’s electron pull and a molecule’s supplied shape as two separate clues.

Use these original models to learn a method, then try your own work independently. Open the glossary or use the quick reference.

First: how uneven is one bond?

Electronegativity describes an atom’s pull on shared electrons. Compare two values using the positive difference: larger minus smaller. The values are unitless. A greater difference usually means more uneven sharing. The stronger-pulling end has a partial negative charge, written δ−; the other end is δ+. “Partial” means less than a whole ion charge.

For invented values 2.9 and 2.1, the difference is 0.8. Numerical cutoffs on introductory charts are approximate classroom conventions, not sharp physical boundaries. Boundaries and labels vary by course; consult the supplied chart and make the classification independently. Use the table and convention your teacher supplies. Real bonding lies on a continuum, so a difference alone is not a complete explanation.

Try the difference now

Explain why the difference cannot be larger than both positive starting values. Then move on to the whole-particle question.

Then: do the bond effects cancel?

To reason about a whole molecule, consider both the local electron-pull effects and the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms. A name for one local effect is not enough to classify the whole particle. First obtain the shape from reliable information or a shape model such as VSEPR (electron groups around an atom spread apart). A Lewis structure helps identify those groups, but its flat arrangement is not itself proof of shape.

For an introductory arrow model, draw each bond’s polarity arrow toward its more electron-attracting end. Combine the arrows in the supplied geometry. Equal arrows in opposite directions cancel; arrows at an angle may leave a net (combined) effect. Unequal opposite arrows do not fully cancel.

These arrows are a qualitative model of charge distribution, not moving electrons, bond order, or measured charge separation in an actual molecule.

Predict → bend the model → explain

Two equal outward arrows start in opposite directions. Predict what happens to their combined effect when the supplied angle shrinks to 90°. Only the angle changes; both arrow lengths stay fixed.

180 degrees; combined arrow length 0.00 model units
Reveal the static comparison after predicting

Static comparison: at 180°, two equal opposite arrows cancel. At 90°, the sideways parts cancel but the upward parts add; the combined length is about 1.41 times one arrow. This is an abstract vector model, not a way to bend a real molecule.

Your turn: use both pieces of evidence

A supplied model has two opposite arrows, but one is twice as long as the other. What happens when you combine them?

Hint 1: Notice

Notice both arrow length and direction.

Hint 2: Recall

Opposite effects cancel only when their magnitudes match.

Hint 3: Choose a strategy

Draw the two arrows head to tail. Look for the distance from start to finish.

Exit check: explain your evidence

A partner wants to classify a real molecule using only the arrangement of symbols in a flat Lewis drawing. What information would you request before deciding? Explain why the drawing alone is not enough.

Check your method after making an attempt

Request reliable geometry and evidence about the size and direction of the relevant charge effects. A flat electron-accounting drawing does not establish the three-dimensional arrangement. Combine the effects only after those facts are available.

If this is difficult, return to the model, make one change, and describe what stays the same. Then explain the checking method without looking.

Glossary: tools for thinking

Electronegativity / Δ
Electronegativity describes attraction for shared electrons. Δ means difference; here use larger minus smaller on the same unitless scale.
δ / partial
The Greek letter δ indicates a partial charge in this context: a fraction of a whole unit charge.
Geometry
The arrangement of parts in space, including angles and directions.
Vector / magnitude / net
A vector has size and direction. Magnitude means its size. Net is the combined result.
VSEPR
Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion: a model using electron groups to help predict arrangement in space.
