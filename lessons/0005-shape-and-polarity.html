Words to know

Separate amount, direction and shape

In a made-up scale, one atom has a pull value of 2.70 and another has 2.30. Which has the greater pull? What is the absolute difference?

Check the entry skill

2.70 is greater. The difference is 2.70 − 2.30 = 0.40 on that scale. Reversing the order of the atoms does not change the size of the difference.

Electronegativity (χ) is a relative measure of pull on shared electrons. A bond dipole models the resulting uneven charge on one bond. Use a crossed tail on the slightly positive end and point the head toward the slightly negative end. The symbols δ+ and δ− are partial charges, smaller charge shifts than making separate full ions in the simple model.

Electronegativity (χ)
A relative number for an atom’s pull on shared electrons. Compare only values from one scale.In the diagram: χ 1.90 under Si and χ 2.58 under S.
Pull difference (Δχ)
The larger χ minus the smaller. Its size says how uneven the sharing is; order does not change it.In the diagram: the panel: 2.58 − 1.90 = 0.68.
Partial charges (δ+, δ−)
Slight charge shifts, smaller than full ion charges. δ− goes on the atom with the larger χ.In the diagram: δ+ above Si and δ− above S.
Bond dipole
An arrow for one bond’s uneven charge: crossed tail at the δ+ end, head toward the δ− end.In the diagram: the arrow from Si toward S.
Bond line
How many pairs a link shares. It says nothing about how evenly they are shared.In the diagram: the one line between Si and S.

Keep two questions apart How many pairs does a link share, and how unevenly? Lines in an electron dot drawing address the first. A pull difference, Δχ, addresses the second. Use the size of the difference for degree of unevenness and the larger χ for direction when the model has a meaningful bond dipole.

Problem type 1

Label one bond from a lookup

Use this when you have two elements, an electronegativity lookup and a cutoff rule, and must label the bond and give its direction.

Example: pair under review: Si and S.

Pauling electronegativity lookup
Elementχ (unitless)
Si: silicon1.90
S: sulfur2.58

The linked Royal Society of Chemistry Atomic data entries give each row. Read the numbers as written, with two decimals.

Rule for this practice set only:

  • 0 ≤ Δχ < 0.20: treat as nonpolar covalent.
  • 0.20 ≤ Δχ < 1.70: treat as polar covalent.
  • 1.70 ≤ Δχ: predicts an ionic candidate; check solid structure.

  1. Step 1 · Look up

    Record both rows from one table

    χ(Si) = 1.90 · χ(S) = 2.58

    Find the Si row and record 1.90. Find the S row and record 2.58. Use one scale for both.

  2. Step 2 · Subtract

    Subtract the smaller value

    Δχ = 2.58 − 1.90 = 0.68

    Δχ = 2.58 − 1.90 = 0.68 (unitless, from this table). Reversing the atoms does not change the size of the difference.

  3. Step 3 · Locate

    Find the interval that owns 0.68

    0.20 ≤ 0.68 < 1.70 → polar covalent

    0.68 lies in this activity’s middle interval, so the expected label is polar covalent.

  4. Step 4 · Direction

    Point the arrow toward the larger χ

    0.68 · polar covalent · δ+ Si, δ− S

    If it is a shared Si–S link, put δ− at S and δ+ at Si; the crossed tail starts near Si.

  5. Step 5 · Report

    Use the course chart for a course label

    Report: Δχ 0.68, toward S

    These cutoffs sort a gradual change. Use this rule for this activity. For a course label, find the course chart, locate the interval, and check which endpoint it includes. If the chart is missing, report the difference and direction and leave the course label open.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Find each element’s row and record its χ from one scale.

    Si 1.90 · S 2.58

    Why?

    Numbers from different scales or rounding conventions do not compare, so both values must come from the same table.

  2. Subtract the smaller value from the larger.

    Δχ = 2.58 − 1.90 = 0.68

    Why?

    The size of the difference measures how unevenly the pair is shared. Which atom you write first does not change it.

  3. Find the interval that contains Δχ, reading each endpoint sign.

    0.20 ≤ 0.68 < 1.70 → polar covalent

    Why?

    ≤ includes its endpoint and < leaves it out, so every value, boundaries included, lands in exactly one interval.

  4. For a polar link, put δ− on the larger χ and start the crossed tail at the other atom.

    δ+ Si → δ− S

    Why?

    The atom with the greater pull draws the shared electrons toward itself, so it carries the slight negative charge and the head points to it.

  5. For a course label, use the course chart; without it, report Δχ and direction only.

    0.68, toward S; course label open

    Why?

    The cutoffs sort a gradual change, and a course may draw its lines or include its endpoints differently.

Words to know

Add bond arrows for a whole molecule

One molecule can hold several bond dipoles. To judge the whole molecule, draw the actual molecular geometry, then add the arrows by length and direction, not by label.

Bond arrow
One bond dipole: its length is the amount, its direction points to δ−.In the diagram: the dashed arrow as drawn, and the red copy.
Shared tail
Slide every arrow so the tails meet at one point, keeping each length and direction.In the diagram: the dot where the red copy starts.
Parts
Each arrow splits into a sideways part and an up-down part.In the diagram: the two blue lines drawn beside the red copy.
Cancel
Equal parts in opposite directions add to zero.In the diagram: not drawn here; it takes a second arrow.
Net dipole
What is left after all the parts are added. Zero means no overall direction of uneven charge.In the diagram: not drawn here; it takes every arrow in the molecule.

Add arrows, not labels Put the arrows at one tail, keeping their lengths and directions. Add the parts: equal, opposite parts cancel, and what remains is the net dipole.

Compare after your attempt

The two arrows point toward F. Their horizontal parts cancel but their downward parts add. In this two-bond-arrow model, the sum points toward the F side down the angle bisector. The measured molecular dipole depends on the full electron distribution, including lone pairs; the NIST entry gives its magnitude, not a measured direction for our arrow. Glossary

Electronegativity
A relative number for how strongly an atom draws shared electrons. Compare only numbers from the same scale and rounding convention.
Bond dipole
A direction and amount of uneven charge in a bond model. The chemistry arrow has a crossed positive tail and a head pointing toward the negative end.
Partial charge
A slightly positive or negative part of an overall charge distribution, written δ+ or δ−. These are distinct from full ion charges.
Molecular geometry
Compare the two 1,2-dichloroethene models: atom counts and bond orders stay fixed, but the positions relative to the double bond differ.
Net dipole
The combined effect of the uneven charge distribution in a whole molecule. Equal, opposite contributions cancel in the model. Compare arrangements A and B to see how a molecule can retain an overall pull even when its individual bond arrows match in strength.
