An intermolecular attraction acts between molecules. Keep the lines inside each molecule when sketching a simple physical change. When a liquid boils, molecules separate into the vapor. During melting, particles become able to move past their neighbors while staying close. Neither process requires breaking all the bonds inside each intact molecule.

Problem type 1

Look for two compatible sites

Use this when you must decide whether a contact between two molecules is a hydrogen bond.

Example: ethanol (left) next to dimethyl ether (right).

Goal: decide whether the pictured contact is a hydrogen bond.

  1. Step 1 · Scale

    Mark the two molecules

    2 molecules: ethanol · dimethyl ether

    The boxes mark separate molecules. A hydrogen bond must cross from one box to the other. These are reduced group sketches, not actual shapes or distances.

  2. Step 2 · Donor

    Is an H joined directly to O?

    donor: ethanol O–H ✓

    The left O–H group is the hydrogen-bond donor, because that H is directly joined to O.

  3. Step 3 · Acceptor

    Does the neighbor offer an available lone pair?

    donor ✓ · acceptor: ether O ✓

    The right O has an available lone pair (two unshared electrons); that makes it the acceptor in this contact.

  4. Step 4 · Contact

    Sites face: draw the contact dashed

    donor ✓ · acceptor ✓ · facing → H···O hydrogen bond

    Here those sites face one another. The dashed H···O contact is a hydrogen bond; the solid O–H line remains a bond inside ethanol.

  5. Step 5 · Failures

    If either check fails, there is no hydrogen bond

    no donor or no acceptor → no hydrogen bond

    Two ways for the contact to fail: replace the left ethanol with dimethyl ether and its H atoms are all on C (no donor site in this model). Keep the left ethanol but replace the right ether with propane and the neighbor has no suitable acceptor in this model. Other attractions can still act.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Mark the separate molecules, and look only at lines that cross between them.

    ethanol | dimethyl ether

    Why?

    A hydrogen bond is an attraction between molecules. A line inside one molecule is a bond, whatever atoms it joins.

  2. Donor check: in one molecule, find an H joined directly to a strongly pulling atom, here O.

    ethanol O–H ✓

    Why?

    An H bonded straight to O is left with a large δ+, and that is what reaches across to the neighbor. An H on C does not pass.

  3. Acceptor check: in the other molecule, find an available lone pair, here on O.

    ether O lone pair ✓

    Why?

    The H needs an unshared pair on the neighbor to point at. A molecule with only C–C and C–H links has none.

  4. If both sites are present and face each other, draw the contact dashed and keep the bond solid.

    O–H ··· O

    Why?

    The dashed H···O line is the hydrogen bond between molecules; the solid O–H line is a bond inside ethanol that a phase change leaves intact.

  5. If either check fails, name no hydrogen bond and look for other attractions.

    no donor or no acceptor → none

    Why?

    Failing the two-site test rules out only this contact. Dipole–dipole and dispersion attractions can still act.

To identify an ordinary dipole–dipole attraction, look for two permanent uneven charge distributions with opposite partial charges facing.

Words to know

Follow a temporary shift

An electron cloud can briefly crowd toward one side. In the lower snapshot, the left cloud’s crowded side repels nearby electrons in the right cloud. That induced shift leaves opposite partial charges facing.

This is one London dispersion contact. It can occur without a permanent dipole and can contribute alongside other attractions.

Words to know

State the property and conditions

A boiling point occurs when the liquid’s vapor pressure matches the outside pressure. Comparing two boiling points? Match pressure first. Stronger total attraction often raises boiling temperature among similar substances at the same pressure, because the liquid holds its molecules more effectively at a given temperature.

A melting point depends on how particles fit in the solid. For solid chloromethane, look at the contacts between neighboring molecules. For solid magnesium sulfide, picture a repeating array of Mg²⁺ and S²⁻. Identify the neighbors of one ion in that array. To rank melting temperatures, also look up the measured transitions and their conditions.

Melting point
The temperature at which the solid’s fixed neighbors start to move past each other. Packing in the solid matters.In the diagram: the melt arrow from solid to liquid.
Boiling point
The temperature at which the liquid’s vapor pressure matches the outside pressure. Match pressure before comparing.In the diagram: the boil arrow from liquid to vapor.
Intact molecule
Melting and boiling change positions and spacing. The bonds inside each molecule stay.In the diagram: the red link in every mark, in all three boxes.
Polarizability
How easily an electron cloud shifts. A larger, easily shifted cloud adds to total attraction.In the diagram: not drawn; see Problem type 2.

One contact is not the total A single named contact cannot tell you total attraction. Include the size of the electron cloud, its polarizability (how easily the cloud shifts), the shape and contact area, and how many contacts each molecule can make. For melting, account for packing; for either phase change, use data when competing factors make a rank uncertain.

Problem type 2

Explain a measured difference

Use this when you must explain why one substance melts or boils at a higher temperature than another.

Example: propane and butane, two alkane chains.

Both are alkanes with dispersion as the main attraction. Values from Gilmour, Zwicker et al. (1967), as listed in the NIST Chemistry WebBook. These temperatures are observations for the two substances, not model output.

At 1 atm: measured normal boiling points
MoleculeNormal boiling point
Propane, three-carbon chain231.1 K
Butane, four-carbon unbranched chain272.7 K

  1. Step 1 · Conditions

    Match the property and the pressure

    property: normal boiling point · 1 atm for both

    Both rows are normal boiling points, measured at 1 atm, so the pressure matches before any comparison.

  2. Step 2 · Read

    Read one row, then the other

    propane 231.1 K · butane 272.7 K

    Read one row: propane’s normal boiling temperature is 231.1 K at 1 atm. The higher observation is butane at 272.7 K under the same pressure.

  3. Step 3 · Attraction

    Name the main attraction

    same main type: dispersion

    Both are alkanes with dispersion as the main attraction. With the type matched, the difference must come from how much of it each molecule has.

  4. Step 4 · Clouds

    Compare the size and shape of the clouds

    butane: one more carbon group → larger cloud

    Adding a carbon group changes the size and shape of the electron cloud. In this closely related pair, butane’s larger, more easily distorted cloud and extended contact surface help explain stronger dispersion attraction overall.

  5. Step 5 · Conclude

    Tie the total attraction to the measurement

    stronger total dispersion → 272.7 K > 231.1 K at 1 atm

    Both have the same main type of attraction, yet the measured temperatures differ. That is a reason to compare the total and the conditions before ranking substances. For other shapes, even molecules with the same atom count can make different contacts or pack differently.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Check that both values are the same property under the same conditions.

    normal boiling point · 1 atm

    Why?

    A boiling point shifts with the outside pressure, so readings at different pressures do not compare.

  2. Read each row and note which is higher.

    231.1 K < 272.7 K

    Why?

    The measurement is the observation to explain. The model explains it; it does not replace it.

  3. Name the main attraction in each substance.

    both: dispersion

    Why?

    When the main type is the same, the type alone cannot explain the difference.

  4. Compare what sets the total: cloud size, how easily it shifts, contact surface and number of contacts.

    butane: larger cloud, more contact

    Why?

    A larger, more polarizable cloud with more surface makes stronger dispersion contacts overall.

  5. Link the stronger total attraction to the higher boiling temperature, and say what the data settle.

    butane boils higher at 1 atm

    Why?

    The liquid that holds its molecules more strongly needs a higher temperature to reach the outside pressure. When factors compete, the data decide.

Glossary

Intermolecular attraction
An attraction between distinct molecules. Find the dashed connector across the two chloromethane boxes, and compare it with the links inside each box.
Carbonyl
A group with a C=O double bond. In acetone, the carbonyl C also connects to two carbon groups.
Hydrogen-bond donor
In the course model for common neutral molecules, a group with H bonded directly to N, O or F. It must face a suitable electron-rich site.
Hydrogen-bond acceptor
A suitable electron-rich atom on a partner, commonly N, O or F with an available lone pair in these examples. Location and availability matter.
Hydrogen bond
Here, a directional attraction from a donor H to a suitable electron-rich site on another molecule. The course shortcut here has exceptions outside these examples.
Dipole–dipole attraction
A contact between molecules with permanent uneven charge. A favorable orientation puts a relatively positive region of one near a relatively negative region of another.
London dispersion
An attraction when a momentary uneven electron distribution induces a shift in its neighbor. It contributes in polar and nonpolar materials.
Polarizability
How easily an electron cloud can be distorted. It helps explain why different molecules can have different dispersion attractions.
Boiling point
The temperature at which the liquid vapor pressure matches the external pressure. The normal boiling point uses 1 atm.
Melting point
At a stated pressure, the temperature at which the solid and liquid can coexist at equilibrium. Particle arrangement in the solid matters.
