Look between the molecules

Zoom out one level

Start by distinguishing the connections within a bounded particle from attractions between separate particles. Intermolecular forces are attractions between molecules. When a molecular liquid boils without decomposing, molecules separate while their internal connections generally remain intact. A physical change rearranges particles without changing their chemical identity.

All atoms and molecules can experience London dispersion attractions arising from temporary uneven electron distributions. Polar molecules also have dipole–dipole attractions between their partial charges. In common introductory examples, hydrogen bonding needs H bonded directly to N, O, or F and a suitable lone pair on a nearby partner. Merely containing H is not enough.

These attractions can coexist. Do not treat a force label as a universal ranking: molecular size, shape, and how easily electron clouds distort also matter. A large nonpolar molecule can have stronger total attractions than a small polar one.

Connect attractions to properties

When comparing otherwise similar molecular substances at the same pressure, stronger total attractions usually mean a higher boiling point: more energy is needed to separate the particles. Viscosity (resistance to flow) and surface tension (resistance to enlarging a liquid’s surface) can also increase with attraction, but temperature and molecular shape matter. Melting points also depend strongly on how particles pack in a solid.

Extended charged-particle crystals require a different model from a collection of separate molecules. Many resist melting strongly, but not all dissolve in water. Mobile ions can carry electric current when molten or in solution; ions fixed in a typical solid lattice cannot move freely. Some solids have continuous networks of internal connections rather than separate molecules. Avoid predicting every property from a single category name.

Glossary

Intermolecular / intramolecular
Intermolecular means between molecules; intramolecular means within a molecule.
Dispersion / dipole
Dispersion attractions arise from temporary uneven electron distributions. A permanent dipole is a lasting separation of partial charge.
Hydrogen bonding
A particular attraction involving H directly bonded to N, O, or F and a suitable nearby lone-pair partner in typical introductory examples.
Boiling point / pressure
Boiling point is the temperature where a liquid’s vapor pressure equals the external pressure. Pressure is force per area.
Viscosity / surface tension
Viscosity is resistance to flow. Surface tension describes resistance to increasing a liquid’s surface area.
Lattice / network solid
A lattice is an ordered repeating arrangement. A covalent network solid contains an extended network of covalent bonds.
