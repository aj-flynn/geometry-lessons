Problem type 1

Count the atoms in one particle

Use this when a question asks how many of each atom a formula shows, or whether two formulas must share an arrangement.

Example: ethyl methanoate, C3H6O2. Two molecules are under consideration.

  • Ethyl methanoate: HCOOCH2CH3
  • Methyl ethanoate: CH3COOCH3

NIST’s ethyl methanoate record and methyl ethanoate record show the two named compounds.

Goal: report counts in one ethyl methanoate molecule and decide if the two entries must have the same arrangement.

  1. Name the unit

    One molecule fixes the unit

    unit: 1 molecule of C₃H₆O₂

    Ethyl methanoate is a molecular substance, so the formula counts the atoms in one molecule.

  2. Read a symbol

    C carries 3

    C₃ → 3 C

    Read one symbol at a time and attach its own number to it. The 3 belongs to C.

  3. Read a symbol

    H carries 6

    3 C · 6 H

    The next capital letter starts a new symbol. The 6 belongs to H.

  4. Read a symbol

    O carries 2

    3 C · 6 H · 2 O

    That gives 3 carbon, 6 hydrogen and 2 oxygen atoms per molecule.

  5. Find a shared group

    Find the COO group in each label

    C=O and C–O in both

    Methyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH3, has the same counts. In each label, the COO group’s C has a double link to one O and a single link to the other.

  6. Follow a link

    Follow the single link to O

    O–CH₂CH₃ vs O–CH₃

    After O, ethyl methanoate continues with CH2CH3; methyl ethanoate continues with CH3. The O-linked groups differ. That difference is about connectivity, which atoms link directly.

The steps

  1. Name the unit the formula counts.

    one molecule

    Why?

    A subscript counts atoms in one unit. For a molecule that unit is one particle; for an ionic solid it is a ratio, so decide which before using the numbers.

  2. Read one symbol at a time and attach its own subscript; no number means 1.

    C₃H₆O₂ → 3 C · 6 H · 2 O

    Why?

    A subscript modifies only the symbol or group written just before it. A capital letter always starts a new symbol, and a lowercase letter stays with the capital before it.

  3. To compare arrangements, find a group the labels share.

    COO in each

    Why?

    Matching counts say nothing about which atoms touch. A shared group gives a fixed place to start comparing connections.

  4. Follow each link out of that group and compare the neighbors.

    O–CH₂CH₃ vs O–CH₃

    Why?

    Connectivity is which atoms link directly. Two molecules with the same tally can still link their atoms differently, so check a drawing or condensed label.

Problem type 2

One molecule or a repeating ratio?

Use this when you must say what a formula counts: atoms in one molecule, or ions in a repeating ratio.

Examples: magnesium sulfide, MgS, a solid; hydrogen selenide, H2Se, separate molecules.

The sketches in the slides record connections and charges, not actual sizes or angles.

  1. Look up the kinds

    Mg is a metal; S is a nonmetal

    Mg: metal · S: nonmetal

    In a lookup sorted by element kind, find Mg on the metals row and S on the nonmetals row. A metal with a nonmetal is a clue for an ionic model.

  2. Find the charges

    Each atom becomes an ion

    Mg → Mg²⁺ · S → S²⁻

    Magnesium loses two electrons in the simple charge model, giving Mg2+; sulfur gains two, giving S2−. Each charged atom is an ion. Opposite charges attract throughout a lattice, a repeating arrangement.

  3. Read the ratio

    The formula is a ratio in a lattice

    +2 and −2 → 1 : 1 → MgS

    A neutral ratio takes one of each, so MgS is a formula unit, the smallest ratio. The drawn boundary cuts a continuing ionic solid.

  4. Look up the kinds

    H and Se are both nonmetals

    H, Se: nonmetal + nonmetal

    Two nonmetals are a clue for shared electrons and separate molecules.

  5. Read the count

    The formula counts one molecule

    H₂Se: 2 H + 1 Se per molecule

    Each separate hydrogen selenide group has two H and one Se. These atoms share electrons in covalent bonds, links within each group. H2Se gives the count for one molecule. Its sketch records connections, not actual sizes or angles.

  6. Check the evidence

    Network or discrete group?

    MgS: ratio · H₂Se: one molecule

    Compare the partner types and the model evidence. Use that contrast as a clue for similar simple two-element samples. Evidence of an extended network or a discrete group can settle cases the element types leave uncertain.

The steps

  1. Find each symbol in the element-kind lookup.

    Mg: metal · S: nonmetal

    Why?

    For simple two-element samples, a metal with a nonmetal points to ions, and two nonmetals point to shared electrons. It is a clue, not a proof.

  2. For a metal with a nonmetal, find each ion’s charge and the neutral ratio.

    Mg²⁺ + S²⁻ → 1 : 1

    Why?

    Opposite charges must balance in a neutral solid. The smallest whole-number ratio that balances them is the formula unit.

  3. Read an ionic formula as a ratio in a lattice, not as one particle.

    MgS = formula unit

    Why?

    The ions continue in a repeating array, so no single MgS pair is a separate particle. The subscripts give how many of each ion per unit of the pattern.

  4. For two nonmetals, read the formula as one molecule.

    H₂Se = 2 H + 1 Se

    Why?

    Covalent bonds hold each group together, and the groups are separate, so the subscripts count atoms in one molecule.

  5. When element kinds leave it open, look for network or group evidence.

    extended → ratio · discrete → molecule

    Why?

    What the sample is made of, a continuing network or separate groups, decides what the formula counts. Element kinds only predict it.

Problem type 3

Ask two questions about an elemental sample

Use this when a question asks whether a particle is one element, two atoms, or both.

Example: One form of solid sulfur has ring units. Carbon monoselenide provides a comparison unit.

The sketches leave out actual bond orders and shape.

  1. Question 1

    How many atoms in one unit?

    ring: 8 atoms

    Count the labels in the sketch. The sulfur ring unit has eight.

  2. Question 2

    How many kinds of atom?

    ring: 1 kind → S₈

    Count the different symbols. The sulfur ring has only one kind, and its full formula is S8.

  3. Question 1

    Count the comparison unit

    C–Se: 2 atoms

    The C–Se particle has two atoms, so it is diatomic.

  4. Question 2

    Two atoms, but two kinds

    C–Se: 2 kinds

    It has two kinds of atom, so it is not a sample of one element.

  5. Know the limit

    A lone symbol does not give the grouping

    S ≠ S₈

    An isolated element symbol names an atom type, not how a sample is grouped. For an elemental form, read an elemental particle formula or a course reference that states the conditions and whether atoms occur separately or in groups. You cannot derive a roster of common elemental forms from the subscript rule.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Count the atoms in one separate unit.

    ring: 8 · pair: 2

    Why?

    Diatomic describes the whole unit: exactly two atoms. The count comes from the particle formula or model, not from the element.

  2. Count the different symbols in that unit.

    ring: 1 kind · pair: 2 kinds

    Why?

    One kind of atom means the sample is one element. A two-atom unit can still mix two elements, so this is a separate test.

  3. For an element’s usual form, read a particle formula or reference for the stated conditions.

    S names an atom type; S₈ names a unit

    Why?

    An isolated symbol names an atom type, not how a sample is grouped, and the subscript rule cannot tell you which elements pair up.

  1. Count the budget

    Add up the valence electrons

    5 + 2 × 1 + 7 = 14

    N is in group 15, so each neutral N contributes 5. The subscript gives two H at 1 each, and Cl brings 7. That is 14 valence electrons to place.

  2. Choose the skeleton

    Put N between the three end atoms

    H, H and Cl around N · 14 left

    Two H atoms can each make only one link here. Cl can fill its shell with one link and three lone pairs. So the three of them sit at the ends, and N goes in the middle.

  3. Draw the links

    One line along each link

    3 lines × 2 = 6 · 14 − 6 = 8 left

    Start with one line along each link. A line is one shared pair, so three lines use 6 electrons.

  4. Place lone pairs

    Give Cl three lone pairs

    3 pairs × 2 = 6 · 8 − 6 = 2 left

    Cl needs three lone pairs to go with its one link. They use 6 more electrons, leaving 2.

    The last 2 become a lone pair on N

    1 pair × 2 = 2 · 2 − 2 = 0 left

    The 2 electrons left become a lone pair on N. Every valence electron is now in the drawing.

    Count the whole drawing, then each atom

    6 in lines + 8 in dots = 14

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Add up the valence electrons from the formula.

    5 + 2 × 1 + 7 = 14

    Why?

    Each neutral atom brings the valence electrons its group gives it, and a subscript says how many of that atom there are. An ion would also gain one electron per negative charge or lose one per positive charge; chloramine is neutral.

  2. Choose the skeleton: put the atoms that make only one link at the ends.

    H, H and Cl around N

    Why?

    H can make only one link here, and Cl can fill its shell with one link and three lone pairs. An atom with only one link cannot sit between two others, so N goes in the middle.

  3. Draw one line for each link and take its electrons off the budget.

    3 lines: 14 − 6 = 8 left

    Why?

    A line is one shared pair. Every connection in the skeleton needs at least one, so the lines are paid for first.

  4. Spend what is left as lone pairs, two electrons at a time.

    Cl: 8 − 6 = 2 · N: 2 − 2 = 0

    Why?

    This model places electrons in pairs. Cl completes its shell with three lone pairs, and the last pair stays on N, so nothing is left over.

  5. Check both ledgers: the total once, then each atom.

    6 + 8 = 14 · N: 6 + 2 = 8

    Why?

    A shared pair counts once in the whole drawing but appears around both atoms it joins. A drawing can fill every shell with the wrong total, or match the total and leave an atom short, so check both.

The steps

  1. Build the skeleton and spend every electron as in Problem type 1.

    middle C sees 4

    Why?

    Placing every electron first shows whether any atom is short. A short atom after all 16 are spent cannot be fixed by adding electrons, because the total is fixed by the formula.

  2. Move a lone pair from a neighbor into the link it shares with the short atom.

    O pair → C–O line · middle C: 4 → 6

    Why?

    The pair stays between the same two atoms, so the global ledger does not change. The neighbor still counts the pair around itself, and the short atom gains two.

  3. Repeat with another neighbor until the short atom sees eight.

    end C pair → C–C line · 6 → 8

    Why?

    Each move adds one line to one connection and keeps the same neighbors. Only the line count on that connection, its multiplicity, goes up.

  4. Check both ledgers again, then count multiplicity per connection.

    12 + 4 = 16 · 2 single, 2 double

    Why?

    A move can fix one atom and leave another short, so recheck every atom. Multiplicity belongs to each connection, so count lines link by link.

Same method, three pairs on one link: hydrogen cyanide

Start with H–C–N and put the last 6 of the 10 electrons on N. C sees only 4. Move two N pairs into the C–N link so both atoms see 8. Check the total: 8 in lines + 2 in dots = 10.

Words to know

Separate amount, direction and shape

In a made-up scale, one atom has a pull value of 2.70 and another has 2.30. Which has the greater pull? What is the absolute difference?

Check the entry skill

2.70 is greater. The difference is 2.70 − 2.30 = 0.40 on that scale. Reversing the order of the atoms does not change the size of the difference.

Electronegativity (χ) is a relative measure of pull on shared electrons. A bond dipole models the resulting uneven charge on one bond. Use a crossed tail on the slightly positive end and point the head toward the slightly negative end. The symbols δ+ and δ− are partial charges, smaller charge shifts than making separate full ions in the simple model.

Rule for this practice set only:

  • 0.20 ≤ Δχ < 1.70: treat as polar covalent.

    Record both rows from one table

    χ(Si) = 1.90 · χ(S) = 2.58

    Find the Si row and record 1.90. Find the S row and record 2.58. Use one scale for both.

  2. Step 2 · Subtract

    Subtract the smaller value

    Δχ = 2.58 − 1.90 = 0.68

    Δχ = 2.58 − 1.90 = 0.68 (unitless, from this table). Reversing the atoms does not change the size of the difference.

  3. Step 3 · Locate

    Find the interval that owns 0.68

    0.20 ≤ 0.68 < 1.70 → polar covalent

    0.68 lies in this activity’s middle interval, so the expected label is polar covalent.

  4. Step 4 · Direction

    Point the arrow toward the larger χ

    0.68 · polar covalent · δ+ Si, δ− S

    If it is a shared Si–S link, put δ− at S and δ+ at Si; the crossed tail starts near Si.

  5. Step 5 · Report

    Use the course chart for a course label

    Report: Δχ 0.68, toward S

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Find each element’s row and record its χ from one scale.

    Si 1.90 · S 2.58

    Why?

    Numbers from different scales or rounding conventions do not compare, so both values must come from the same table.

  2. Subtract the smaller value from the larger.

    Δχ = 2.58 − 1.90 = 0.68

    Why?

    The size of the difference measures how unevenly the pair is shared. Which atom you write first does not change it.

  3. Find the interval that contains Δχ, reading each endpoint sign.

    0.20 ≤ 0.68 < 1.70 → polar covalent

    Why?

    ≤ includes its endpoint and < leaves it out, so every value, boundaries included, lands in exactly one interval.

  4. For a polar link, put δ− on the larger χ and start the crossed tail at the other atom.

    δ+ Si → δ− S

    Why?

    The atom with the greater pull draws the shared electrons toward itself, so it carries the slight negative charge and the head points to it.

  5. For a course label, use the course chart; without it, report Δχ and direction only.

    0.68, toward S; course label open

    Why?

    The cutoffs sort a gradual change, and a course may draw its lines or include its endpoints differently.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Mark the separate molecules, and look only at lines that cross between them.

    ethanol | dimethyl ether

    Why?

    A hydrogen bond is an attraction between molecules. A line inside one molecule is a bond, whatever atoms it joins.

  2. Donor check: in one molecule, find an H joined directly to a strongly pulling atom, here O.

    ethanol O–H ✓

    Why?

    An H bonded straight to O is left with a large δ+, and that is what reaches across to the neighbor. An H on C does not pass.

  3. Acceptor check: in the other molecule, find an available lone pair, here on O.

    ether O lone pair ✓

    Why?

    The H needs an unshared pair on the neighbor to point at. A molecule with only C–C and C–H links has none.

  4. If both sites are present and face each other, draw the contact dashed and keep the bond solid.

    O–H ··· O

    Why?

    The dashed H···O line is the hydrogen bond between molecules; the solid O–H line is a bond inside ethanol that a phase change leaves intact.

  5. If either check fails, name no hydrogen bond and look for other attractions.

    no donor or no acceptor → none

    Why?

    Failing the two-site test rules out only this contact. Dipole–dipole and dispersion attractions can still act.

State the property and conditions

A boiling point occurs when the liquid’s vapor pressure matches the outside pressure. Comparing two boiling points? Match pressure first. Stronger total attraction often raises boiling temperature among similar substances at the same pressure, because the liquid holds its molecules more effectively at a given temperature.

A melting point depends on how particles fit in the solid. For solid chloromethane, look at the contacts between neighboring molecules. Glossary

Subscript
A small lowered number written after an element symbol or a group. It multiplies that symbol or group within the formula. An unwritten subscript is 1.
Atom
One unit of an element; its chemical symbol names the element. A symbol starts with a capital letter and may have a second, lowercase letter.
Particle
An individual unit at the scale of the model; it can be an atom, a molecule or an ion.
Composition
In the two ester examples, compare the C, H and O tallies. They match even though the O-linked groups differ. Use that distinction when matching the course term to evidence.
Connectivity
Which atoms are direct neighbors. Compare the two ester examples to see which detail changes even when every atom count agrees.
Ion
An atom or bonded group with net electric charge. Losing electrons gives a positive charge; gaining electrons gives a negative charge.
Lattice
Here, the repeating arrangement of positive and negative ions in an ionic solid. A small drawing is a slice of that extended pattern.
Formula unit
For a simple ionic solid, the lowest whole-number ratio of the ions, written as a chemical formula. It is a counting unit and need not be an isolated particle.
Covalent bond
A link between atoms with shared electrons. The single, double and triple line labels describe the number of shared pairs in the electron dot model.
Ionic solid
A solid held together by electrical attractions in an extended array of ions. Use the MgS example to check how a change in the electron count gives each ion its charge.
Diatomic
In the C–Se model this course term describes the complete unit. Count the labeled atoms in the model and check separately whether they come from the same element.
Valence electrons
Outer electrons available in the simple main-group bonding model. For a neutral atom, use the group lookup shown in the activity.
Skeleton
The starting pattern of which atoms are connected. Use one line per link at first, then decide whether any links require more shared pairs.
Lewis electron dot structure
A drawing that shows bonded atoms, shared electron pairs and unshared valence electrons. A line counts as a shared pair; two dots next to one symbol count as a lone pair.
Bond multiplicity
The number of shared pairs along one connection. Identify the line count of the connection and match the vocabulary to an original drawing. Multiplicity does not say how evenly those pairs are shared.
Lone pair
Two electrons assigned to one atom in the Lewis drawing. They belong in that atom’s local count and in the molecule’s total count.
Octet check
Eight electrons around an atom, counting shared electrons as neighbors of both atoms. H instead has room for a duet. This is a check for the examples here, with exceptions in other species.
Formal charge
A bookkeeping label found by assigning one electron from each shared pair to each partner and both lone electrons to their own atom. Sum these labels to get the overall charge. These are distinct from partial charges.
Electronegativity
A relative number for how strongly an atom draws shared electrons. Compare only numbers from the same scale and rounding convention.
Bond dipole
A direction and amount of uneven charge in a bond model. The chemistry arrow has a crossed positive tail and a head pointing toward the negative end.
Partial charge
A slightly positive or negative part of an overall charge distribution, written δ+ or δ−. These are distinct from full ion charges.
Molecular geometry
Compare the two 1,2-dichloroethene models: atom counts and bond orders stay fixed, but the positions relative to the double bond differ.
Net dipole
The combined effect of the uneven charge distribution in a whole molecule. Equal, opposite contributions cancel in the model. Compare arrangements A and B to see how a molecule can retain an overall pull even when its individual bond arrows match in strength.
Intermolecular attraction
An attraction between distinct molecules. Find the dashed connector across the two chloromethane boxes, and compare it with the links inside each box.
Carbonyl
A group with a C=O double bond. In acetone, the carbonyl C also connects to two carbon groups.
Hydrogen-bond donor
In the course model for common neutral molecules, a group with H bonded directly to N, O or F. It must face a suitable electron-rich site.
Hydrogen-bond acceptor
A suitable electron-rich atom on a partner, commonly N, O or F with an available lone pair in these examples. Location and availability matter.
Hydrogen bond
Here, a directional attraction from a donor H to a suitable electron-rich site on another molecule. The course shortcut here has exceptions outside these examples.
Dipole–dipole attraction
A contact between molecules with permanent uneven charge. A favorable orientation puts a relatively positive region of one near a relatively negative region of another.
London dispersion
An attraction when a momentary uneven electron distribution induces a shift in its neighbor. It contributes in polar and nonpolar materials.
Polarizability
How easily an electron cloud can be distorted. It helps explain why different molecules can have different dispersion attractions.
Boiling point
The temperature at which the liquid vapor pressure matches the external pressure. The normal boiling point uses 1 atm.
Melting point
At a stated pressure, the temperature at which the solid and liquid can coexist at equilibrium. Particle arrangement in the solid matters.
