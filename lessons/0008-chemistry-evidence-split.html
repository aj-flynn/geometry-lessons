From formulas to forces

01 · Formulas

What are you counting?

Separate one particle’s recipe from the number of copies. Use a connection drawing if you need to know which atoms are neighbors.

Evidence · One particle

Read one connected particle

Symbol
Read Cl as one symbol for chlorine. C is carbon; H is hydrogen.
Small number after a symbol
Counts that atom type in one molecule. If absent, count one.
Large number in front
Counts whole copies of the recipe.

Read in two passes

A particle is the unit you count, such as an atom, a molecule (a group of bonded atoms), or an ion (an atom or group with net charge). First read the inventory in one unit. Then use the coefficient, the number before the whole formula, to scale every count.

Read each symbol before reading its subscript, the small lower number. A lowercase letter stays with its preceding capital. Two letters can name one element, so Cl does not count as two atoms.

Worked example

Turn the drawing into a formula

  1. Pick the unit. The evidence drawing shows one molecule. Count the labels: 2 C, 5 H, and 1 Cl.
  2. Write that recipe once. C2H5Cl keeps the subscripts next to the symbols they count. One Cl needs no printed subscript.
  3. Now count three copies. In 3 C2H5Cl, the sample has 3 × 2 = 6 C atoms, 3 × 5 = 15 H atoms, and 3 × 1 = 3 Cl atoms. The recipe stays fixed.

The formula gives an inventory. The drawing tells you that Cl touches carbon. Two arrangements can have the same inventory: the fictional chains Q—R—Q—R and Q—Q—R—R each contain two Q and two R.

What does the sample report tell you?

Common crystalline sulfur at room temperature and ordinary pressure contains discrete rings with eight S atoms each, packed into a solid. One ring is S8: eight atoms of one element. Other forms of sulfur can have different arrangements. In an extended ionic crystal, the formula instead records a ratio of ions in a repeating pattern. It does not count atoms in a single, separate molecule.

Try it yourself

A label without a drawing

A sample label reads 2 C3H6O. The 2 counts complete molecules. C, H, and O are carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. Write the inventory for one molecule and for the whole sample. What evidence would tell you which atoms oxygen touches?

Compare your inventories and evidence

One molecule contains 3 C, 6 H, and 1 O. The sample contains 6 C, 12 H, and 2 O. If you got only one oxygen for the sample, write both copies out and count each O. The label does not give oxygen’s neighbors; a connection drawing or structural evidence would.

Retry. At room temperature, a sample report shows Q—Q—Q as one particle. Q is a placeholder for an atom of one element. How many atoms and different elements does the particle contain? Does this report establish the arrangement at other temperatures?

Check the retry

Three atoms, one element. The report describes its stated conditions. You would need evidence for any other conditions.

Decide what the model should show

An electron-rich region between atoms in a molecule supports a shared-electron model. In an extended solid, a repeating pattern of particles with opposite net charges supports an ionic model. Losing a negative electron makes an atom more positive; gaining one makes it more negative. Moving an electron between two atoms preserves their combined charge.

A periodic-table group is a numbered column. Groups 1 (except H) and 2 often suggest losing one or two electrons; group 16 can suggest gaining two with a donor. In MgSe, Mg is in group 2 and Se is in group 16, so the clues support a charged-particle prediction. Confirm it with charge and arrangement evidence.

Which formula pairs an element from group 1 or 2 with an element from group 16: Rb2S or GeS2? Use the lookup before comparing.

Check the model prediction

Rb is in group 1 and S is in group 16, so Rb2S gives both clues. Ge is in group 14; this particular shortcut cannot decide a model for GeS2. Look for charge and structure evidence. If your course asks for a term, match it to the evidence in your course reference.

Use direct evidence. Report A shows opposite net charges in a repeating solid array. Report B shows an electron-rich region between two nuclei in a discrete particle. Choose a model for each and identify the evidence that supports it.

Compare the two reports

A supports an ionic model: opposite charges repeat through the solid. B supports a shared-electron model: the electron-rich region lies between the nuclei. A group-based prediction alone would not supply either observation.

Worked example

Use an existing pair to finish two shells

A Lewis drawing tracks valence electrons, the outer electrons used in bonding. Start with a skeleton, a proposal for the connections. In these simple structures H is terminal (at an end), because it can share only one pair. Build a connected framework from the remaining atoms, using any evidence about neighbors. A formula can allow several skeletons.

For neutral main-group atoms, groups 1 and 2 usually give 1 and 2 valence electrons, and groups 13–18 give 3–8; helium has 2. This shortcut does not cover transition metals. For a negatively charged species, add one electron per unit of negative charge. For a positively charged species, subtract one per unit of positive charge.

Start ethene, C2H4, with the supplied connections: the carbons are joined and each has two H neighbors. The neutral budget is 2 × 4 + 4 × 1 = 12 electrons. Draw one line per connection; five lines spend 10 electrons. A lone pair is two electrons not shared in a bond. If the remaining pair stays alone on the left C, the left C counts eight and the right C only six.

Predict: If the pair becomes shared between the carbons, what happens to the left count, the right count, and the whole budget?

Read the construction stages
  1. Start with the six atoms and a twelve-electron budget.
  2. Five connections use ten electrons. Two electrons remain.
  3. A trial lone pair uses the remainder, but the right carbon is two electrons short of its local octet.
  4. Move the trial pair between the carbons. The same twelve electrons now complete both carbon octets and all hydrogen shells.

The left carbon already counted the pair before it moved, so its local count stays at eight. The right carbon gains local credit and goes from six to eight. The whole drawing still has twelve electrons.

Predict, then step through the construction. All four stages are also explained in text.

In a fresh trial, start with single connections, complete terminal shells, then use remaining electrons on interior atoms. If the budget is spent and a neighbor lacks an octet (eight outer electrons), try moving a pair into a shared position. Recheck each atom. The two-electron target for H and the octet rule for the other atoms in these examples are model limits; other structures may need different rules or several valid electron placements.

Try it yourself

Choose the connections, then audit them

A neutral molecule has formula C2H4O. Structural evidence gives oxygen exactly one neighbor. In this model each C shares four pairs, O shares two, and H shares one. Draw a skeleton and show all shared and lone pairs. Use the group lookup to check your budget, and audit each local shell.

Compare the complete drawing

Hydrogen is terminal, and O has just one neighbor, so the carbons must connect to each other to keep the group connected. An H cannot supply O with two shared pairs. One C therefore shares two pairs with O. The two C atoms together have eight shared-pair positions: O uses two and the four H atoms use four. The last two positions, one on each C, make the single C—C connection.

Whole: 2 × 4 + 4 × 1 + 6 = 18 electrons. Seven shared pairs and two lone pairs account for 14 + 4 = 18. Local: each C has four shared pairs; O has two shared and two lone pairs; every H has one shared pair.

If O has two neighbors in your drawing, return to the sample clue. If your total is right but one C counts six, recount the pairs attached to that C. Without the one-neighbor clue, the formula allows other connections.

Retry. Draw the electron pairs for H—C—C—Cl (chloroacetylene). The marks here show neighbors only. Where must you add shared pairs beyond one per connection?

Compare the retry drawing

The budget is 1 + 2 × 4 + 7 = 16 electrons. Three starting lines and three lone pairs on Cl spend 12. The last four electrons make two more shared pairs between the carbons. Each C and Cl now counts eight locally; H counts two. Putting one lone pair on each C would leave both short at six.

Extra practice · dimethylamine

In dimethylamine, N joins two carbon atoms and one H. Each C also joins three H atoms. Draw the Lewis model before comparing.

Compare the electron drawing

There are 5 + 2 × 4 + 7 × 1 = 20 electrons. Nine lines use 18. The H and C shells already fit; N counts six. Its lone pair brings it to eight. Sharing that pair instead would give another atom extra local credit it does not need.

Which effects cancel?

Read one bond using electron pull. Read the whole molecule using the directions and strengths of its bond effects.

Keep three pieces of evidence separate

Electronegativity describes an atom’s pull on shared electrons. For the unitless difference ΔEN, subtract the smaller rating from the larger one. The stronger-pulling end is δ− (partial negative); the weaker is δ+ (partial positive). With equal pulls there is no stronger end. The bond dipole arrow has a crossed tail at δ+ and points toward δ−.

The number of bond lines tells how many pairs are shared. The ratings tell how uneven the sharing is in this model. A whole-molecule prediction also needs geometry, the arrangement in space. A flat Lewis drawing gives connections, so ask for a supplied shape or use a shape model such as VSEPR before treating its angles as real.

This practice key controls a display style. It does not set chemical categories. With equal pulls, ΔEN is zero and no bond dipole arrow is drawn. For a chemical label use the boundary and wording in your own course key. The signs ≤ and ≥ include equality; < excludes it.

  1. Find the size. With invented ratings 1.46 on the left and 2.69 on the right, ΔEN = 2.69 − 1.46 = 1.23.
  2. Read the boundary. The striped row includes 1.23. Mark δ+ and the crossed tail on the left. Mark δ− and point the tip right.
  3. Zoom out. If a supplied linear model has two equal effects in opposite directions, the combined effect is zero even though each bond has uneven sharing. If one effect is stronger, a combined effect remains toward the stronger one.
When a large difference suggests a charge model

On a separate invented scale, a training key flags a charged-particle prediction at a difference of 2.23 or more. Decide whether A (3.85 and 1.55) or B (3.85 and 1.65) meets it. What evidence would confirm the prediction for a solid?

Compare the two predictions

A gives 2.30 and meets the flag; B gives 2.20 and does not. The flag is a practice rule. For a real solid, test for opposite net charges and a repeating particle arrangement.

Reveal the static comparison after predicting

At 180°, two equal opposite vectors cancel. At 90°, the vectors point up-left and up-right. Their sideways parts cancel and their upward parts add, leaving a resultant about 1.41 model units straight up. Each input vector is 1 model unit long. Changing the supplied angle explores how the directions affect the sum.

To add plain vectors, move the second arrow’s tail to the first arrow’s tip without turning it. The arrow from the original start to the final tip is their sum. These abstract vectors show how supplied directions combine; they cannot predict the molecule’s shape.

Try it yourself

Use the bond sketch below. Look up both values. Find ΔEN, use the practice fill key, and draw the crossed-tail arrow with both partial charges. If a second shared pair is added while the ratings stay fixed, what changes in the drawing?

Separately, a supplied model has an effect of 2 model units pointing left and an effect of 3 model units pointing right. Find the combined size and direction. What extra evidence would you need if you had only a flat connection drawing?

The opposing effects leave 3 − 2 = 1 model unit pointing right. With only a connection drawing, ask for the shape in space and the directions and strengths of the effects.

Retry. A new training bond rates the left end 2.23 and the right end 2.60. Which fill includes the difference? Where is the crossed tail?

04 · Attractions

What moves apart?

Locate each attraction and ask what the conditions let you compare.

Evidence · Between molecules

Temporary shifts, lasting ends

One contact
Check which groups face each other and which interactions are possible.
Whole sample
Check the number and strength of contacts, then the conditions for the property.

Find the boundary around a molecule

Intermolecular forces are interactions between molecules. When a molecular liquid boils without decomposing, intact molecules separate. The bonds inside each molecule stay in place.

Two A—B pairs start near each other in the diagram. The sample will spread out. Two possible stories are that whole pairs move apart or that both A—B bonds break. Before running the model, predict the total atom count and the number of connected A—B groups for each story. Which count could distinguish them? Then use Next step or reveal the stages to compare with the model.

Reveal the model stages after predicting
  1. Start with four atoms in two connected A—B groups. The dotted gap between the groups is short.
  2. The dotted gap is longer. There are still two connected A—B groups.
  3. The groups are farther apart. Both A—B connections are still present and the total is still four atoms.

Both proposed stories preserve four atoms. Two connected A—B groups remain if the pairs move intact; none remain if both bonds break. The model shows two connected groups after they separate. That count distinguishes the stories; a larger gap alone does not.

Make a prediction, then reveal the stages or use Next step.

Temporary shifts in electron distribution can induce shifts in neighbors, giving London dispersion attractions. All atoms and molecules can experience these. Molecules with a permanent dipole, a lasting separation of partial charge, can also have dipole–dipole attractions. The two contributions can coexist.

Worked example

Test both conditions for one contact

In common introductory examples, hydrogen bonding needs H directly bonded to N, O, or F, plus a suitable nearby lone pair. Use the given lone-pair evidence here. These are partial connection sketches; distance and orientation also matter.

Contact A meets both conditions. B has the nearby pair, but H is bonded to C. C has the required direct bond, but no suitable nearby pair. Check both columns independently before naming the contact.

Then compare the whole sample

In a fictional additive model, one R contact takes 4 energy units to separate and one S contact takes 2. Packet A has one R; packet B has three S. One R is stronger than one S. Yet packet B takes 3 × 2 = 6 energy units, more than A’s 4. A course chart can rank typical individual contacts. A real sample’s total also depends on how many contacts form, how close particles get, and how easily their electrons shift.

A liquid boils when its vapor pressure, the pressure from its own vapor at balance with the liquid, reaches the external pressure. At the same external pressure, otherwise similar molecular liquids with stronger total attractions usually boil higher. For melting, ask how the particles pack in the solid. Extended ionic patterns and molecular solids need different particle models; a force name alone cannot determine an exact melting point.

Try it yourself

Make a fair property comparison

Check two near misses. One proposed contact has S—H facing an available suitable lone pair. Another has N—H but no available suitable nearby pair. Which condition fails in each? Is finding just one condition enough?

Compare the two missing conditions

S—H fails the stated introductory direct-bond condition, which requires H bonded to N, O, or F, even with a nearby pair. N—H meets that condition but lacks the suitable partner pair. Neither meets both stated conditions.

Choose what to compare. In a new fictional additive model, each T contact costs 3 energy units to separate and each U costs 1. Packet X has two T contacts; packet Y has seven U contacts. Which single contact is stronger? Which packet costs more to separate? Show both calculations.

Compare one contact and each whole packet

One T is stronger than one U: 3 energy units versus 1. Packet X costs 2 × 3 = 6 energy units; packet Y costs 7 × 1 = 7 energy units. Y costs more in total despite having weaker individual contacts. These invented additive units do not measure a real liquid's attractions.

Invented liquids A and B contain molecules of similar size and shape. B has stronger total attractions. Report 1 measures boiling at the same external pressure. Report 2 compares melting, but gives no evidence about packing. Predict what each report can support.

Which comparison follows from these conditions?

Compare the two property claims

At fixed pressure, otherwise similar B is likely to boil higher because it has stronger total attractions. The melting claim needs solid packing evidence.

Retry. A third report measures B at a lower pressure than A. Can its two boiling temperatures isolate the effect of attractions? Explain.

Check the retry

No. Lower external pressure changes when the vapor reaches the boiling condition. Ask for measurements at the same pressure before comparing the effect of attractions.

Independent practice

Which evidence would you use?

Write a response to each report before opening its comparison. For each one, name the fact that supports your claim and say what it leaves undecided.

Report A

In a fictional model, Q, R, and T label three different atom types. A tray label reads 3 Q2R4T and counts three complete particles. A sensor records three T atoms. Can the label and reading both be right? Write the rest of the sample inventory. Can you identify T’s neighbors from those counts?

Compare report A

Yes: one T per particle means three across the tray. There are 3 × 2 = 6 Q atoms and 3 × 4 = 12 R atoms. These counts do not identify T’s neighbors; ask for a connection drawing.

Report B

A connected neutral particle has two H atoms and one Se atom. H is in group 1 and Se in group 16. The sample uses the simple shell model. Make a drawing showing the connections and all electron pairs. An instrument now removes one valence electron from one particle. What can you say about its charge and total remaining valence electrons? Does that count give its new arrangement?

Compare report B

H must be at both ends. Put Se between them and draw two single connections. The 2 × 1 + 6 = 8 valence electrons include four in bonds and four as two lone pairs on Se. Se counts eight locally and each H counts two. Removing one electron leaves charge +1 and seven valence electrons. The budget alone does not show the new structure.

Report C

A supplied linear model is L—M—R. The middle atom M has a rating of 1.9; both outer atoms L and R have ratings of 2.7 on the same invented scale. The bonds are identical, and each line represents one shared pair. A classmate says that two uneven connections guarantee a lasting uneven charge across the group. Draw the two bond-effect directions. What does this model support?

Compare report C

Each local effect has ΔEN = 2.7 − 1.9 = 0.8 and points toward its higher rating. The two supplied effects have equal size and opposite direction, so they cancel in the model. One line records one shared pair; a count of lines cannot settle the whole-group claim.

Report D

A liquid and its vapor contain the same connected groups. In the liquid, an N—H group faces a suitable lone pair on a neighboring molecule. Each molecule has lasting partial positive and negative ends. Brief electron shifts also induce shifts in neighbors. A different sample has similar-sized molecules and unknown contact counts. Which attractions does the evidence support, and why? Does it show broken N—H connections? Can you rank the samples’ boiling temperatures from the observed contact alone?

Compare report D

The same connected groups support separation without breaking N—H bonds. The direct N—H connection and suitable partner pair support a possible hydrogen-bond contact. Lasting partial-charge ends support dipole–dipole attractions; the temporary induced shifts support dispersion. These contributions can coexist. One contact cannot determine total attraction. Ask about contact strengths and counts, molecular shape, and a common external pressure.

Shared reference

Glossary

Particle
A general term for the unit being counted. Say whether it is an atom, molecule, ion, or another unit.
Molecule
A discrete group of atoms held together by bonds.
Ion
An atom or group with a net electric charge. Net means the overall balance.
Subscript
A small number that follows a symbol or group in a formula. It counts that part within one written recipe.
Coefficient
A number before a complete formula that counts copies of the whole recipe.
Ionic crystal
A solid with ions arranged in an ordered repeating pattern.
Periodic-table group
A numbered column in the periodic table.
Valence electrons
Electrons that can take part in bonding. For the main-group atoms here, count the outer-shell electrons.
Lewis drawing
An accounting model for valence electrons and connections. Here a line represents a shared pair and two dots show a lone pair.
Skeleton
A proposal for which atoms connect. Terminal atoms are at the ends.
Lone pair
Two electrons shown on one atom without being shared in a bond.
Octet
Eight electrons around an atom, a useful shell target in many simple models.
Electronegativity / ΔEN
An atom’s pull on shared electrons. ΔEN is the absolute difference between the two unitless ratings from the same scale.
Partial charge
Uneven electron distribution leaves one region slightly negative (δ−) and another slightly positive (δ+).
Bond dipole arrow
A chemical arrow whose tip points toward the partial negative end and whose crossed tail marks the partial positive end.
Geometry
The arrangement of parts in space, including their directions and angles.
VSEPR
Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion: a model for shape based on how groups of electrons spread around an atom.
Vector
An arrow with size and direction. The sum of several vectors is their resultant.
Intermolecular
Between molecules. Intramolecular means within a molecule.
London dispersion
Attraction arising from temporary, correlated shifts in electron distribution.
Permanent dipole
A lasting separation of partial positive and partial negative charge within a particle.
Hydrogen bonding
In these examples, an attraction involving H directly bonded to N, O, or F and a suitable nearby lone pair.
Vapor pressure
The pressure of a substance’s vapor when evaporation and condensation balance. Boiling starts when vapor pressure reaches the surrounding pressure.
Packing
How particles fit next to one another in a solid.
