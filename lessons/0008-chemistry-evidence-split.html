Read what a formula fixes

In Si2H6, what does the small 2 modify? Name the symbol that starts with S.

Check the entry skill

It modifies Si, the symbol for silicon. A lowercase i belongs to the same symbol as S here.

Magnesium loses two electrons in the simple charge model, giving Mg2+; sulfur gains two, giving S2−. Each charged atom is an ion. Opposite charges attract throughout a lattice, a repeating arrangement. A neutral ratio takes one of each, so MgS is a formula unit, the smallest ratio. The drawn boundary cuts a continuing ionic solid.

Each separate hydrogen selenide group has two H and one Se. These atoms share electrons in covalent bonds, links within each group. H2Se gives the count for one molecule. Its sketch records connections, not actual sizes or angles.

In a lookup sorted by element kind, find Mg on the metals row and S on the nonmetals row. For the MgS and H₂Se cases, compare the partner types and the model evidence. Use that contrast as a clue for similar simple two-element samples. Evidence of an extended network or a discrete group can settle cases the element types leave uncertain.

Check the entry skill

The whole drawing contains two electrons in that pair. Both atoms have those same two electrons around them.

A Lewis electron dot structure records where the outer electrons are counted. A skeleton shows which atoms link. A line is one shared pair. Two dots beside one atom are a lone pair, a pair assigned to that atom alone. The starting stock is the number of valence electrons, the outer electrons counted in this model.

Read subscripts as atom counts; multiply each count by its valence entry. For an ion, add one electron per unit of negative charge or remove one per unit of positive charge. The neutral examples below need neither adjustment. Check the total once for the whole particle and check each atom separately. For H look for two around it; for the other atoms here use the octet check, looking for eight. These targets are useful for this set; odd-electron particles and some other species fall outside the shortcut.

When ready, trace molecular pull.

Separate amount, direction and shape

In a made-up scale, one atom has a pull value of 2.70 and another has 2.30. Which has the greater pull? What is the absolute difference?

Check the entry skill

2.70 is greater. The difference is 2.70 − 2.30 = 0.40 on that scale. Reversing the order of the atoms does not change the size of the difference.

Electronegativity (χ) is a relative measure of pull on shared electrons. A bond dipole models the resulting uneven charge on one bond. Use a crossed tail on the slightly positive end and point the head toward the slightly negative end. The symbols δ+ and δ− are partial charges, smaller charge shifts than making separate full ions in the simple model.

Keep two questions apart: how many pairs does a link share, and how unevenly? Lines in an electron dot drawing address the first. A pull difference, Δχ, addresses the second. Use the size of the difference for degree of unevenness and the larger χ for direction when the model has a meaningful bond dipole.

State the property and conditions

A boiling point occurs when the liquid’s vapor pressure matches the outside pressure. Comparing two boiling points? Match pressure first. Stronger total attraction often raises boiling temperature among similar substances at the same pressure, because the liquid holds its molecules more effectively at a given temperature.

A melting point depends on how particles fit in the solid. For solid chloromethane, look at the contacts between neighboring molecules. For solid magnesium sulfide, picture a repeating array of Mg²⁺ and S²⁻. Identify the neighbors of one ion in that array. To rank melting temperatures, also look up the measured transitions and their conditions.

A single named contact cannot tell you total attraction. Glossary

Subscript
A small lowered number written after an element symbol or a group. It multiplies that symbol or group within the formula. An unwritten subscript is 1.
Atom
One unit of an element; its chemical symbol names the element. A symbol starts with a capital letter and may have a second, lowercase letter.
Particle
An individual unit at the scale of the model; it can be an atom, a molecule or an ion.
Composition
In the two ester examples, compare the C, H and O tallies. They match even though the O-linked groups differ. Use that distinction when matching the course term to evidence.
Connectivity
Which atoms are direct neighbors. Compare the two ester examples to see which detail changes even when every atom count agrees.
Ion
An atom or bonded group with net electric charge. Losing electrons gives a positive charge; gaining electrons gives a negative charge.
Lattice
Here, the repeating arrangement of positive and negative ions in an ionic solid. A small drawing is a slice of that extended pattern.
Formula unit
For a simple ionic solid, the lowest whole-number ratio of the ions, written as a chemical formula. It is a counting unit and need not be an isolated particle.
Covalent bond
A link between atoms with shared electrons. The single, double and triple line labels describe the number of shared pairs in the electron dot model.
Ionic solid
A solid held together by electrical attractions in an extended array of ions. Use the MgS example to check how a change in the electron count gives each ion its charge.
Diatomic
In the C–Se model this course term describes the complete unit. Count the labeled atoms in the model and check separately whether they come from the same element.
Valence electrons
Outer electrons available in the simple main-group bonding model. For a neutral atom, use the group lookup shown in the activity.
Skeleton
The starting pattern of which atoms are connected. Use one line per link at first, then decide whether any links require more shared pairs.
Lewis electron dot structure
A drawing that shows bonded atoms, shared electron pairs and unshared valence electrons. A line counts as a shared pair; two dots next to one symbol count as a lone pair.
Bond multiplicity
The number of shared pairs along one connection. Identify the line count of the connection and match the vocabulary to an original drawing. Multiplicity does not say how evenly those pairs are shared.
Lone pair
Two electrons assigned to one atom in the Lewis drawing. They belong in that atom’s local count and in the molecule’s total count.
Octet check
Eight electrons around an atom, counting shared electrons as neighbors of both atoms. H instead has room for a duet. This is a check for the examples here, with exceptions in other species.
Formal charge
A bookkeeping label found by assigning one electron from each shared pair to each partner and both lone electrons to their own atom. Sum these labels to get the overall charge. These are distinct from partial charges.
Electronegativity
A relative number for how strongly an atom draws shared electrons. Compare only numbers from the same scale and rounding convention.
Bond dipole
A direction and amount of uneven charge in a bond model. The chemistry arrow has a crossed positive tail and a head pointing toward the negative end.
Partial charge
A slightly positive or negative part of an overall charge distribution, written δ+ or δ−. These are distinct from full ion charges.
Molecular geometry
Compare the two 1,2-dichloroethene models: atom counts and bond orders stay fixed, but the positions relative to the double bond differ.
Net dipole
The combined effect of the uneven charge distribution in a whole molecule. Equal, opposite contributions cancel in the model. Compare arrangements A and B to see how a molecule can retain an overall pull even when its individual bond arrows match in strength.
Intermolecular attraction
An attraction between distinct molecules. Find the dashed connector across the two chloromethane boxes, and compare it with the links inside each box.
Carbonyl
A group with a C=O double bond. In acetone, the carbonyl C also connects to two carbon groups.
Hydrogen-bond donor
In the course model for common neutral molecules, a group with H bonded directly to N, O or F. It must face a suitable electron-rich site.
Hydrogen-bond acceptor
A suitable electron-rich atom on a partner, commonly N, O or F with an available lone pair in these examples. Location and availability matter.
Hydrogen bond
Here, a directional attraction from a donor H to a suitable electron-rich site on another molecule. The course shortcut here has exceptions outside these examples.
Dipole–dipole attraction
A contact between molecules with permanent uneven charge. A favorable orientation puts a relatively positive region of one near a relatively negative region of another.
London dispersion
An attraction when a momentary uneven electron distribution induces a shift in its neighbor. It contributes in polar and nonpolar materials.
Polarizability
How easily an electron cloud can be distorted. It helps explain why different molecules can have different dispersion attractions.
Boiling point
The temperature at which the liquid vapor pressure matches the external pressure. The normal boiling point uses 1 atm.
Melting point
At a stated pressure, the temperature at which the solid and liquid can coexist at equilibrium. Particle arrangement in the solid matters.
