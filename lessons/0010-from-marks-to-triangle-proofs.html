Words to know

Read the marks before you reason

A diagram tells you a fact only through its marks. Use ≅ for congruent figures: they match in size and shape. Matching marks say two parts are congruent.

Tick marks
Matching ticks mark equal lengths.In the diagram: the two segments with one tick each.
Arcs
Matching arcs mark equal angle measures.In the diagram: the two angles with one arc each, turned different ways.
Square corner
A little square marks a 90° angle.In the diagram: the corner with the small square.
Arrowheads
Matching arrowheads on straight lines mark parallel lines: they never meet.In the diagram: the two lines with one arrowhead each.

A mark is evidence. How the drawing looks is not.

Words to know

What two crossing strips tell you

G, S, K lie on one straight segment in that order. H, S, W lie on another in that order. Here GS ≅ SK is given by the ticks. Nothing marks HS and SW as equal.

Vertical angles
Opposite angles formed by two intersecting lines. ∠GSH ≅ ∠KSW: each makes a straight angle when added to ∠HSK, so they have the same measure.In the diagram: the two blue arcs at S. ∠HSK is the yellow arc between them.
Midpoint
S is the midpoint of GK. S lies on GK and GS ≅ SK. A midpoint needs both facts.In the diagram: S, with one tick on each side of it along GK.
Bisect
HW bisects GK. It passes through GK’s midpoint. To say they bisect each other, you would also need S to be the midpoint of HW.In the diagram: HS and SW have no marks, so HW is not shown bisected.

V is on LF and LV = VF = 9 cm, so V is the midpoint of LF. ER passes through V, so ER bisects LF. The reverse fails: EV and VR are 4 cm and 6 cm, so V is not the midpoint of ER. Revise any claim that says both braces are bisected.

Words to know

Decide what fixes a triangle

Two triangles are congruent when their corresponding parts match: the parts that play the same roles. You do not need all six pairs. Each test below names a set of three pairs that is enough. The word included matters: an included angle is between the two named sides, and an included side joins the two named angles.

Quick method card

Each card shows the marks on one triangle. The other triangle needs the matching marks.

SSS

Three corresponding side pairs.

In the diagram: one, two and three ticks, one pair on every side.
SAS

Two side pairs and the angle pair between those sides.

In the diagram: the arc sits at the corner where the two ticked sides meet.
ASA

Two angle pairs and the side pair joining those angles.

In the diagram: the ticked side runs from one arc to the other.
AAS

Two angle pairs and a side pair not joining those angles. Two equal angle pairs force the third pair equal, which lets you use ASA.

In the diagram: the ticked side touches only one of the marked angles.
HL

For two right triangles only: a hypotenuse pair (sides opposite the right angles) and a leg pair (sides touching the right angles).

In the diagram: the square marks the right angle, the hypotenuse is across from it, and the leg touches it.

Facts with no mark Sometimes a needed fact has no mark. A shared side is congruent to itself (the reflexive property). If you know both angles are right, they are congruent. If you only think they look right, you cannot use that fact.

Problem type 1

Match the corners in order

Use this before you write any congruence statement. The example is two triangular brackets, one turned upside down.

  1. Read the marks

    List the marked pairs

    KR ≅ UT, KV ≅ UJ, ∠RKV ≅ ∠TUJ

    KR and UT have one tick. KV and UJ have two ticks. ∠RKV and ∠TUJ have one arc.

  2. Start at a corner

    Start where two marked sides touch

    K ↔ U

    KR and KV meet at K. Their partners UT and UJ meet at U. So K matches U.

  3. One tick

    Follow the one-tick sides

    R ↔ T

    KR matches UT and K matches U, so the other ends match: R with T.

  4. Two ticks

    Follow the two-tick sides

    V ↔ J

    KV matches UJ, so V matches J. These are corresponding vertices: they play the same roles.

  5. Write it

    Keep the order

    △KRV ≅ △UTJ

    Notice the order: K ↔ U, R ↔ T, V ↔ J. Matching letters sit in the same positions, wherever the triangles sit on the page.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. List each marked pair from the marks.

    KR ≅ UT, KV ≅ UJ, ∠RKV ≅ ∠TUJ

    Why?

    Matching ticks mark equal lengths and matching arcs mark equal angle measures. A mark is evidence; how the drawing looks is not.

  2. Start where two marked sides touch, in both triangles.

    K ↔ U

    Why?

    The corner shared by two marked sides has to match the corner shared by their partners. Corresponding vertices play the same roles.

  3. Follow each marked side to its other endpoint.

    R ↔ T, V ↔ J

    Why?

    A side is named by its two endpoints. Once one endpoint is matched, the other endpoint of the partner side must match the other endpoint.

  4. Write the two triangles with matching letters in the same positions.

    △KRV ≅ △UTJ

    Why?

    The order of the letters is the matching. It does not depend on where the triangles sit on the page; one bracket here is upside down.

Problem type 2

Choose the congruence test

Use this when the marks give three pairs and you have to name the test that fits, or say that none does.

  1. Sort the marks

    Count sides and angles

    S: KR ≅ UT · S: KV ≅ UJ · A: ∠RKV ≅ ∠TUJ

    Two side pairs, by the ticks, and one angle pair, by the arcs.

  2. Where is the angle?

    Is the angle between the two sides?

    KR and KV meet at K

    The arc is at K, where KR and KV meet. So ∠RKV and ∠TUJ are included angles: the angles between the two known sides.

  3. Why it works

    Side, angle, side leaves no choice

    R, K and V are fixed

    Line up one known side, open the same angle, and go the same distance along the second side. Both outer endpoints are fixed, so the third side RV has no choice.

  4. Name the test

    Match the card

    △KRV ≅ △UTJ by SAS

    SAS: side-angle-side. Two side pairs and the angle pair between those sides.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Sort the marked pairs into sides and angles.

    S, S, A

    Why?

    Every test on the method card is made of three pairs. Knowing how many are sides and how many are angles narrows it to one or two tests.

  2. Check where each marked angle sits compared with the marked sides.

    arc at K, between KR and KV

    Why?

    An included angle is between the two named sides; an included side joins the vertices of the two named angles. The tests depend on this position, not just on the count.

  3. Match the pattern to a test on the card. If none fits, say what is missing.

    SAS

    Why?

    SAS needs the angle between the two sides. Line up one side, open the same angle, and go the same distance along the second side: both outer endpoints are fixed, so the third side has no choice and there is only one triangle.

  4. Write the statement in matching order, with the test as the reason.

    △KRV ≅ △UTJ by SAS

    Why?

    The test is the reason for the statement. Keeping the letters in matching order is what lets you use the matched parts next.

Predict

What if the angle is at the other end?

Suppose the base AB and side BG are fixed, and the angle at A is fixed. Predict: can the last corner land in two spots on the same ray from A?

Compare with a two-position sketch

Yes. Take △ABG and △ABH. They share AB and the angle at A, and BG ≅ BH. But AG and AH have different lengths. Here the known angle is not between the two known sides. Two side pairs and a non-included angle (SSA) do not guarantee congruence.

Angle marks alone cannot fix size either. You can enlarge a triangle and keep all its angles.

Hint 1 · Match like marks

Find the two one-arc corners first, then the two two-arc corners. That leaves one vertex in each triangle.

Hint 2 · Follow the side

Check the congruence test

Compare your full reasoning

△CFI ≅ △MOS by AAS. The single arcs pair C with M; the double arcs pair F with O. I matches S. The side that would join the marked angles is CF (and MO), but the tick is on FI (and OS). If you wrote ASA, revise by naming the endpoints of the marked side.

Problem type 3

Conclude matching parts with CPCTC

Use this when you have proved two triangles congruent and need a side or angle that was not marked.

  1. Proved first

    Start from the proved statement

    △KRV ≅ △UTJ (SAS)

    CPCTC works only after the triangles are proved congruent.

  2. Name the part

    Find the part by its endpoints

    RV: 2nd and 3rd letters of △KRV

    RV has no mark. Its endpoints, R and V, are the second and third letters of △KRV.

  3. Same positions

    Read the same positions in the other triangle

    2nd and 3rd letters of △UTJ: T, J

    Match both endpoints, not where the sides sit on the page. R ↔ T and V ↔ J.

  4. Conclude

    State it with the reason

    RV ≅ TJ by CPCTC

    Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are congruent.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Prove the triangles congruent before you use their parts.

    △KRV ≅ △UTJ by SAS

    Why?

    CPCTC starts from a congruence. Without a proved congruence, the parts have not been matched yet.

  2. Name the part you want by its endpoints and find their positions.

    RV → R (2nd), V (3rd)

    Why?

    Corresponding parts match by position in the ordered statement, not by where they sit on the page.

  3. Read the same positions in the other triangle.

    T (2nd), J (3rd) → TJ

    Why?

    The order K ↔ U, R ↔ T, V ↔ J was fixed when you wrote the statement, so the same positions give the matching part.

  4. Write the conclusion with CPCTC as the reason.

    RV ≅ TJ

    Why?

    Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are congruent. Once the triangles match, every pair of matching parts matches.

Words to know

Use an angle fact in either direction

Look at ∠MKW and ∠KWQ. Both are between the upright lines, but one is on each side of the slanted line. They are alternate interior angles.

Transversal
A line that crosses two other lines.In the diagram: the slanted line through K and W.
Interior
The region between the two crossed lines.In the diagram: the shaded band between the upright lines.
Alternate interior angles
Angles between the two lines and on opposite sides of the transversal.In the diagram: ∠MKW and ∠KWQ.

Start with parallel lines If MK ∥ WQ is known, then ∠MKW ≅ ∠KWQ.

Start with matching angles If ∠MKW ≅ ∠KWQ is known, then MK ∥ WQ. Reversing the direction is called taking the converse.

Use one of these rules only after you have proved its starting fact or read it in the givens.

Mixed practice

Prove it on your own

Write your statements and reasons before opening any comparison. Use the diagram and the givens, not a ruler.

Prove it

A frame with an unmarked pair of rails

Can you prove that AN and EH are parallel? Start by writing any triangle congruence you can prove, in matching order, and give a reason for each fact you use. Then justify your answer about the rails.

Compare after trying both parts
  1. AE ≅ HN (ticks) and AH ≅ HA (shared side).
  2. ∠EAH ≅ ∠NHA. AE and NH are parallel (arrows), and AH crosses both. Those angles lie between the parallels on opposite sides of AH: they are alternate interior angles.
  3. △AEH ≅ △HNA by SAS. The angles in step 2 lie between the two sides named in step 1.
  4. ∠EHA ≅ ∠NAH by CPCTC. These angles sit between EH and AN, on opposite sides of AH. By the converse of the alternate interior angles theorem, EH ∥ AN.

If you assumed both pairs of outside rails were parallel, remove that line. Only the arrowed pair is given. Replace the assumption with the angle pair from the proven congruence.

Fresh retry

The panel with two square corners

What can you prove about the two triangles? Write a conclusion in matching order and justify it. If it follows, also prove YT ≅ ZR without measuring.

Compare after writing your reasons

△RYT and △TZR are right triangles because of the square marks. RT is opposite both right angles, so it is a shared hypotenuse. RY ≅ TZ by the ticks. Therefore △RYT ≅ △TZR by HL. Y matches Z and T matches R, so YT ≅ ZR by CPCTC. If you wrote SAS, check whether the right angle is between the marked leg and shared hypotenuse. It is not.

When you go back to a diagram

  1. Label each fact: marked, stated, or proved. Do not add facts from appearance.
  2. Match vertices and check the exact conditions of the test. If none fits, say what is missing.
  3. Only then use the matched parts to prove something new. Give a reason for each step.

Glossary

Vertex
A corner. In a three-letter angle name, the vertex is the middle letter.
Parallel
Distinct straight lines in the same plane that never meet. Write ∥. Parallel segments lie on parallel lines.
Congruent
Exactly the same size and shape, even after a slide, turn, or flip. Write ≅. For two angles, this means equal measures. For two segments, it means equal lengths.
Vertical angles
Opposite angles formed where two straight lines intersect. They have equal measures. Adjacent angles at the intersection are not vertical.
Midpoint
The point on a segment that divides it into two equally long parts.
Bisect
Divide into two equal parts. For one segment to bisect another, it must pass through the other segment’s midpoint. For mutual bisection, that point must be the midpoint of both.
Corresponding parts
Parts that match by position in an ordered congruence statement, not by where they sit on the page. Match the endpoints to pair sides.
Included angle or side
The included angle is between the two named sides. The included side joins the vertices of the two named angles.
CPCTC
Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are congruent. Once the triangles are proved congruent, the order tells you which angles and sides match.
Hypotenuse
The side opposite the right angle in a right triangle. The other two sides are the legs.
Reflexive property of congruence
A figure is congruent to itself. When two triangles use the same segment as a side, that side is the same length in both triangles.
Alternate interior angles
Angles between two lines and on opposite sides of a third line that crosses them both (a transversal). If the crossed lines are parallel, the angle pair is congruent.
Converse
A statement that reverses the if and then of another statement. For alternate interior angles, the converse says that if the angle pair is congruent, the crossed lines are parallel. A converse needs its own justification; not every converse is true.

Read a little more

Euclid’s Elements, Book I, Proposition 4 shows the idea behind SAS: line up one side, use the included angle to line up the other side, and use the two lengths to match the endpoints. The language is older, but the “place one triangle on top of the other” idea is the same.
