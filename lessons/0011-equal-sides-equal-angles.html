Geometry · Isosceles triangles

Equal sides, equal angles

Two equal sides force two equal angles. Two equal angles force two equal sides. The skill is finding which ones.

Before you start

In triangle ABC, which side is opposite the angle at B? Which two sides touch the angle at B?

Compare the warm-up

The side opposite B is AC: it is the one side that does not have B as an endpoint. The two sides that touch B are BA and BC. Every angle in a triangle has exactly one opposite side. This whole lesson runs on that pairing.

Words to know

The parts of an isosceles triangle

Matching tick marks mean congruent sides: the same length. A triangle with at least two congruent sides is isosceles. Each part has a name, and the names follow the marks, not which way the triangle is turned.

Legs
The two congruent sides.In the diagram: JK and JL, each with one tick.
Vertex angle
The angle between the two legs, at the corner where they meet.In the diagram: ∠KJL, at J.
Base
The third side. It joins the far ends of the legs.In the diagram: KL.
Base angles
The two angles at the ends of the base. Each one is across from a leg.In the diagram: ∠JKL at K and ∠JLK at L.

Isosceles Triangle Theorem (Base Angles Theorem) If two sides of a triangle are congruent, then the angles opposite those sides are congruent.

Why is the theorem true?

Draw the segment from J to the midpoint of KL; call that point W. Then JK ≅ JL (given), KW ≅ WL (midpoint) and JW ≅ JW (shared side). So △JKW ≅ △JLW by SSS, and the corresponding angles ∠JKW and ∠JLW are congruent. Those are the two base angles.

Problem type 1

Equal sides → which angles match?

Use this when the marks are on two sides and the question asks about angles.

  1. Read the marks

    Find the congruent sides

    JK ≅ JL

    JK and JL each have one tick mark, so they are congruent. They are the legs.

  2. Across from JL

    Go across from the first marked side

    opposite JL → ∠JKL

    JL touches J and L. The one corner it does not touch is K, so the angle opposite JL is ∠JKL.

  3. Across from JK

    Go across from the second marked side

    opposite JK → ∠JLK

    JK touches J and K. The corner it does not touch is L, so the angle opposite JK is ∠JLK.

  4. Leave one out

    Skip the angle between the marks

    ∠KJL is not in the pair

    Both marked sides touch J, so the angle at J is opposite neither of them. It is the vertex angle. Including it is the most common slip.

  5. Conclude

    State the congruent angles

    ∠JKL ≅ ∠JLK

    The two base angles are congruent. Three-letter names say exactly which angle you mean, with the corner in the middle.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Find the two congruent sides from the marks.

    JK ≅ JL

    Why?

    Matching tick marks mean congruent segments. The theorem needs two congruent sides of the same triangle, so start by finding them.

  2. For each marked side, go across to the corner it does not touch.

    JL → K, JK → L

    Why?

    The theorem pairs each side with its opposite angle. In a triangle, the angle opposite a side is at the one vertex that is not an endpoint of that side.

  3. Leave out the angle where the two marked sides meet.

    skip ∠KJL

    Why?

    That vertex angle touches both marked sides, so it is opposite neither. The theorem says nothing about it.

  4. Write the congruence with three-letter angle names.

    ∠JKL ≅ ∠JLK

    Why?

    The middle letter names the corner. Three letters make the statement exact, which matters as soon as a corner belongs to more than one angle.

Try it

Which angles match?

Name the two angles that must be congruent. For each marked side, say which angle is opposite it.

Hint · Find the angle between the marks

The two marked sides meet at one vertex. That angle is between them, so it is not in the pair. Look at the other two corners.

Check your pair of angles

Compare your reasoning

GF and GH are the legs. They meet at G, so ∠FGH is the vertex angle and FH is the base. GH is opposite F, and GF is opposite H. So ∠GFH ≅ ∠GHF. If you included the angle at G, go back to the warm-up: an angle's opposite side never touches it.

Problem type 2

Equal angles → which sides match?

Use this when the marks are on two angles and the question asks about sides. This is the converse: the if and then are swapped.

Converse of the Isosceles Triangle Theorem If two angles of a triangle are congruent, then the sides opposite those angles are congruent.

  1. Read the marks

    Find the congruent angles

    ∠PQR ≅ ∠PRQ

    The angles at Q and at R each have one arc, so they are congruent. No side is marked yet.

  2. Across from Q

    Go across from the first marked angle

    opposite ∠Q → PR

    The angle at Q is opposite the side that does not touch Q. That side is PR.

  3. Across from R

    Go across from the second marked angle

    opposite ∠R → PQ

    The angle at R is opposite the side that does not touch R. That side is PQ.

  4. Conclude

    State the congruent sides

    PR ≅ PQ

    By the converse, PR and PQ are congruent. △PQR is isosceles with legs PQ and PR.

  5. Name the base

    The side between the marked angles

    QR is the base

    QR joins the two marked angles, so it touches both and is opposite neither. Nothing says it matches anything.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Find the two congruent angles from the arcs.

    ∠PQR ≅ ∠PRQ

    Why?

    Matching arcs mean congruent angles. The converse needs two congruent angles in one triangle.

  2. For each marked angle, go across to the side it does not touch.

    ∠Q → PR, ∠R → PQ

    Why?

    The converse pairs each angle with the side opposite it, the same pairing as the theorem, just read the other way.

  3. State that those two sides are congruent.

    PR ≅ PQ

    Why?

    That is exactly what the converse says. It is true, but it needs its own proof, because swapping if and then does not automatically keep a statement true.

  4. Name the base: the side joining the marked angles.

    QR

    Why?

    The base touches both marked angles, so neither angle is opposite it. You can say nothing about its length from these marks.

Why does the converse need its own reason?

Reversing a true statement does not automatically give a true statement. Here a separate proof works: draw the bisector of ∠QPR to meet QR at W. Then ∠PQW ≅ ∠PRW (given), ∠QPW ≅ ∠RPW (bisector) and PW ≅ PW (shared). So △PQW ≅ △PRW by AAS, and PQ ≅ PR.

Try it

Use the converse with lengths

In △SVT, m∠S = 50° and m∠V = 50°. ST = 7 cm and SV = 9 cm. Predict TV first, and name the side opposite each marked angle.

Hint · Which side touches both arcs?

One side joins S and V. It touches both marked angles, so it is opposite neither. Look for the sides across from S and across from V.

Compare your reasoning, including without scripts

The angle at S is opposite TV. The angle at V is opposite ST. The angles are congruent, so TV ≅ ST and TV = 7 cm. SV = 9 cm is the base: it joins the two marked angles. Retry with a different triangle: in a new △SVT with no lengths given, suppose m∠T = m∠V. Which side matches ST? Predict before opening the next comparison.

Compare the retry

Now the marked angles are at T and V. The angle at T is opposite SV and the angle at V is opposite ST, so SV ≅ ST. The base would be TV.

Problem type 3

When triangles share corners

In a bigger figure, one letter can be the corner of several angles. Then a one-letter name like ∠L is unclear. Find the triangle first, and give each angle all three letters.

  1. Read the marks

    Start from the given fact

    KL ≅ KN

    Suppose KL ≅ KN. The figure has two triangles that share side LN, so be careful which one you use.

  2. Find the triangle

    Which triangle has both sides?

    △KLN

    KL and KN share the endpoint K. Their other endpoints are L and N. So the triangle is △KLN, the top one.

  3. Go across

    Find the angle across from each side

    opposite KN → at L, opposite KL → at N

    Inside △KLN, the angle opposite KN is at L, and the angle opposite KL is at N.

  4. Name it

    Use three letters

    ∠KLN, not ∠NLM

    At L there are three angles: ∠KLN, ∠NLM and the large ∠KLM. Only ∠KLN is inside △KLN. At N, likewise, use ∠KNL.

  5. Conclude

    State the congruent angles

    ∠KLN ≅ ∠KNL

    Use only the given fact. The bottom triangle may look a certain way, but nothing here is marked about it.

Tip: the arrow keys also move through the steps.

The steps

  1. Find the one triangle that contains both given sides.

    KL and KN → △KLN

    Why?

    The theorem only works inside a single triangle. Two given sides share a corner, the apex, and their other endpoints are the base corners.

  2. Inside that triangle, go across from each side.

    KN → L, KL → N

    Why?

    Opposite means the corner a side does not touch, measured inside the triangle you chose, not in the whole figure.

  3. Name each angle with three letters.

    ∠KLN and ∠KNL

    Why?

    A pattern that helps: apex, corner, other base corner. K–L–N and K–N–L. The middle letter is always the corner.

  4. State the result, using only what is given.

    ∠KLN ≅ ∠KNL

    Why?

    A drawing can look symmetric without being marked. Conclusions come from the given facts and theorems, not from how the picture looks.

Try it

Angles given, sides wanted

Here the given fact is ∠LMN ≅ ∠LNM. Complete the statement: then ___ ≅ ___. Write which triangle you used first.

Hint 1 · Read the three letters

The outside letters of ∠LMN and ∠LNM are L, M and N. Which triangle uses exactly those three points?

Hint 2 · Go across

Inside that triangle, find the side that does not touch M. Then find the side that does not touch N.

Check your pair of sides

Compare your reasoning

The letters L, M and N give △LMN. The angle at M is opposite LN. The angle at N (inside △LMN) is opposite LM. So LN ≅ LM. MN joins the two marked angles, so it is the base. Notice that the angle at N in this triangle is ∠LNM, not ∠KNL: same corner, different triangle.

Mixed practice

Your turn

Each statement gives one fact. Use that fact only, not how the drawing looks. Complete it, and name the triangle you used. Try all three before opening any comparison.

  1. A. If XP ≅ XQ, then ∠___ ≅ ∠___.

  2. B. If XS ≅ RS, which angle of △PXQ must be congruent to ∠XRS? Name it with three letters.

  3. C. If XP ≅ XS, what does the Isosceles Triangle Theorem tell you about angles?

Hint 1 · Check that it is one triangle

The theorem needs two sides of the same triangle. Two segments that lie along one straight line cannot both be sides of a triangle.

Hint 2 · Find the apex, then look across X

The two given sides meet at one point, the apex. The congruent angles are at the other two corners, even when one of those corners is X. For B, find that angle at X first, then find the angle straight across from it.

Compare A

XP and XQ share X, and their other endpoints give △PXQ. The angle opposite XQ is at P: ∠XPQ. The angle opposite XP is at Q: ∠XQP. So ∠XPQ ≅ ∠XQP. Since X lies on PS, ∠SPQ names the same angle as ∠XPQ, so that answer is also fine.

Compare B

XS and RS share S, and their other endpoints give △RXS. The angle opposite XS is at R: ∠XRS. The angle opposite RS is at X, inside △RXS: ∠RXS. So ∠XRS ≅ ∠RXS. Four angles meet at X. ∠RXS and ∠PXQ are vertical angles, so they are congruent too. So ∠PXQ ≅ ∠XRS. Here the apex of △RXS is S, so X is a base corner this time.

Compare C

Nothing. P, X and S lie on one straight segment, so XP and XS are pieces of the same segment, not two sides of one triangle. The theorem does not apply. It only says X is the midpoint of PS. Checking that both pieces belong to one triangle is the first step every time.

Before you go back to your own work

  • Did you find the one triangle that contains both given parts?
  • Did you go across from each side (or angle), not next to it?
  • Did you leave out the part between the two given parts?
  • Did you use three letters wherever a corner belongs to more than one angle?

Keep the idea

Tomorrow, before opening this page, try this from memory: in △TUW, ∠T ≅ ∠W. Which two sides are congruent, and which side is the base?

Compare the recall question

The angle at T is opposite UW and the angle at W is opposite TU, so UW ≅ TU. The base is TW, which joins the two congruent angles. If you were unsure, reread Problem type 2.

Glossary

Opposite side (of an angle)
In a triangle, the side that does not touch a given angle. The side opposite the angle at B in △ABC is AC.
Congruent
Exactly the same size and shape. Write ≅. Congruent segments have equal lengths; congruent angles have equal measures. Tick marks and arc marks show congruence in a drawing.
Isosceles triangle
A triangle with at least two congruent sides.
Legs
The two congruent sides of an isosceles triangle.
Vertex angle
The angle formed by the two legs. Its corner is where the legs meet.
Base
The third side of an isosceles triangle, joining the ends of the legs. It can point any direction on the page.
Base angles
The two angles at the ends of the base. Each is opposite one leg. The theorem says they are congruent.
Vertical angles
The two angles across from each other where two straight lines cross. They are always congruent.
Converse
The statement made by swapping the if and then parts of another statement. A converse needs its own justification, because not every converse is true.
