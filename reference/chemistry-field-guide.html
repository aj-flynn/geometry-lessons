Four checks, with a model first

Before starting a procedure, ask what the evidence supports. An inventory alone does not establish charges, connections, or an electron-sharing picture. Specify whether you are describing a whole particle, its interior, or its neighbors; compatible models may apply at different scales. Try the fresh evidence-to-model decisions before choosing a drawing method.

1. What particles and how many?
Separate the recipe within one particle from the number of copies. Multiply each per-particle count by the coefficient. An ionic formula describes an ion ratio in a crystal. Formula lesson and glossary.
2. Where are the valence electrons?
For a small molecule, add the valence counts and adjust for any charge. Choose the connections and start with a shared pair on each. Fill the end atoms’ shells, then check each interior atom. Track any remaining lone pairs. If a shell still falls short, check whether moving a pair into a shared position completes both shells. Count the two electrons in each pair once in the total. Count shared pairs around both neighbors when you check each shell. Use the worked pair-placement checks to compare those two counts.
3. Do the polarity effects combine?
Use the absolute electronegativity difference and your course’s key. A large difference can suggest a charged-particle model to check. Obtain geometry independently, then combine bond effects in space. Equal opposite effects cancel; unequal effects do not. Polarity lesson and glossary.
4. What separates during a change?
Distinguish internal bonds from attractions between molecules. Name the evidence for each contribution. Look for a lasting separation of partial charge, a temporary shift that induces a nearby shift, or the connections and available pair that permit hydrogen bonding. Use your course’s comparison when the question is about typical individual contacts. When comparing substances, check the evidence for total attraction, the conditions, and what separates. Practice choosing the right comparison.

Check your reasoning

What evidence did I use? Which relationship connects it to my claim? What did I assume? Would changing one assumption change my conclusion?
