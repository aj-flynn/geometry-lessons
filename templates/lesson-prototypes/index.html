    Geometry · Similar polygons

    Same shape at a new scale

    Match corners and edges, decide which measurement changes, and justify when two shapes are similar.

    Quick check

    A 5 cm strip becomes 8 cm long. What do you multiply 5 by? What undoes the change?

    Check the starting idea

    Multiply by 8/5, the target divided by the original. Multiply by 5/8 to undo the change. These fractions are reciprocals, meaning their product is 1.

    Match the corners, then choose the factor

    Two polygons are similar when matching angles have equal measures and all matching side lengths share one multiplier. A shape can be turned or reflected (flipped) and still have the same shape. The corners and sides that match are called corresponding.

    The scale factor k is a target length divided by its original partner. Side lengths and perimeter use one factor k. Area uses k², because a face grows in two directions at once.

    Predict. A rectangular stamp starts 7 cm wide and 5 cm tall. If both lengths double, how many copies of the original face cover the target? How many times as long is the trip around its edge?

    Read the stages after predicting
    1. Original stamp: 7 cm by 5 cm. It is 24 cm around and covers 35 cm².
    2. Factor 2: 14 cm by 10 cm. The trip around doubles to 48 cm. Two rows of two original tiles cover the face, so the area is 4 × 35 cm² = 140 cm².

    Two similar metal badges

    Given. These pentagonal badges are similar. Equal arc marks identify matching angles, and small boxes mark right angles. The first badge has AB = 7 cm, BC = 3 cm, EA = 6 cm and m∠D ≈ 143.1° (≈ means “approximately”). The target has VW = 9.8 cm, XY = 7 cm, YZ = 4.2 cm and ZV = 8.4 cm.

    Use the marks and givens as evidence. The drawings show shape, not measured length.

    Match the badges and find missing parts

    Goal. Write the matching order and the side-length proportion. Then find one missing length in each badge and the marked angle in the target.

    1. One arc gives C ↔ X and two arcs give D ↔ Y. Follow the edge from the one-arc corner toward the two-arc corner and continue around both outlines. This gives ABCDE ∼ VWXYZ. The symbol ∼ means “is similar to.”
    2. Matched angles are congruent, so ∠Y measures about 143.1°, because its partner ∠D does. Angle measures stay the same when you scale.
    3. Pair both endpoints of each edge, including the edge that closes the walk. The scale factor is the target length over its original partner.
      VW/AB = WX/BC = XY/CD = YZ/DE = ZV/EA = k
      k = (9.8 cm)/(7 cm) = 7/5
    4. Original → target: multiply by k.
      WX = (7/5)(3 cm) = 4.2 cm
    5. Target → original: divide by 7/5, or multiply by its reciprocal 5/7.
      DE = (5/7)(4.2 cm) = 3 cm

    Try it in new situations

    Try a different direction

    The original is polygon KLMNP and the target is similar polygon UTQSR, in matching order. RU = 8.8 cm, PK = 5.5 cm and QS = 12 cm. Write every angle pair and a complete proportion. Find MN and give the factor from target to original.

    Check your work and retry

    ∠K ≅ ∠U, ∠L ≅ ∠T, ∠M ≅ ∠Q, ∠N ≅ ∠S and ∠P ≅ ∠R. With the original on top:

    KL/UT = LM/TQ = MN/QS = NP/SR = PK/RU = 5/8

    The last-to-first edge gives the factor, and MN = (5/8)(12 cm) = 7.5 cm. If you got a larger length, check which figure belongs on top. Retry by writing the five fractions in the opposite direction. What does the new factor mean?

    Compare the retry

    Reverse every fraction. The factor becomes 8/5, which turns each original length into its target length.

    Choose a factor before calculating

    A display maker reduces a pentagonal design. A 26 mm edge becomes a 19.5 mm edge. The new design covers 207 mm². Find the area of the larger design and explain whether you multiply or divide by the area factor.

    Hint · which figure is given?

    Write the factor from the large figure to the small figure. Square it, then decide which area you already know.

    Compare your setup

    Small/large length = 19.5/26 = 3/4. Small/large area = (3/4)² = 9/16. The known area is the small one, so divide.

    207 mm² ÷ (9/16) = 368 mm²

    Dividing by 3/4 undoes only one dimension. Retry with perimeter: if the small design has perimeter 90 mm, find the large perimeter.

    Compare the perimeter retry

    Perimeter is a sum of lengths, so divide 90 mm by 3/4 to get 120 mm. The unit stays mm.

    Glossary

    Similar (∼)
    Matching angles are congruent, and matching side lengths all have the same target-to-original ratio.
    Corresponding and congruent
    Corresponding parts are matched partners. Congruent angles have equal measures, shown with ≅.
    Scale factor
    The ratio of a target length to the corresponding original length.
    Reciprocal
    The number that multiplies with a given nonzero number to give 1. The reciprocal of 7/5 is 5/7.
    Perimeter and area
    Perimeter measures the outline in length units such as cm. Area measures the face in square units such as cm² or mm².

    Recall tomorrow

    Sketch any similar pair. Name the original and the target. Explain how to get the new perimeter and area from one known side pair, then explain how you would reverse both changes.

    • Match corners by their marks before pairing sides.
    • Lengths and perimeter scale by k. Area scales by k².
    • To reverse a change, use the reciprocal, or its square for area.

    English · Close reading

    What a line lets you infer

    Put older dialogue into today’s English. Then use the writer’s exact words to explain what an image does or what a character’s choices reveal.

    Before you start

    In “Did you lock the cabinet?” is the speaker asking about a fact or stating one? Hold on to that difference when you rewrite a line.

    Check the starting idea

    The speaker is asking. The helping verb at the front (“Did you…”) and the question mark mark a question. A rewrite has to stay a question. It should not become “You locked the cabinet.”

    Keep the meaning. Explain the image.

    Start with who speaks and who hears. A speaker says the line and a character is a person in the story. A character’s judgment belongs to that character unless the text gives you a reason to attribute it elsewhere.

    In older dialogue, thou and thee mean you and thy means your. Dost thou trust? puts the helping verb first, just as Do you trust? does today. Rewrite in two passes. First work out the words and their order. Then say the thought naturally, keeping any question, condition, uncertainty or negative.

    Figurative language adds meaning through moves such as comparison or exaggeration. Each of these moves is a device:

    • A metaphor treats one thing as another. A simile marks the comparison with like or as: “The labels curled like dry leaves.”
    • Personification gives a thing a human action: “The hinges protested.”
    • Hyperbole stretches the facts for emphasis: “That queue took a century.”

    To explain a character, make an inference, a conclusion drawn from clues, about a trait, a tendency in how the character behaves. Say what the words support, then stop where the evidence stops.

    A line from a counting room

    Read the line in four moves

    1. Observe and quote. Copy “This ledger is a sieve.” That sentence contains the book and the thing it is compared to. Keep the following question in view as context.
    2. Name the device. The line uses a metaphor because the clerk calls the book a sieve, treating one thing as another to suggest a shared quality.
    3. Put it plainly. First read the question as “Do you trust it with the grain count?” Then unpack the image: “This record book lets entries slip through. Can you trust it to keep track of the grain?”
    4. Explain the effect. A sieve has holes, which makes missing entries the likely concern. The question tests whether the coworker will rely on the book. The line gives the clerk’s view. The actual cause of the gaps is still open.

    Read new scenes

    Check your reading

    [A mapmaker looks at a rough draft.]

    Mapmaker If thou dost use that sketch, wilt thou lose thy way? The ink has swallowed the footpaths.

    Wilt thou means will you. Rewrite both sentences aloud and identify the device in the second sentence. Then pick the explanation that keeps the meaning and explains the image.

    Which explanation fits both sentences?

    Compare the three readings

    The middle reading keeps the question and the condition. “The ink has swallowed the footpaths” is a metaphor that treats ink like something that can swallow; in context the paths on the page are obscured. The first reading turns a question into a certainty. The last moves the effect from the page to the real paths.

    Retry with one changed clue

    Suppose the mapmaker asks “Wilt thou lose thy way even if thou dost leave that sketch here?” Say it in modern English first. Then compare: “Will you get lost even if you leave that sketch here?” Keep even if and keep the question.

    Check your claim

    [A team is about to hang a mural.]

    Installer I brought a spare hook. Show me how you’d hang it. We can test both methods on this board.

    Find the shortest continuous quotation that shows the installer is open to another person’s idea. Say how the words support the trait before choosing.

    Which quotation gives the most direct evidence?

    Compare the evidence

    “Show me how you’d hang it.” is enough to support the claim. The spare hook supports being prepared. The whole speech includes both clues, which makes it less precise for this claim.

    Retry with a different claim

    For “The installer wants a fair comparison,” try “We can test both methods on this board.” The installer proposes comparing both approaches on the same surface, which makes the conditions more alike.

    Glossary

    Speaker and character
    The speaker says a line. A character is a person in the story. The narrator tells the story and the author writes it.
    Figurative language
    Language that uses images, exaggeration or other moves beyond the ordinary literal meaning.
    Device
    A technique the writer uses to create an effect.
    Metaphor and simile
    A metaphor treats one thing as another. A simile marks the comparison with a word such as like or as.
    Personification and hyperbole
    Personification gives a thing a human action or quality. Hyperbole exaggerates for emphasis.
    Inference and trait
    An inference is a conclusion drawn from clues. A trait is a tendency in how a character behaves.

    Take the method to another text

    Before reopening this lesson, write the three moves from memory: what you observe, what the words mean, and what the words let you infer. Try them on a line you have never read.

    • Put your exact quotation next to the original and check the wording.
    • Check that the plain meaning keeps any question, condition or uncertainty.
    • Underline the word your analysis depends on and explain what it contributes.

    Chemistry · Formulas

    Read particle recipes

    Count atoms by separating the recipe for one particle from the number of particles.

    Count by groups

    One box holds 3 apples and 2 pears. How many pears are in 4 identical boxes? Which number did you leave out, and why?

    Check the starting idea

    4 × 2 = 8 pears. Count what is inside one box, then multiply by the number of boxes. The apples are left out because the question asks only about pears.

    Start with one particle

    Imagine a tray holding groups of lettered tokens. Pick one group. Its recipe tells you which tokens it contains and how many of each. We use Q and R as pretend atom labels. An atom is one unit of an element. A particle is the unit you are counting, such as one atom or one whole group.

    The recipe Q3R2 means three Q tokens and two R tokens in one group. The small lower numbers are subscripts. When a label has no subscript, count one.

    A large number in front is a coefficient. It counts copies of the whole group. To count the tokens in a sample, multiply each count in one group by the number of groups.

    3 Q3R2 → 3 × 3 = 9 Q and 3 × 2 = 6 R

    Add whole groups

    Predict. Start with one group. The display needs at least five R tokens, and you can add only whole groups. Will one or two added groups be enough? Write down how many Q and R tokens the display will have at that point.

    Read the stages after predicting
    1. One Q₃R₂ group contains 3 Q and 2 R, which is 5 atoms altogether.
    2. Two groups give 6 Q and 4 R. Four R falls short of the goal.
    3. Three groups give 9 Q and 6 R. Six R meets the goal, so you can stop after adding two groups.

    From a drawing to a recipe

    Use the same method with real symbols. This original schematic shows one chloroethane particle. C labels carbon, H labels hydrogen and Cl labels chlorine.

    1. Find one particle. The drawing shows one particle, so every count below is for one particle.
    2. Read each symbol. A lowercase letter belongs with the capital before it, so Cl is one two-letter symbol, not C plus something else.
    3. Make an inventory inside the particle.
      Atom labels in one drawn particle
      SymbolElementCount
      Ccarbon2
      Hhydrogen5
      Clchlorine1
    4. Write the recipe. Put each count as a subscript after its symbol and leave out a subscript of 1.
      C2H5Cl
    5. Read it back. C₂H₅Cl gives 2 C, 5 H and 1 Cl, matching the inventory. Three identical particles would be written 3 C₂H₅Cl: the coefficient changes and the subscripts stay fixed.

    Your turn with new recipes

    Count one kind of token

    A fictional display contains four identical groups, each with two Q tokens and five R tokens. Count just the R tokens across the display.

    Hint 1 · Notice

    Find the number of complete groups.

    Hint 2 · Recall

    Each group contributes the same five R tokens.

    Compare reasoning after an attempt

    Four groups each contribute five R tokens: 4 × 5 = 20. The Q tokens are a different type, so they stay out of this count.

    Read a two-letter symbol

    A label contains Zr with no subscript. How should the symbol and its count be read?

    Compare reasoning after an attempt

    Zr is one two-letter symbol and the missing subscript means one. Symbol letters and atom counts are different things.

    Exit check · read a new label

    A sample label gives only 2 C3H6O. The 2 counts complete molecules. List the number of each kind of atom in one molecule and in the whole sample. Can you tell from the label which atom or atoms the oxygen is bonded to?

    Hint · read the label in two passes

    Use each symbol and its subscript for the first inventory. Then apply the leading 2 to every count. For the last question, look for information about which atoms connect.

    Compare after trying all three questions

    One molecule has three carbon, six hydrogen and one oxygen atom. The two molecules together contain six carbon, twelve hydrogen and two oxygen atoms. The label gives counts, not connections. To find oxygen’s neighbors, you would need more information, such as a drawing.

    Glossary

    Atom and symbol
    An atom is one unit of an element. A symbol is a compact label for an element, such as C, H or Cl.
    Particle and molecule
    A particle is a general word for a counted unit; say whether you mean an atom, a molecule or an ion. A molecule is a bounded group of bonded atoms.
    Subscript
    A small lower number attached to a symbol. It counts that atom inside one particle.
    Coefficient
    A multiplier written before a complete recipe. It counts copies of the whole particle.

    Remember it tomorrow

    Close the lesson. Sketch one connected group of three matching tokens and explain how it differs from three separate tokens. Then explain coefficient versus subscript in one sentence each. Tomorrow, try again without looking.

    Chemistry · Electrons

    Follow the electrons

    Build and audit an electron drawing: keep the total budget and each atom’s shell in balance.

    Count in pairs

    If two dots mean one pair, how many electrons do three pairs use? Think “three groups of two.” If you are unsure, sketch three pairs before continuing.

    Check the starting idea

    Three pairs use 3 × 2 = 6 electrons. Every line or pair in an electron drawing costs two electrons.

    An electron budget

    Electrons carry negative charge. The outer electrons used in bonding are valence electrons. A Lewis drawing is an electron-accounting model: dots track electrons and each line stands for one shared pair.

    Build a budget before drawing. Add the valence electrons from every atom, add one for each unit of negative charge, and subtract one for each unit of positive charge.

    Valence electrons for main-group columns
    Periodic-table groupValence electrons (neutral atom)
    11
    22
    13 to 183 to 8 (group number minus 10)
    Helium, group 182 (exception)

    A lone pair is a pair not shared between atoms. Many introductory models aim for eight electrons around an atom, an octet. Hydrogen aims for two. These are limited guidelines, not universal rules.

    Two different counts. In the whole budget, each pair counts once. When you check one atom’s shell, a shared pair counts around both of its atoms.

    Construct ethene, C₂H₄

    Predict. A trial drawing has 8 electrons around the left carbon, 6 around the right carbon, and 12 in its whole budget. If a lone pair on the left becomes shared between the carbons, what happens to each carbon’s count and to the whole budget? Make three separate predictions.

    Read the stages after predicting
    1. Start with the six atoms and a twelve-electron budget.
    2. Five connections use ten electrons. Two electrons remain.
    3. A trial lone pair uses the remainder, but the right carbon is two electrons short of its octet.
    4. Move the trial pair between the carbons. The same twelve electrons now complete both carbon octets and all hydrogen shells.

    Audit the ethene budget

    Ethene has two carbon atoms joined together, each attached to two hydrogens. This connection pattern is supplied; a formula alone does not always decide connections.

    1. Find the supply. Carbon is in group 14, so it supplies 4. Hydrogen is in group 1, so it supplies 1.
      2 × 4 + 4 × 1 = 12 electrons
    2. Draw the connections. Four C—H lines and one C—C line use 5 × 2 = 10 electrons. Two remain.
    3. Place the remainder and audit each atom. A lone pair on the left carbon uses the last two electrons, but the right carbon counts only 6.
    4. Repair without adding electrons. Move that pair between the carbons. The left carbon still counts it, and now the right carbon does too.
    5. Audit both ways. Whole drawing: 6 lines × 2 = 12 electrons, matching the budget. Each atom: both carbons count 8 and each hydrogen counts 2.

    Construct and audit

    Check two kinds of counting

    Two atoms share a pair. A learner counts that pair twice in the total budget because both atoms use it in their shell check. Which audit repairs the mistake?

    Compare reasoning after an attempt

    One shared pair is two electrons in the whole structure. Both neighboring atoms include that pair when checking their own shells.

    Repair a short shell

    A new trial drawing uses exactly its budget, but one carbon has only six electrons around it. What should you inspect next?

    Compare reasoning after an attempt

    Inspect whether an existing neighboring pair can be shared while preserving the total and every required shell count. After any move, audit each atom again.

    Construct, then audit a new molecule

    Sketch the neutral molecule C2H4O in a sample whose oxygen has exactly one neighboring atom. Each carbon shares four pairs in all, oxygen shares two, and each hydrogen shares one. Two pairs can connect the same two neighbors. Propose the connections first, then show all shared and lone pairs.

    Hint 1 · place the ends

    Mark the atoms that can have only one neighbor. Which atoms can connect the rest of the molecule into one piece?

    Hint 2 · check both budgets

    Use groups 14, 1 and 16 to count the supplied electrons for C, H and O. Track each pair once in the total, then count the pairs around each atom.

    Compare the complete drawing after an attempt

    The two carbons must connect, because only they can be interior atoms here. Oxygen connects to one carbon with two shared pairs. The carbon bonded to oxygen has room for one H, and the other carbon connects to three H atoms. This structure is called ethanal.

    2 × 4 + 4 × 1 + 6 = 18 electrons = 7 lines × 2 + 2 lone pairs × 2

    If your oxygen has two neighbors, revisit the sample evidence. Close the comparison, revise, and audit each atom again.

    Glossary

    Valence electrons
    Outer electrons used in bonding. Electrons carry negative charge.
    Lewis drawing
    A flat model that tracks valence electrons and connections. It does not show the shape in space.
    Budget
    The total number of valence electrons available for the whole drawing. Each represented pair costs two.
    Lone pair
    A pair of electrons that is not shared between atoms.
    Octet
    An eight-electron outer-shell goal used in some simple models. Hydrogen uses two.

    Remember it tomorrow

    Close the lesson. Explain why one bond line costs two electrons in the total budget, even though both atoms count the pair when checking their shells. Tomorrow, try again without looking.

    • Budget first: supply, plus negative charge, minus positive charge.
    • Connections next, then lone pairs, never past the budget.
    • Audit globally once and locally around every atom.

    Chemistry · Polarity

    Combine bond effects

    Use a bond’s electron pull and a molecule’s supplied shape as two separate clues.

    Distance on a number line

    How far apart are 2.9 and 2.1 on a number line? Does the order you subtract them change the distance?

    Check the starting idea

    They are 0.8 apart either way. Subtract the smaller from the larger. A distance is never negative.

    How uneven is one bond?

    Electronegativity describes an atom’s pull on shared electrons. Find the absolute difference by subtracting the smaller value from the larger one. The values are unitless, and a greater difference usually means more uneven sharing.

    ΔEN = |ENA − ENB| = larger − smaller

    When the pulls differ, label the stronger-pulling end δ− (partial negative) and the weaker-pulling end δ+ (partial positive). “Partial” means a fraction of a full unit of charge.

    A bond dipole arrow marks which end pulls more strongly. Put the crossed tail by δ+ and point the tip toward δ−. Different courses use different cutoffs for naming bond types, and real bonding changes gradually across those cutoffs.

    Look up a real pair

    Given. A diagram places sulfur (S) on the left and silicon (Si) on the right. Goal. Choose a fill and a direction for the bond dipole arrow.

    1. Look up both values on one scale. From the Pauling table, S is 2.58 and Si is 1.90.
    2. Subtract smaller from larger.
      ΔEN = 2.58 − 1.90 = 0.68
    3. Trace the difference to one row. 0.37 ≤ 0.68 < 1.23, so the display is solid.
    4. Choose the direction. S has the higher value, so the tip points left toward S (δ−) and the crossed tail sits by Si (δ+).
    5. State the limit. The display key is a practice convention. For a chemical classification, use your own course’s boundary and label.

    Use the difference and the key

    Try the difference now

    Compare reasoning after an attempt

    3.4 − 2.2 = 1.2. Switching the order gives −1.2, whose size is still 1.2, which is why the absolute difference is used.

    Decide at a boundary

    In a training bond, the left end is rated 1.46 and the right end 2.69 on the same invented scale. Write ΔEN, trace it to a row, and sketch the arrow.

    How should the display draw this arrow?

    Compare after an attempt

    2.69 − 1.46 = 1.23. The striped row includes 1.23 because of ≥. The tip points right toward the higher value (δ−), and the crossed tail is on the left (δ+).

    Exit check · one bond and a whole molecule

    One bond. The left end is rated 3.3 and the right end 2.6. Choose a fill, draw the arrow, and label δ+ and δ−. A second line is then drawn between the same atoms, with the ratings held fixed. What changes in the drawing, and what happens to the difference?

    A whole molecule. A shape model shows two opposing bond effects, and one is stronger. Do they balance completely? What would you ask for if you had only a flat drawing of the connections?

    Compare after explaining both cases

    ΔEN = 3.3 − 2.6 = 0.7, in the solid row. The tip points left toward 3.3 (δ−) and the crossed tail is on the right (δ+). The extra line records an extra pair; the fixed ratings still give 0.7.

    Opposing effects of unequal strength only partly balance, leaving a combined effect toward the stronger one. With only a connection drawing, ask for the shape in space and the relative strengths and directions of the bond effects.

    Glossary

    Electronegativity and ΔEN
    Electronegativity describes attraction for shared electrons. ΔEN is the difference between two values on the same unitless scale.
    Absolute difference, ≤ and ≥
    The distance between two values, including zero for equal values. ≤ and ≥ include their boundary.
    δ and partial charge
    The Greek letter δ marks a partial charge, a fraction of a whole unit of charge.
    Bond dipole arrow
    The crossed tail marks the δ+ end of a bond. The tip points to the δ− end.

    Remember it tomorrow

    Name the two kinds of information needed before deciding whether local δ+ and δ− ends leave an uneven charge across a whole molecule. Sketch a case where two effects cancel. Tomorrow, try again without looking.

    Chemistry · Attractions

    Look between particles

    Explain a physical-property trend by identifying which particles attract and what separates.

    Within or between?

    When a pot of water boils, do the water molecules break apart into atoms, or do whole molecules move away from one another? Decide before reading on.

    Check the starting idea

    Whole molecules move apart. Boiling without decomposing is a physical change: the particles keep their chemical identity, and only the attractions between them are overcome.

    Zoom out one level

    Intermolecular forces are attractions between molecules. When a molecular liquid boils without decomposing, molecules separate while their internal connections generally remain intact.

    • All atoms and molecules experience London dispersion attractions from temporary uneven electron distributions.
    • Molecules with a permanent dipole also attract through dipole–dipole contacts between partial charges.
    • In common introductory examples, hydrogen bonding needs H bonded directly to N, O or F and a suitable lone pair on a nearby partner. Merely containing H is not enough.

    Predict → separate → explain. Two A—B pairs start near each other. If the pairs stay intact as the sample spreads out, what happens to the gap between the pairs and to the number of connected A—B groups?

    Read the stages after predicting
    1. Four atoms in two connected A—B groups. The dotted gap between the groups is short.
    2. The dotted gap is longer. There are still two connected A—B groups.
    3. The groups are farther apart. Both A—B connections remain and there are still four atoms.

    Evidence for three proposed contacts

    Each fragment shows the atom directly bonded to H. The nearby partner is a separate particle. Treat the stated lone-pair availability as given; these are partial connection models.

    Three proposed hydrogen-bond contacts
    ContactConnection to HSuitable nearby lone pair?
    AO—HAvailable
    BC—HAvailable
    CO—HUnavailable

    Apply both conditions, one column at a time

    1. State both conditions. (1) H is bonded directly to N, O or F. (2) A nearby partner offers a suitable lone pair.
    2. Contact A. O—H passes condition 1, and the available lone pair passes condition 2. A meets both.
    3. Contact B. C—H fails condition 1, because C is not N, O or F. The available partner pair cannot rescue it.
    4. Contact C. O—H passes condition 1, but no suitable partner pair is available, so condition 2 fails.
    5. Conclude within the evidence. Only A supports the possibility of hydrogen bonding. Actual distance and orientation also matter, and this check does not rank boiling points.

    Compare fairly

    Compare a contact and a whole sample

    In a fictional model, each R contact takes four energy units to separate and each S contact takes two. Packet A has one R contact. Packet B has three S contacts. Add the contact energies to compare packets.

    Which contact is stronger, and which packet takes more energy to separate?

    Compare after an attempt

    Per contact, R (4 units) beats S (2 units). Per packet, B needs 3 × 2 = 6 units and A needs 4. Real samples need separate evidence about their total attraction.

    Compare boiling and melting fairly

    Invented liquids A and B have similarly sized and shaped molecules. B has stronger total attractions between molecules.

    Conditions for two simulated reports
    ReportConditionsQuestion
    1Same pressureWhich liquid will likely boil higher?
    2Solid packing unknownWhich solid melts higher?
    Which conclusion uses the stated evidence for both reports?

    Compare reasoning after an attempt

    Fixed pressure supports a boiling trend for B. Melting depends on how the solid packs, so it needs more evidence.

    Retry with a third report

    If A is measured at one pressure and B at a lower one, the temperatures cannot isolate the effect of attractions. Ask for data at the same pressure.

    Glossary

    Intermolecular and intramolecular
    Intermolecular means between molecules; intramolecular means within a molecule.
    Dispersion
    Attraction arising from temporary uneven electron distributions. Every atom and molecule has it.
    Permanent dipole
    A lasting separation of partial positive and partial negative charge in a molecule.
    Hydrogen bonding
    An attraction involving H directly bonded to N, O or F and a suitable nearby lone-pair partner, in typical introductory examples.
    Boiling point and vapor pressure
    Boiling happens when a liquid’s vapor pressure equals the external pressure. Compare boiling points only at the same pressure.

    Remember it tomorrow

    Draw two bonded pairs. Mark a bond within a pair and an attraction between pairs. Explain which changes when the pairs separate without decomposing.

    • Locate the attraction: inside a particle or between particles?
    • Ask what separates during the change.
    • Check that the comparison holds conditions such as pressure fixed.

Your template, lesson and favourite live only in the address and in page memory, and practice responses disappear when you leave.

    1. Reading Column A calm, book-like single column. Every part appears in teaching order with numbered section markers and a short contents list. This is the baseline, with the least navigation and the most continuous reading.
    2. Evidence Split On wide screens the diagram, passage or data stays pinned beside the explanation, worked reasoning and practice, so learners never scroll away from what they are reasoning about. On phones the evidence sits inline after the explanation, with jump-back links.
    3. Guided Steps One part at a time with a progress track and Back/Next buttons. Worked reasoning is revealed one step at a time, and the glossary stays one tap away in a drawer. Suited to phones and to learners who need a short visible goal.
    4. Bento Overview A dashboard of tiles shows the whole lesson at a glance. Goal, warm-up, idea, evidence, worked steps, practice, glossary and recap each get a tile sized to their role, and worked steps become a row of cards.
    5. Reasoning Ladder A vertical route with a rail and stops. Every worked step and every practice task becomes its own rung, making the order of reasoning visible and easy to resume.
    6. Inquiry First Opens with the evidence and a noticing prompt before any explanation. It then checks prior knowledge, makes sense of the idea, follows the reasoning, tests it and looks back. This is a predict–observe–explain order.
    7. Reference Three-Pane A documentation layout. An outline tracks your place, the lesson sits in the centre, and a facts panel and an always-visible glossary fill the right rail. Built for looking things up and returning later.
    8. Editorial Spread A magazine treatment. It has a bold hero with the warm-up as a sidebar and the evidence as a full-width feature. Text-only explanations set in columns, worked steps get large numerals, practice sits in an inverted band, and the recap is a pull quote.
    9. Paper Worksheet A printable handout on a desk, with lettered boxes and notes beside the evidence. Each practice task gets ruled working space, and the sheet ends with an exit ticket and a word bank. Designed to print well and to be worked on paper.

    Shared content contract

    Every sample is an <article data-lesson> with these parts, read by every template:

    • intro: eyebrow, title and objective
    • prerequisite: a quick check with a closed comparison
    • explanation: terms linked to the glossary, optional equations, tables and stepped models
    • evidence: data-kind is diagram, passage or data, with an optional noticing prompt
    • worked: a setup and an ordered list of justified steps
    • practice: one or more items with hints, optional choice or number checks, and closed comparisons
    • glossary: dt ids that terms link to
    • recap: later recall and a return checklist
